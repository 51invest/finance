How money, credit and institutions work.

“How does money travel across time and risk?”

The principles behind money, credit, financial institutions, accounting and corporate finance.

18 modules 58 articles about 9 hr 17 min total

Station brief

Start from a concrete scene, then set out the questions, methods and boundaries of this station.

A mortgage is signed. The borrower receives money, the bank adds an asset, and depositors’ balances are still there. Finance lines these events up on one timeline: whose purchasing power is lent today, whose future cash flow repays it, and who carries default and run risk in between.

Start with balance sheets. Banks, funds, insurers and firms differ less by name than by what they hold as assets, whom they owe, and how much leverage they can carry. Reading one statement is worth more than memorising ten product labels.

Three prices do most of the work: the interest rate prices time, the risk premium prices uncertainty, and liquidity prices the ability to exit. Together they explain why the same cash flow is worth different amounts at different dates and with different issuers.

Financial numbers look exact, which is why assumptions disappear so easily. Compounding frequency, day count, tax, fees and inflation can each change the answer. Given a return, ask for the convention first, then the risk, then the horizon.

Finance also builds fragility. Maturity transformation lets banks fund long loans with short deposits, which is useful and also makes runs possible. Leverage magnifies gains and losses. Capital buffers and regulation address these structural problems, not individual greed alone.

After this station, you should be able to read a bank balance sheet, discount a cash flow, and explain why a product is cheap or expensive without repeating a sales line.

After this station, you should be able to answer

  • Why does a rise in interest rates lower long-duration asset valuations?
  • Why are banks afraid of a run, not only of bad loans?
  • Why does compounding create such large gaps over long horizons?

Four common misreadings

These claims come up again and again in reader mail, comments and working discussions. Naming them is more useful than repeating definitions.

“Finance is money making money.”

Finance arranges savings, risk, maturity and information. Returns come from bearing risk, supplying liquidity and moving resources across time, not from counting the same money twice.

“Deposits are the bank’s own money.”

Deposits are liabilities: the bank owes depositors a withdrawable claim. Its own funds are equity, held to absorb losses. Treating deposits as capital understates run and solvency risk.

“Compounding guarantees high returns.”

Compounding magnifies whatever return is there, including losses, fees and inflation. Long-run outcomes depend on net return and duration, not on the word itself.

“An interest rate is just one number.”

Rates differ by compounding convention, annualisation, tenor, credit and collateral. Two rates both quoted at 5% can carry very different funding costs.

A small factory borrows a million for a machine

Three or four articles from this station, applied to one decision. Every number is a teaching assumption for practising the order of judgement, not an estimate of a real market.

A machine costs 1 million yuan, lasts eight years, and is expected to add 250,000 yuan of sales a year while adding 80,000 yuan of labour and power. Depreciation is 125,000 yuan; at a 6% loan rate, interest is about 60,000 yuan a year. Do not stop at “250,000 extra sales”; work out the cash that actually stays in the business.

The bank wants principal back in three years, while the machine takes eight years to pay down. That is a maturity mismatch. If orders fall in year three and the loan is not renewed, the machine may fetch half its book value, and the shareholder has to fill the gap.

Equity does not have to be repaid, but it dilutes ownership. The founder has two questions: in the worst year, can cash cover interest and essential maintenance; in a good year, how much ownership is worth giving up for the “no repayment” insurance.

Lay out three cases — orders on plan, orders at 70%, orders halved — and compute interest cover and year-end cash for each. The conclusion should read “under these conditions, which financing survives a bad year”, not a general verdict that debt is good or bad.

Key terms

These words recur throughout the station. Learn the sense used here before moving into the articles.

money 货币

A generally accepted means of payment that also serves as unit of account and store of value; all three functions depend on acceptability and price stability.

FIN-001 · Functions of Money
credit 信用

A contractual relationship that moves purchasing power across time: the lender gives it up, the borrower promises to repay on stated terms.

FIN-002 · Credit Relationships
compounding 复利

Interest is added to principal and earns interest itself; different frequencies produce different future values and effective annual rates.

FIN-010 · Simple and Compound Interest
present value 现值

A future cash flow converted to today at a given discount rate; the rate, timing and cash-flow assumptions jointly set the answer.

FIN-011 · Present Value and Discounted Cash Flows
risk premium 风险溢价

The excess of expected return over the risk-free rate; it compensates undiversifiable risk, not every kind of fluctuation.

FIN-013 · Risk Premium
cost of capital 资本成本

The return investors require to supply funds and the hurdle a project must clear; it applies only to projects with comparable risk.
