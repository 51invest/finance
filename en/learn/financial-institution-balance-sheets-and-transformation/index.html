Financial Institution Balance Sheets and Maturity/Credit Transformation

Financial institutions turn short-term liabilities into long-term assets, bearing maturity, credit and liquidity risk; capital and liquidity buffers determine how much shock they can absorb.

Definition

A financial institution's balance sheet has assets on the left (loans, securities, cash and interbank assets) and liabilities plus equity on the right (deposits, borrowings, issued debt). Maturity transformation funds long assets with short liabilities; credit transformation turns borrowers' credit risk into claims held by depositors or investors. Net interest margin (NIM) is net interest income divided by interest-earning assets; it measures the asset-liability spread, not risk-adjusted profit.

Intuition

A bank is a maturity transformer: it takes deposits that can be withdrawn at any time and makes loans that mature years later. Depositors want daily access, borrowers want long-term money, and the bank connects them, earning a maturity premium and a credit premium. The machine has two weak points: if many depositors withdraw at once, short-term liabilities can run on long-term assets; if borrowers fail to repay, credit losses on the asset side eat capital. The balance sheet is the map that shows where the money comes from, where it goes and how long it takes to return.

Mechanism

Banks fund long loans with short deposits and earn a term premium; depositors can withdraw at any time while loans are illiquid, creating a liquidity mismatch. Credit transformation puts borrower default risk on the bank, with capital absorbing losses and provisions covering expected loss. Capital adequacy constrains risk-weighted assets, the liquidity coverage ratio requires high-quality liquid assets to cover short-term net outflows, and deposit insurance protects small depositors but not bank capital. Shadow banks and money funds perform similar transformation without the same capital and liquidity requirements [1][2][3].

Worked example

Suppose a bank has CNY 10 billion of assets: CNY 9 billion of five-year loans and CNY 1 billion cash; liabilities are CNY 9 billion of one-year deposits and CNY 1 billion equity. Loan yield is 6% and deposit cost is 2%, so net interest income is 0.54 − 0.18 = CNY 0.36 billion, or NIM of about 3.6%. If deposit rates reprice to 4%, net interest income falls to CNY 0.18 billion. If 20% of loans default with 50% recovery, the loss is CNY 0.9 billion and equity falls from CNY 1.0 billion to CNY 0.1 billion, nearly exhausting the buffer. If depositors run CNY 3.0 billion and cash is only CNY 1.0 billion, the bank must sell loans at a discount. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees and tax.

Second worked example: net interest margin, credit loss and duration gap

Suppose a bank has CNY 10 billion of assets: CNY 9 billion of loans yielding 6% and CNY 1 billion of reserves yielding 1%; CNY 9.2 billion of deposits costing 2% and CNY 0.8 billion of capital. Interest income = 90 × 6% + 10 × 1% = CNY 0.55 billion; interest expense = 92 × 2% = CNY 0.184 billion; net interest income is CNY 0.366 billion, or 3.66% of assets. If 10% of loans, CNY 0.9 billion, are lost, capital falls from CNY 0.8 billion to negative CNY 0.1 billion and the bank is insolvent. If asset duration is five years and liability duration one year, a 1% rate rise changes equity by about −(5 − 1) × 100 × 1% = −CNY 0.4 billion, halving the capital buffer. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees, tax and off-balance-sheet items.

Formal expression

The balance-sheet identity for a financial institution is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Net interest margin can be written as NIM = net interest income / earning assets, and the duration gap as DG = D_A − (L/A) × D_L, where D_A is asset duration, D_L is liability duration, and L/A is the liability-to-asset ratio. A positive gap usually means rising rates depress equity value; a negative gap reverses the direction.

Boundary and common mistakes

First, a high NIM does not prove a good bank; it may reflect higher credit or liquidity risk. Second, capital adequacy is not a liquidity metric; a well-capitalised bank can still fail in a run. Third, deposit insurance protects depositors, not shareholders or creditors, and does not cover every product. Fourth, off-balance-sheet and shadow-banking activities also transform maturity with different disclosure and regulation. Fifth, market-value and accounting losses can differ, and held-to-maturity accounting can delay recognition.

Failure modes

The first failure is treating deposits as permanently stable: retail deposits migrate in stress too, and online banking makes runs faster; model demand and term deposits with separate runoff assumptions. The second is looking only at book capital, not risk-weighted assets: the same 8% capital ratio can hide very different real buffers. The third is ignoring the rate effect on capital: held-to-maturity accounting can defer market-value losses, but the duration gap still hits economic value. The fourth is off-balance-sheet and shadow banking: guarantees, wealth products and conduit lending can return to the balance sheet in stress. The fifth is treating the liquidity coverage ratio as a universal shield: high-quality liquid assets can still be discounted in stress, and intraday liquidity is separate.

Three checks

First, are asset and liability maturities, rates and cash flows matched item by item, and how large is the gap if rates rise or deposits run? Second, is the capital buffer tested against risk-weighted assets and credit-loss stress, and are off-balance-sheet items included? Third, are liquidity sources diversified: deposits, wholesale funding, central-bank facilities and eligible collateral, and can they be used in stress? These three determine whether the bank is a maturity transformer or a leveraged maturity mismatch.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Banks are easily confused with insurers and asset managers. A bank's core is maturity transformation and credit creation, with loans on the asset side, deposits on the liability side and capital and liquidity regulation; an insurer underwrites risk, holds bonds and carries reserves; an asset manager runs client money without putting assets and liabilities on its own balance sheet. Risk sources differ. A counterexample is 'an 8% capital ratio is safe': the same 8% can hide very different risk-weighted asset quality, off-balance-sheet guarantees and duration gaps; the ratio is a regulatory floor, not a stress test. Another is 'deposits are stable': retail deposits migrate in stress and online banking speeds runs; model demand, term and wholesale funding with separate runoff assumptions. Compare financial institutions on balance sheet, risk source and liquidity source together.

Scope and limits

This financial-institution balance-sheet framework applies to banks, insurers and asset managers with public statements, separable interest and non-interest income, and identifiable regulatory capital. If off-balance-sheet guarantees, derivatives and related-party deals are opaque, simple asset-liability maturity comparison understates risk. It does not apply to treating book capital as stress-absorbing capacity, nor to cross-institution comparisons that ignore accounting classification. Separate on- and off-balance-sheet items, then check maturity and liquidity, and recompute under rate, credit and redemption scenarios; capital ratios are not stress tests.

Finally, off-balance-sheet items should be listed separately.

This reconciliation sheet does not start with “worth it” or “not worth it”. Ask when the cash flows occur, who bears which risk, and whether “Balance sheet and net interest margin” and “Maturity and credit transformation” share the same scope. This is a concept article: pin down the definition and the boundary before looking for a policy conclusion.

The first line is Balance sheet and net interest margin: nominal or real, before or after tax, annualised or period value? Is the denominator principal, assets or income? “treating correlation as causation” often comes from mixing two denominators. Write the interest basis, the fee convention and the start date before comparing yields.

The second line is the transmission from Maturity and credit transformation to Capital, liquidity and deposit insurance: fees, tenor, liquidity or credit conditions change one cash flow first, then the result. Order the statement by date, not by percentage; cash arriving today and cash arriving in two days can be two different trades.
