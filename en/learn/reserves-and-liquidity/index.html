Reserves and Liquidity

Reserves are balances that banks hold at the central bank, while liquidity is a bank's ability to pay on time without unacceptable losses; reserves are only one layer of liquidity, and reserve requirements are no substitute for liquidity regulation.

Definition

Reserves usually mean the balances that banks hold at the central bank, used for interbank payments and reserve requirements. For compliance purposes the Board's Regulation D provides that reserve requirements must be satisfied by holding vault cash and, if vault cash is insufficient, by maintaining a balance at a Federal Reserve Bank [1]; elsewhere in this article "reserves" means those balances, and whether vault cash counts depends on the rule being cited. Liquidity is a bank's ability to pay maturing obligations and meet withdrawals on time without incurring unacceptable losses.

Intuition

All amounts in this article are in units of RMB 100 million. A bank holding 12 of reserves can still fail to cover 30 of deposit outflows. Reserves are the first buffer, but securities must be sold at a discount, loans are hard to sell quickly, and wholesale funding may not roll over. Liquidity is the match between all available funds and outflows as they fall due.

The Basel framework uses two ratios to measure liquidity:

Liquidity coverage ratio = high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) ÷ 30-day net cash outflows ≥ 100%
Net stable funding ratio = available stable funding ÷ required stable funding ≥ 100%

The first requires enough unencumbered high-quality liquid assets to cover net cash outflows in a 30-day stress scenario[2]; the second requires a stable funding profile over a one-year horizon relative to assets and off-balance-sheet activities[3]. Jurisdictions set the detailed scope.

Conditions and limits

  1. One bank versus the system. Interbank reserve payments only shift balances and leave the system total unchanged; that total is a Federal Reserve liability and moves with the central bank's own balance-sheet operations and the public's currency holdings. This article does not claim the list is exhaustive: transactions between the central bank and sectors outside the banking system, such as movements of government deposits, also change the total, and that addition is this article's own framing rather than a claim supported sentence by sentence by the registered sources.
  2. Reserve requirements are not liquidity requirements. The Federal Reserve's reserve requirement ratios are now zero, but the three liability types reached zero at different dates: net transaction accounts from March 26, 2020, and nonpersonal time deposits and Eurocurrency liabilities from December 27, 1990[1]; zero does not mean no liquidity need.
  3. The LCR is a stress metric. Haircuts, outflow rates and funding assumptions are set by rules, not forecasts; check the basis across jurisdictions.
  4. Central bank liquidity is not solvency. Lending against collateral is limited by haircuts, quotas and stigma; an insolvent bank needs capital.
  5. Foreign currency liquidity is separate. Foreign liabilities need foreign assets or swap lines; domestic liquidity cannot cover a currency mismatch.

Example and calculation

Using Table 1 (teaching assumptions), deposits fall by 30 and wholesale funding of 10 is not renewed, so net cash outflows total 30 + 10 = 40 over 30 days. Reserves are usable in full and provide 12; securities realize 30 × (1 - 40%) = 18 after a 40% haircut, so the high-quality assets available total 12 + 18 = 30. The LCR is 30 ÷ 40 = 75%, a shortfall of 40 - 30 = 10. The bank must add 10 of high-quality assets or cut outflows to 30 to reach 100%; selling loans may realize a discount and erode equity.

Common mistakes

  • Large reserves mean no liquidity risk. Reserves cover only part of outflows; a funding stop and fire-sale discounts can exhaust the buffer.
  • A zero reserve ratio means no reserves are needed. Payments still require balances, and liquidity rules remain.
  • Liquidity equals capital. Capital absorbs losses; liquidity meets payment timing. They are not substitutes.
  • The central bank can supply unlimited liquidity. Lending requires eligible collateral and does not fix insolvency.

Reading checklist

A final check is the difference between a ratio and a return. A high current ratio can hide slow inventory and weak receivables; a low ratio can reflect efficient working capital. Decompose liquidity into cash, receivables and inventory quality before drawing a conclusion.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Reserves and Liquidity"

The subject of this article is "Reserves and Liquidity". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "reserves". Column two states the transmission channel of "liquidity". Column three states the institutional boundary of "liquidity coverage ratio". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "liquidity" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. liquidity coverage ratio is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Boundary case: The same reserves can leave very different liquidity gaps

Over 30 days, deposits outflow 3 billion yuan and 1 billion yuan of interbank funding is not renewed, for a net outflow of 4 billion. Reserves are 1.2 billion; 3 billion of securities can be converted at a 40% haircut into 1.8 billion, so high-quality liquid assets total 3 billion. The LCR is 75%, leaving a 1 billion gap. If the haircut rises to 60%, only 1.2 billion can be raised, HQLA falls to 2.4 billion, the LCR falls to 60% and the gap widens to 1.6 billion. If outflows are reduced to 2.5 billion, the LCR is 120% and the same portfolio passes.

The conclusion reverses when outflow maturity lengthens: if the 3 billion of deposits matures in 90 days, the 30-day net outflow is only 1 billion and the LCR improves sharply. The boundary is that reserves are a stock, while liquidity is a cash-flow match under stress. Judging liquidity safety by the statutory reserve ratio misses haircuts, maturity and funding concentration.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which is a liquidity requirement, not a capital requirement? A. Common Equity Tier 1; B. Liquidity coverage ratio; C. Leverage ratio; D. Large exposure limit.
  2. Calculation. HQLA are 48 and 30-day net cash outflows are 60 (RMB 100 million). What is the LCR, and how much more HQLA is needed to reach 100%?
  3. Conditions. A bank faces a zero reserve requirement and holds large reserves. Can it still suffer a liquidity crisis?
Suggested answers
  1. B. The other three are capital or exposure constraints.
  2. 48 ÷ 60 = 80%, a shortfall of 60 - 48 = 12; adding 12 of HQLA reaches 100%.
  3. Yes. The reserve ratio only sets a legal requirement; concentrated withdrawals, a funding stop and asset discounts can still cause a crisis.
