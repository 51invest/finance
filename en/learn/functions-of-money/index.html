Functions of Money

Money is what is generally accepted in exchange and settlement. Its roles as a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value lower transaction costs, but acceptance and stable value are conditions rather than guarantees.

Definition

Money is what people generally accept in exchange for goods and in settlement of debts. The European Central Bank describes modern money as fiat money, not tied to a commodity, and summarizes its roles as a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value [1]. OpenStax defines money as what people regularly use when buying or selling goods and services [2].

Intuition

Barter requires a double coincidence of wants: the baker wants shoes, and the shoemaker happens to want bread. Money splits one exchange into a sale and a later purchase, so the seller can choose when and from whom to buy. Money is not an end in itself; its usefulness lies in the goods and services it can buy, and it makes exchange and arrangements across time easier.

Three functions

  1. Medium of exchange. Money enters a transaction as a means of payment, reducing the time spent finding a counterparty and verifying goods.
  2. Unit of account. Prices are quoted in one unit. With n goods, the number of bilateral price ratios falls from n(n-1)/2 to n — that pairing count is this article’s own arithmetic (three goods give three pairs: AB, AC, BC, checked by enumeration in a script); the textbook states the comparison function but never gives the formula[2].
  3. Store of value. Money carries purchasing power into the future. Cash and deposits both store value, but their real purchasing power depends on the price level.

The ECB summarizes money with these three functions; OpenStax also lists a fourth, a standard of deferred payment, which is not developed here [2].

Formal expression

If P is the price level and M is the nominal quantity of money, real purchasing power can be written as M/P: the medium of exchange is used to pay when a transaction takes place, the unit of account defines the price scale, and the store of value depends on how M/P changes over time.

Example and calculation

Suppose a market with only three goods (teaching assumptions; prices are in yuan, CNY):

Good Price
Bread 10 yuan
Shoes 80 yuan
Coat 200 yuan

By the unit-of-account count, three goods need 3 × 2 ÷ 2 = 3 bilateral price ratios, and quoting them in one unit also needs 3 prices; the gap between the two conventions only widens as the number of goods grows.

The baker sells 30 loaves for 300 yuan; this is the medium-of-exchange function. He compares the relative prices of shoes and coats in the same unit; this is the unit-of-account function. He keeps the 300 yuan until next month to buy flour; this is the store-of-value function. If the price level rises by 5% next month, the real purchasing power of those 300 yuan is about 300 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 285.71 yuan, measured at this month's prices.

Conditions and limits

  1. Acceptability. Fiat money has no intrinsic value. Its circulation depends on legal tender rules, the issuer's credibility and social convention [2]. The ECB stresses that acceptance rests on trust that money will keep its value stable [1].
  2. Stable value. High inflation weakens the store-of-value and unit-of-account functions. OpenStax notes that inflation erodes money's buying power each year, yet money remains money [2]; impaired functions do not make it disappear at once.
  3. Liquidity has a cost. The textbook wording is the other direction: a bond that is hard to sell typically has to offer a higher yield[4]. This article inverts that to say cash and demand deposits usually yield least, and treats the convenience itself as a return — the inversion and the "convenience is a return" claim are this article’s reading; the cited page never discusses cash or demand-deposit yields.
  4. Measurement is a convention. The Federal Reserve's H.6 release summarizes M1 as currency, demand deposits and other liquid deposits, and M2 as M1 plus small-denomination time deposits and retail money market funds [3]. Different definitions produce different totals.
  5. Functions can separate. Under high inflation or capital controls, people may store value in foreign currency while paying locally in domestic money. This is the article’s own framing: none of the registered sources discusses currency substitution or dollarization (27.1 contains no such wording) and this article retrieved no statistics, so the point is not presented as a cited finding.

Common mistakes

  • Money is cash. Demand deposits can also be used to pay and are counted in M1 [3]; cash is only one part of money.
  • Money is all wealth. Money is one of the most liquid assets, while wealth also includes housing, equities and other assets.
  • Money is income. Money is a stock and income is a flow; a monthly salary of 10,000 yuan does not mean holding 10,000 yuan.
  • Crypto assets are money by nature. An asset performs the functions of money only when it is widely accepted, stable as a unit of account and able to store value; sharp swings weaken the latter two functions.

Knowledge-point review

functions of money

Definition: Money serves as a medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value and standard of deferred payment, cutting the matching costs of barter.

Mechanism: The functions reinforce one another: general acceptance supports pricing and payment, while stable value supports saving; high inflation weakens the first two.

Example: For example, pricing goods in the domestic currency lets buyers and sellers avoid comparing many barter ratios.

Boundary: Money is not the only store of value; property, bonds and foreign currency also store value, and digital or local currencies can share some functions.

credit relationships

Definition: A credit relationship exchanges resources now for promised future repayment with interest, relying on ability, willingness and enforceable constraints.

Mechanism: Credit is priced through interest, collateral, guarantees, maturity and reputation; information asymmetry and default risk determine spreads and limits.

Example: For example, a bank checks a firm's cash flow before lending, the firm promises quarterly interest, and default damages its credit record.

Boundary: Credit is not mere trust; shocks, fraud, maturity mismatch and implicit guarantees change default odds, and relationship lending can exclude newcomers.

deposits

Definition: Deposits are customers' monetary claims on banks, withdrawable, transferable or pledgeable, with demand, time and notice variants.

Mechanism: Banks use deposits to lend and invest but must hold reserves and liquidity; deposit insurance and regulation limit runs.

Example: For example, demand deposits pay anytime, while time deposits offer higher rates but may lose interest on early withdrawal.

Boundary: Deposits are not cash in a vault; they are bank liabilities exposed to bank risk, insurance caps and negative rates, and stable balances can still lose purchasing power.

base money

Definition: Base money is the sum of currency in circulation and banks' reserves, the central bank's liabilities and the ultimate settlement asset for deposits.

Mechanism: The central bank changes it through asset purchases, lending and open-market operations; reserve ratios, cash leakage and credit demand shape multiplication.

Example: For example, the central bank buys government bonds and pays banks, raising reserves and expanding base money.

Boundary: More base money need not expand broad money proportionally; reluctant lending, weak capital and cash demand weaken multiplication, and QE can sit as reserves.

broad money

Definition: Broad money is the banking system's liabilities to households and non-financial firms, usually including currency, demand and time deposits.

Mechanism: It is shaped by credit creation and base money; lending creates deposits and repayment destroys them.

Example: For example, a 1 million yuan loan credits the firm's account and raises broad money even though currency is unchanged.

Boundary: Broad money is not a quantity the central bank directly controls; credit demand, regulation and innovation change its link to output, and definitions evolve with instruments.

monetary aggregates

Definition: Monetary aggregates classify M0, M1, M2 and others by liquidity and transaction use; M0 is usually currency, M1 adds demand deposits, and M2 adds time and savings deposits.

Mechanism: The layers reflect payment ability and store-of-value functions; definitions vary by country, institution and product, and shadow banking or e-wallets blur boundaries.

Example: For example, a firm's demand deposit is immediately spendable and counts in M1, while a time deposit is less liquid and usually sits in M2.

Boundary: Aggregates are not natural kinds; the same instrument can fall in different layers across countries, definitional changes create false signals, and M2 growth alone cannot judge policy.

Data and institutional check: money

Before using "money" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns medium of exchange.

Third, the institutional boundary. unit of account is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: money

Learning "money" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "medium of exchange": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "unit of account" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of money still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind medium of exchange reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "money" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "medium of exchange" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Numerical boundary

With 100 of currency and 4% annual inflation, purchasing power after three years is 100/(1.04)^3, about 88.9, a loss of 11.1%. Even if it is a medium of exchange and unit of account, inflation weakens the store-of-value function; the three functions cannot substitute for each other.

The boundary is acceptability, legal tender and price stability; a payment instrument can be accepted locally without carrying the full functions of money. These are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which function does each action mainly use: buying a coffee for 12 yuan; depositing 5,000 yuan in a demand account for rent in three months; quoting every good in yuan?
  2. Calculation. You hold 1,000 yuan in cash for a year while prices rise by 4%. At year-end prices, what is the real purchasing power, and roughly what percentage is lost?
  3. Conditions. A country has 40% annual inflation. Domestic money is still used for daily payments, but residents save in foreign currency. Use the three functions to explain what has happened.
Suggested answers
  1. Buying coffee is the medium of exchange; the deposit for rent is the store of value; quoting prices in one unit is the unit of account.
  2. 1,000 ÷ 1.04 ≈ 961.54 yuan, a loss of about 38.46 yuan, or 3.85% of the initial purchasing power. The real loss rate is 1/1.04 - 1 ≈ -3.85%, not 4%.
  3. Domestic money still serves as the medium of exchange because of payment habits and legal-tender status, while the unit of account and store of value shift to foreign currency. The three functions need not be performed by one asset; stable value is a condition for acceptability.
  4. Concept check. Explain "money" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  5. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "money" to "medium of exchange" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.
