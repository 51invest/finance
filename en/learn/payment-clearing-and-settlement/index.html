Payment, Clearing and Settlement

Payment starts a transfer of value, clearing reconciles instructions and calculates what participants owe, and settlement completes the transfer with central bank money or eligible assets; netting saves liquidity but concentrates credit and liquidity risk in the clearing arrangement.

Definition

Payment is the transfer of value from payer to payee. Clearing is the pre-settlement process of transmitting, reconciling and confirming instructions, calculating amounts due between participants and establishing final positions for settlement [1]. Settlement is the actual transfer of funds or securities using central bank money or eligible assets to discharge obligations; completed settlement reaches settlement finality: it is irrevocable and unconditional [1].

Intuition

Amounts below are in yuan (assumed CNY; teaching assumptions). A owes B 100 yuan, B owes C 90 yuan, and C owes A 70 yuan. Gross payments total 260 yuan; after multilateral netting A pays 30 yuan, B receives 10 yuan and C receives 20 yuan, so only 30 yuan has to be settled. Clearing computes the net positions; settlement moves the money. Netting offsets receivables against payables and cuts both the number and the value of payments to be settled, which saves liquidity but concentrates credit and liquidity risk in the clearing and settlement arrangement and in participants' credit [1].

Formal expression

Table 1: Receivables, payables and net positions in a three-party example (units: yuan, assumed CNY; teaching assumptions)

Participant Receivable Payable Net position
A 70 100 -30
B 100 90 +10
C 90 70 +20
Total 260 260 0

Net positions equal receivables minus payables and sum to zero across participants. Gross payments are 260 yuan, net debits total 30 yuan, and the funds needed to settle fall by 230 yuan, or 230 ÷ 260 ≈ 88.5%.

Payment systems usually settle in one of two ways. Real-time gross settlement (RTGS) settles each payment individually, in full, in real time, and needs intraday liquidity. Deferred net settlement (DNS) settles net amounts at the end of a predefined settlement cycle, often at end of day or in several intraday batches, which saves liquidity but defers settlement and concentrates credit and liquidity risk at that point [1]. PFMI words the classification verbatim: "Payment systems can be categorised generally into real-time gross settlement (RTGS) systems, deferred (or designated-time) net settlement (DNS) systems" (a third hybrid category follows), and defines the first verbatim as "RTGS is the real-time settlement of payments, transfer instructions, or other obligations individually on a transaction-by-transaction basis."; the risk side is also quoted verbatim: "Multiple-batch settlement based on a DNS mechanism, for example, may expose participants to settlement risks for the period during which settlement is deferred." [1]

Conditions and limits

  1. Netting depends on law. A netting or set-off arrangement must remain enforceable when a participant becomes insolvent; otherwise courts or liquidators can challenge the net positions and participants fall back on gross obligations [1]. Cross-border netting may not be recognized everywhere. What netting buys is also stated verbatim: "netting reduces the number and value of deliveries and payments needed to settle a given set of trades, thereby reducing liquidity risks and transaction costs" [1].
  2. Finality depends on law and rules. Principle 8 requires verbatim: "An FMI should provide clear and certain final settlement, at a minimum by the end of the value date. Where necessary or preferable, an FMI should provide final settlement intraday or in real time." and its explanatory note defines verbatim that "Final settlement is defined as the irrevocable and unconditional transfer of an asset or financial instrument, or the discharge of an obligation by the FMI or its participants in accordance with the terms of the underlying contract." together with "The value date is the day on which the payment, transfer instruction, or other obligation is due and the associated funds and securities are typically available to the receiving participant." [1]; RTGS is usually final payment by payment, DNS only at the agreed settlement point; receipt of funds and authorization are not settlement, because the same text states verbatim that "A payment is final at the point in time when it becomes irrevocable and unconditional. This precise moment typically depends on the underlying legal regime and the rules of the payment system itself." [1]
  3. Securities and FX use special arrangements. Securities use delivery versus payment (DvP), where the final transfer of securities and of funds are conditioned on each other, which eliminates principal risk; FX uses payment versus payment (PvP), where the final transfer of a payment in one currency occurs if and only if the final transfer in another currency takes place, which addresses cross-currency and cross-time-zone mismatches [1].
  4. Liquidity and default arrangements differ. RTGS needs intraday liquidity and collateral; DNS concentrates exposures at the settlement point and needs pre-agreed default handling and loss-sharing arrangements [1].
  5. Coverage differs. Fedwire is an RTGS for depository institutions whose transfers are immediate, final and irrevocable once processed [2]; the Eurosystem's T2 is an RTGS, T2S settles securities and TIPS settles instant payments in central bank money [3]. Cross-border payments also involve correspondents, time zones and compliance.

Example and calculation

Using Table 1 (teaching assumptions, amounts in yuan): if A must pay B 100 before receiving C's 70, A's intraday liquidity need under RTGS is 100, not the net 30; at the end of the settlement cycle A's net position is still -30. If C defaults before paying, the outcome depends on the settlement mode. Under RTGS, A has already settled its 100 payment to B and simply loses the 70 receivable from C, so the loss is its 70 exposure to C. Under DNS, the netting can be revoked and positions recalculated, so A settles the gross 100 it owes B — 70 more than the net 30 it expected — and again does not receive the 70 from C.

Table 2: Division of labor among payment, clearing and settlement (teaching schematic)

Stage What happens Key risk or condition

Clearing Instructions reconciled, net amounts calculated Counterparty credit, enforceability of netting
Settlement Funds transferred or DvP completed Liquidity, settlement failure

Common mistakes

  • A completed payment is a completed settlement. Authorization and "sent" status may be unsettled; only an irrevocable and unconditional final transfer discharges the debt, and that moment is set by the legal regime and the system's rules [1].
  • Clearing equals settlement. Clearing calculates obligations, settlement transfers value; different institutions may perform each.
  • Netting is always safer. It saves liquidity but concentrates risk at the settlement point; RTGS reduces delay but needs more intraday funds.
  • Central bank systems have no risk. Central bank money has no domestic credit risk, but operational, liquidity and legal risks remain.

Knowledge-point review

payment instruction

Definition: A payment instruction authorises a bank or payment provider to debit an account, specifying amount, payee and conditions.

Mechanism: It passes authentication, balance or limit checks and fraud screening before clearing; error, duplicate and revocation rules shape final risk.

Example: For example, a corporate online payment is checked against signatures, limits and sanctions lists before processing.

Boundary: An instruction is not completion; delays, returns, freezes and fraud can stop it, and authorisation must be separated from execution.

clearing

Definition: Clearing calculates participants' obligations, matches instructions and prepares settlement after a payment or securities trade.

Mechanism: It uses netting or gross methods, central counterparties and margin to manage counterparty risk; membership, collateral and default rules shape efficiency.

Example: For example, banks net thousands of daily transfers and settle only the net difference, reducing funding needs.

Boundary: Clearing is not settlement; netting saves liquidity but accumulates counterparty risk, and central clearing reduces contagion yet can create systemic nodes.

settlement

Definition: Settlement is the actual transfer of funds or securities that discharges cleared obligations and produces final legal effect.

Mechanism: Timing, finality and irrevocability determine credit risk; central-bank money, commercial-bank money and securities depositories are main channels.

Example: For example, a securities trade uses delivery-versus-payment so the buyer pays and receives the security simultaneously.

Boundary: Settlement is not clearing; delayed settlement, intraday overdrafts and legal uncertainty create Herstatt risk, and cross-border settlement faces time-zone and legal gaps.

finality

Definition: Finality means a completed payment or securities transfer is irrevocable and legally recognised.

Mechanism: It depends on settlement-system rules, insolvency law and timing; real-time gross systems finalise each item, while net systems may finalise at day-end.

Example: For example, once a large-value transfer settles, the receiving bank can use the funds and the payer cannot unilaterally reverse it.

Boundary: Finality is not a technical acknowledgement; outages, fraud, insolvency clawbacks and cross-border conflicts can challenge it, and delayed finality widens exposure.

securities settlement

Definition: Securities settlement transfers ownership and payment together or in an agreed sequence, usually through a central depository and delivery-versus-payment.

Mechanism: DVP reduces principal risk, while net settlement saves liquidity but adds replacement risk; cycle length, fail handling and collateral matter.

Example: For example, a stock trade settles T+2, with buyer funds and seller securities exchanged in the depository.

Boundary: Settlement is not trade execution; a completed trade can fail to settle, and fails, short positions and cross-border custody gaps create default and contagion.

custody and asset segregation

Definition: Custody safekeeps securities, records ownership and handles corporate actions; asset segregation separates client assets from the institution's own assets.

Mechanism: Segregation protects clients if the institution fails, and independent custody, reconciliation and audit improve verifiability.

Example: For example, a fund keeps securities at an independent custodian, and the manager can trade but cannot divert the assets.

Boundary: Segregation is not absolute safety; sub-custody, cross-border law, collateral reuse and fraud can pierce it, and nominee structures may hide risk.

Data and institutional check: payment

Before using "payment" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns clearing.

Third, the institutional boundary. settlement is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: payment

Learning "payment" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "clearing": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "settlement" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of payment still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind clearing reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "payment" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "clearing" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. At which stage does multilateral netting occur? A. Payment instruction; B. Clearing; C. Settlement; D. Custody.
  2. Calculation. A pays B 120 yuan, B pays C 80 yuan, C pays A 50 yuan. What are the net positions, and how much less funding is needed than gross (all amounts in yuan)?
  3. Conditions. A system settles net amounts at end of day and participants send instructions in the morning. What risk does the payee face before end of day?
Suggested answers
  1. B. Clearing reconciles instructions and calculates net amounts; settlement moves the funds.
  2. A: 50 - 120 = -70; B: 120 - 80 = +40; C: 80 - 50 = +30 (yuan). Gross is 250 yuan, net debits total 70 yuan, so 180 yuan less is needed, a 180 ÷ 250 = 72% reduction.
  3. Intraday liquidity and credit risk: funds are usable only at end of day; if a payer defaults, net settlement may fail or be recalculated, with guarantees and loss sharing determining the loss.
