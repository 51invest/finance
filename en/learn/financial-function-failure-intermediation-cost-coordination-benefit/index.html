When Financial Functions Fail: Intermediation Cost Above Coordination Benefit

The financial system exists to lower search, assessment, monitoring and settlement costs between savers and investors. When fragmentation, information asymmetry or regulatory friction push intermediation cost above coordination benefit, financial functions fail: cash sits idle while projects cannot borrow. This article uses a loan intermediation-cost account to separate the failure points of six functions.

Definition and intuition

The financial system is usually summarised by six functions: payments and settlement, pooling, intertemporal allocation, risk management, information discovery and governance monitoring. Each has a cost: settlement, search, maturity mismatch, pricing, assessment and agency cost.

Financial function failure is not the absence of a price but intermediation cost above coordination benefit: savers and investors have complementary needs yet cannot transact because search, assessment, monitoring or compliance is too expensive. Failure can be local (small firms cannot borrow) or systemic (banks distrust each other and repo markets freeze)[3].

Judge failure by comparing direct matching cost with intermediation cost. Intermediation is not automatically efficient; it creates value only when scale, specialisation and risk sharing save more than its operating and agency costs[1][2].

Formal expression: cost account

Suppose a one-year loan of 1 million yuan. The bank's funding cost is 2%, operating and staff cost 1%, expected default loss 1.5%, capital charge 0.8% and compliance cost 0.5%. The loan rate must be at least 2+1+1.5+0.8+0.5 = 5.8% to cover cost and leave a thin margin.

If the borrower can afford only 4%, the bank will not lend; the firm turns to family or supply-chain finance, which may demand collateral, guarantees or higher implicit costs. Direct bond issuance may cost 2–4% in underwriting, rating, legal and roadshow fees, prohibitive for small issues.

When intermediation cost exceeds coordination benefit, the market shows 'savings, projects, no transaction'. The problem is not that rates are too high but that risk pricing and compliance leave no spread. Remedies include standardised information, enforceable collateral, larger funding pools, technology that lowers assessment cost, or public risk sharing. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Causes of failure

Information asymmetry: borrowers know more about project risk than lenders, creating adverse selection and moral hazard; higher assessment cost raises rates and drives low-risk borrowers out.

Maturity mismatch: savers want short liquidity, investors want long funding; banks borrow short and lend long, earning a term premium while bearing run risk.

Regulatory friction: capital, liquidity, compliance and licensing raise safety but also unit cost, especially for small and inclusive loans.

Geographic and market fragmentation: cross-border payments, sanctions, capital controls and local licences split one market into pools, reducing scale economies and raising search and settlement costs.

The four can compound: asymmetry raises risk premia, mismatch makes banks cautious, regulation raises cost and fragmentation shrinks pools; small firms are crowded out first[3][4]. OpenStax's markets-and-participants chapter frames the same trade-off between intermediation and direct access[4].

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: intermediation cost is not all bad. Assessment, monitoring and compliance are necessary inputs for risk pricing; cutting them to zero can raise defaults and crises.

Boundary 2: direct finance also costs. Bonds, listings and crowdfunding require underwriting, rating, legal and disclosure costs; direct finance is not inherently cheaper than banks[4].

Boundary 3: public risk sharing creates moral hazard. Guarantees or subsidies lower intermediation cost but can encourage risk-taking; design must tie loss sharing to supervision.

Boundary 4: failure can be cyclical. In crises banks distrust each other and repo markets freeze; the cost account that works in normal times deteriorates under stress.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: less intermediation is always better. Intermediation lowers search and monitoring cost; without it, savers and investors may not match at all.

Mistake 2: high rates mean bank greed. Rates cover funding, risk, capital and compliance; when cost exceeds benefit, not lending is rational.

Mistake 3: technology always lowers intermediation cost. It reduces assessment and payment cost but adds cyber, privacy and model risk; the net effect depends on institutions.

Deeper note: from local cost to systemic freeze

Local and systemic failure differ in mechanism, not degree. In local failure, intermediation cost exceeds coordination benefit in a segment - small-business loans, micro-insurance or small cross-border payments - while funds stay in the system and simply bypass that market. In systemic failure, payments, collateral and funding break down together, participants cannot judge whether counterparties are safe, and trading turns from a price problem into a trust problem.

Commercial paper and repo in 2008 are the classic case: money funds refused paper longer than overnight, repo demanded larger haircuts and banks hoarded liquidity; even borrowers with collateral could not transact. After Silicon Valley Bank failed in 2023, deposit outflows and fear over uninsured deposits pushed regional-bank funding costs up, and intermediation cost moved from a spread issue to a survival issue within days.

An adverse-selection spiral makes failure self-reinforcing. Higher rates should filter out risky borrowers, but low-risk borrowers exit first, leaving a riskier pool; banks raise rates again and the pool deteriorates further. The market ends up with only borrowers who must borrow, the rate no longer reflects average risk, and volume shrinks.

The boundary of policy: public guarantees and liquidity facilities cut trust cost but cannot replace solvency; standardised information cuts assessment cost but needs credible data infrastructure; regulatory forbearance cuts compliance cost but accumulates risk. Judge policy by which cost it reduces, not by how much money it deploys.

Measuring failure uses three indicators: credit spreads over the risk-free rate, bank lending-standard surveys, and time from application to disbursement. When all three worsen together, it is not a single price move but a contraction of intermediation.

Measurement and common misuses

Measuring intermediation cost is not just the loan rate. A fuller measure is all-in borrower cost minus the risk-free rate: interest, arrangement fees, guarantee fees, collateral haircuts, waiting time and compliance cost. Two loans with the same headline rate differ greatly in true cost if one demands 30% collateral and the other is unsecured.

Decompose credit spreads. A wider spread may reflect expected default, liquidity premium, or tax and regulatory cost; treating it as 'risk rising' misreads the policy space. Read spreads with default rates, recovery rates, CDS and issuance volumes to separate 'risk repricing' from 'market freeze'.

Three common misuses: first, equating financial deepening with better financial function, when expansion may only lengthen the intermediation chain; second, equating low rates with easy finance, when low rates may come with higher collateral demands; third, treating public guarantees as a free lunch, when they cut trust cost and also transfer risk.

Ultimately, financial function improves when borrowers of the same risk obtain funds at lower all-in cost and in less time - not when financial institutions' assets or trading volumes grow.

Further reading

Merton and Bodie's functional view argues that financial analysis should start from functions, not institutions: the same function can be performed by banks, markets or funds, and institutional forms change while functional needs persist. Levine's work on financial development stresses that financial structure - bank-based or market-based - matters less than the quality of financial functions. Diamond and Dybvig's bank-run model shows that maturity transformation itself creates liquidity risk, with deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort as the backstop when functions fail.

Read these works with this article's cost ledger in mind: which cost, which failure and which policy tool corresponds to each function. That keeps 'financial innovation' from being automatically read as 'better function', and 'institutional expansion' as 'serving the real economy'. A final caution: functional analysis is a layered tool, not a denial of institutional differences; when different institutions perform the same function, cost, risk and regulatory boundaries still need item-by-item comparison, and the comparison should be repeated as technology and regulation change.

What would change the conclusion

Financial-function failure depends on intermediation structure and regulatory boundaries:

  1. Non-banks replace banks. If money funds, private credit and insurers carry most intermediation, a bank-only cost account misses new failure points.
  2. Regulation subsidises small institutions. If deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort access or licensing protects weak players, failure comes from policy rather than function.
  3. Information costs fall. If digital registries, credit bureaus and collateral valuation lower coordination costs, the intermediation benefit rises and the failure judgement must be recalculated.
