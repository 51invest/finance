The Cash-Flow Statement

The cash-flow statement reports cash receipts and payments over a period in operating, investing and financing activities, and reconciles net income to operating cash flow under the indirect method; it complements the income statement but does not by itself establish a company's value.

Definition

The cash-flow statement reports a company's cash inflows and outflows over a period, classifies them as operating, investing or financing activities, and explains the change in cash from the opening to the closing balance.[1][2]

Intuition

The income statement recognizes revenue and expenses on an accrual basis, but cash may arrive late or be paid early. The cash-flow statement translates profit into actual cash movement: start with net income, remove non-cash items such as depreciation, and adjust for changes in receivables, inventory and payables.

Formal expression

Ending cash = Beginning cash + Operating cash flow + Investing cash flow + Financing cash flow

Under the indirect method, operating cash flow = net income + non-cash expenses - increases in working capital (+ decreases in working capital), with gains and losses on disposal of long-term assets removed (their cash proceeds belong in investing). Investing cash flow mainly covers purchases and sales of long-term assets; financing cash flow mainly covers borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares and paying dividends.[2][3]

Conditions and limits

  1. A period statement: it shows cash receipts and payments over a period, not the full position at the end.
  2. Classification: the OpenStax 16.2 examples classify interest and dividend revenue and interest expense as operating, and dividend distributions as financing.[2] The page does not compare GAAP with IFRS. This article adds the cross-standard caution: align the classification of interest and dividends before comparing statements.
  3. Non-cash transactions: buying assets with shares or converting debt into equity does not enter the cash totals. 16.3 states verbatim that such transactions may be disclosed "either as a notation at the bottom of the statement of cash flow, or in the notes to the financial statements."[3] The decision case in 16.2 says that the example company's policy is a separate statement after the cash-flow statement.[2]
  4. The indirect method reconciles from net income and links naturally to the income statement; the direct method lists cash receipts and payments directly.
  5. Cash definition: restricted cash, foreign-currency cash and exchange-rate effects need separate consideration. This item is the article's own: neither 16.2 nor 16.3 discusses restricted cash, foreign-currency cash or exchange-rate effects on cash equivalents.
  6. Operating cash flow is not free cash flow; the page gives the arithmetic verbatim: "Free cash flow is calculated as cash flow from operating activities, reduced by capital expenditures, the value for which is normally obtained from the investing section of the statement of cash flows." [2], which matches our "deducts capital expenditure".

Worked example

Continuing the income-statement example (teaching assumptions, amounts in yuan, not a real company):

Item Amount (yuan)
Net income 165,000
Add: depreciation 40,000
Increase in receivables (20,000)
Increase in inventory (15,000)
Increase in payables 10,000
Operating cash flow 180,000
Purchase of equipment (70,000)
Investing cash flow (70,000)
Repayment of borrowing (30,000)
Dividends paid (50,000)
Financing cash flow (80,000)
Net increase in cash 30,000
Beginning cash 50,000
Ending cash 80,000

Calculation: 165,000 + 40,000 - 20,000 - 15,000 + 10,000 = 180,000; 180,000 - 70,000 = 110,000; 110,000 - 30,000 - 50,000 = 30,000; 50,000 + 30,000 = 80,000.

Common mistakes

Positive operating cash flow means the company is profitable. Releasing working capital or delaying payments can lift operating cash flow without any improvement in underlying operations.

Capital expenditure can be ignored. Maintenance capital expenditure is a necessary cost of continuing operations.

Operating cash flow is free cash flow. The page defines free cash flow verbatim as operating cash flow reduced by capital expenditures, with that figure normally taken from the investing section [2].

The cash-flow statement cannot be managed. Payment timing, classification choices and one-off transactions all affect the reported cash flow.

Cash flow can replace the income statement. The two are complementary: the income statement shows period performance, while the cash-flow statement shows cash generation and use.

Reading checklist

Cash flow also separates maintenance from growth capex. Maintenance spending keeps existing capacity; growth spending buys incremental capacity. Mixing them overstates or understates free cash flow. Use operating cash flow minus maintenance capex, then assess growth investment on its own return.

Further check

Before applying “The Cash-Flow Statement”, test the weakest link between “cash-flow statement” and “operating cash flow”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Working paper: a four-column check for "The Cash-Flow Statement"

The subject of this article is "The Cash-Flow Statement". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "cash-flow statement". Column two states the transmission channel of "operating cash flow". Column three states the institutional boundary of "investing activities". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "operating cash flow" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. investing activities is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: Positive net income can coexist with negative operating cash flow

Net income is 165,000 yuan, depreciation 40,000, receivables rise 20,000, inventory rises 15,000 and payables rise 10,000, giving operating cash flow of 180,000. Investment is 70,000 and financing is -80,000, so cash rises 30,000 to a closing balance of 80,000. If receivables rise by 60,000 instead of 20,000, operating cash flow falls to 140,000; if inventory also rises by another 30,000, it falls to 110,000 even though net income is still 165,000.

The conclusion reverses when receivables and inventory are seasonal and later collected: if next quarter brings 50,000 of collections and 20,000 of inventory drawdown, operating cash flow rebounds. Reading one quarter alone mistakes seasonality for a quality problem. The boundary is that the cash-flow statement explains sources and uses of cash, while the income statement explains period performance. Substituting net income for cash flow misses working capital and capital spending.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not company accounts.

Cash-flow quality also depends on capital spending and maintenance investment. Positive operating cash flow can still leave free cash flow negative if maintenance spending is higher; growth built on receivables and inventory lowers cash conversion. Linking operating, investing and financing sections shows whether cash comes from operations or from external funding.

Reading and review

The cash-flow statement turns profit back into cash, but the three sections must not be mixed. Operating cash flow shows the core business, investing shows long-lived assets and acquisitions, financing shows debt and equity. Positive operating cash flow with heavily negative investing can be expansion; negative operating with positive financing can be dependence on outside money. Check persistence: receivables, inventory and payables can temporarily lift or depress operating cash flow, and maintenance capex must be separated from growth capex. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Which activity category includes the purchase of equipment?
  2. Calculation. Net income is 80,000, depreciation is 20,000, receivables increase by 10,000, and payables increase by 5,000. Find operating cash flow.
  3. Conditions. A company extends its payable period by 30 days, and operating cash flow rises. Does that prove operating performance improved?
Suggested answers
  1. Investing activities.
  2. 80,000 + 20,000 - 10,000 + 5,000 = 95,000.
  3. No. Extending payment terms is a one-time use of suppliers' funds and may reverse later; also examine operating profit and trends in receivables and inventory.
