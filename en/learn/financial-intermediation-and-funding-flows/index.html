Financial Intermediation and Direct Financing: How Savings Become Investment

Finance is not money breeding money. It rearranges scattered savings, risks and maturities and sends them to projects that need funding. Direct financing puts users of funds in front of investors; indirect financing has banks and other intermediaries collect deposits first and lend afterwards. The two routes allocate cost, information and risk differently.

Definition

Financial intermediation is the process that turns savings in surplus units into funds that deficit units can use. Households, firms and governments can be surplus and deficit units at the same time; the financial system repackages maturity, size, credit and risk so that “I do not need the money now” can meet “I need it now”[1]. Direct financing has the user of funds issue shares, bonds or fund units directly to investors. Indirect financing has banks, insurers or funds raise money from savers first and then deploy it to users.

This article does not rank one route above the other. It puts both on one flow table: who supplies the funds, who bears credit risk, who earns the spread, and when an intermediary is cheaper than a direct match.

Intuition

Think of the financial system as a water network. Savings are water scattered across thousands of households; investment is a factory that needs steady pressure. Direct financing is the factory connecting a pipe to each household: it removes the pumping station but must judge every household’s volume and quality. Indirect financing is a pumping station that collects the water, then sends it through the factory’s pipe: the station bears leakage and mismatch cost.

The intermediary is not there to “add a fee”. It does three things a direct match struggles to do: aggregate small savings into large amounts, convert short deposits into long loans, and use specialist review to reduce information asymmetry. Its spread is a charge for these three services and a payment for bearing risk[2].

When it applies

  • Intermediation adds value when savers and users cannot directly match maturity, size and risk.
  • The intermediary needs stable, relatively cheap funding; otherwise maturity mismatch becomes liquidity risk.
  • The higher the information asymmetry, the more valuable specialist review and monitoring become; review changes risk sharing but does not remove all risk.
  • Direct financing fits standardised assets whose investors can price the risk; complex or relationship-based assets usually still need intermediaries.
  • Capital rules, deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort arrangements change intermediary cost, so book spread alone is incomplete.

Example and calculation

Suppose a bank raises 100 units of deposits at 2.0%, lends 100 units at 6.0%, and incurs 1.0 unit of operating cost and 0.8 unit of expected credit loss. Gross spread is 6.0%−2.0%=4.0%6.0%-2.0%=4.0%6.0%−2.0%=4.0%; after operating and credit cost, net spread is 4.0%−1.0%−0.8%=2.2%4.0%-1.0%-0.8%=2.2%4.0%−1.0%−0.8%=2.2%.

If default probability rises from 2% to 5% and loss given default stays at 40%, expected credit loss rises from 0.8% to 2.0%, and net spread falls to 1.0%. At the same lending rate, higher risk eats the intermediary’s buffer first; it is not automatically pushed onto depositors.

Common mistakes

“An intermediary is just an extra fee.” The fee pays for aggregation, maturity transformation and risk review. If a platform or fund performs those functions, the cost changes its name; it does not disappear[2].

“Direct financing is always cheaper.” It removes the bank spread, but the issuer bears underwriting, rating, investor-relations and disclosure cost. The less transparent the asset, the higher those costs[3].

“Deposits carry no risk.” Deposit insurance and supervision reduce run risk, but real purchasing power still depends on rates, inflation and the institution’s soundness. What is protected is the nominal claim, not its purchasing power.

Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Direct and indirect financing" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Intermediary spread, information and risk bearing is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet.
