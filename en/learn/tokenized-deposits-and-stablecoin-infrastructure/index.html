Tokenized Deposits and Stablecoins: The Ledger of Payment Infrastructure

Stablecoins and tokenized deposits both move “deposits” onto programmable ledgers, but the obligor, reserve assets and redemption rights differ. Safety cannot be read from the on-chain price alone; it depends on reserve cover, redemption order, settlement finality and who bears a run.

Definition

A stablecoin is usually a private issuer’s promise to redeem at par, backed by reserve assets. A tokenized deposit is a bank deposit represented on a distributed ledger; the liability remains the bank’s. A central bank digital currency (CBDC) is a direct central-bank liability. All three can use the same cryptography, but their legal obligations differ[1].

Reserve cover is the market value of reserve assets divided by outstanding tokens; settlement finality is the point at which a transfer cannot be reversed. On-chain confirmation is not legal finality; the issuer, custodian and bank can still fail[3].

Intuition

A stablecoin is like a redeemable locker ticket: the ticket circulates, and the locker holds cash and short-term bonds. If everyone comes at once, whether the assets can be sold at par and in what order decides whether the ticket is truly stable. A tokenized deposit is more like an electronic key to a bank account: the key can change hands, but the account liability stays with the bank.

Technology changes the booking and transfer method, not three old questions: who owes you, can the assets be liquidated, and who stands first in line if something fails.

Formal expression

Let outstanding tokens be (T) and reserve assets (R). Reserve cover is:

$$ C=\frac{R}{T} $$

If redemptions of size (r) occur and liquid assets are sold at haircut (h), post-run cover is:

$$ C'=\frac{R(1-h)-r}{T-r} $$

Settlement cost can be written as:

$$ \text{Cost}=F+v,Q $$

(F) is fixed access cost, (v) is variable cost per payment and (Q) is the number of payments. On-chain transfer lowers some variable costs, but compliance, custody and liquidity costs remain.

When it applies

  • Reserve assets must be liquid, transparent and auditable; short-term bonds can still trade at a discount in stress.
  • Redemption order and legal priority must be clear; the same token can represent different rights in different jurisdictions.
  • Settlement finality must be guaranteed by law and systems, not only by block confirmations.
  • Responsibility across issuer, custodian, bank and wallet provider must be traceable; a technical failure is not an excuse.
  • Cross-border payments still face FX, sanctions compliance and local licensing; on-chain speed is not cross-border legality.
  • Privacy and anti-money-laundering requirements must be designed with payment efficiency, not bolted on afterwards.

Example and calculation

A stablecoin has 100 units outstanding and 100 units of reserves, of which 102 units are quickly realizable; coverage is 102%. If 30 units are redeemed, remaining reserves are 72 and remaining tokens 70, so coverage is about 102.9%. If liquid assets take a 10% haircut in stress, realizable value is 64.8 and coverage against 70 falls to 92.6%.

Outstanding tokens 100 Liability

Post-run cover 92.6% 64.8/70

If 20% of reserves take more than three months to realize, initial cover may still look like 102%, but stress cover can fall below 90%. That is the difference between “fully backed on paper” and “redeemable under stress”.

Common mistakes

“A 1:1 market price means safe.” Market price reflects marginal trades, not large-redemption liquidity and legal order[2].

“A stablecoin is a digital deposit.” Deposits carry deposit insurance and bank supervision; a stablecoin is a private liability with a different claimant[1].

“On-chain settlement removes cross-border cost.” On-chain settlement is fast, but FX, compliance, licensing and custody still cost money; total cost is the whole chain[3].
