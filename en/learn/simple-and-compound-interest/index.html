Simple and Compound Interest

Simple interest is charged only on principal, while compound interest adds interest to principal and earns interest on it; the same rate and horizon can produce different future values and effective annual rates depending on the method and compounding frequency.

Definition

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal; interest is not added to principal. Compound interest adds each period’s interest to principal, so the next period earns on principal plus accrued interest — “interest on interest.” Simple growth is linear; compound growth accelerates over time.

Intuition

1,000 (currency units are assumed; state the currency when applying this to real figures) at 5% for three years. Simple interest is 50 each year, ending at 1,150. Under compounding, year 1 earns 50, year 2 earns 52.50 on 1,050, and year 3 earns 55.13 on 1,102.50, ending at 1,157.63. The extra 7.63 comes from interest earning interest.

Formal expression

Simple interest and future value:

I = PV × r × t
FV = PV × (1 + r × t)

Compound future value:

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n

With m compounding periods per year:

FV = PV × (1 + r/m)^(m×n)

Effective annual rate:

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m - 1

Continuous compounding:

FV = PV × e^(r×t)

PV is the principal (the present value at the start) expressed in currency, e ≈ 2.71828, and rates are always written as decimals. What r, t, n and m stand for depends on the formula, and the uses must not be mixed:

  • I = PV × r × t and FV = PV × (1 + r × t): r is the nominal annual rate and t is years;
  • FV = PV × (1 + r)^n: r is the rate per period and n is the number of periods, and the two must use the same time unit (an annual rate with years, a monthly rate with months);
  • FV = PV × (1 + r/m)^(m×n) and EAR = (1 + r/m)^m - 1: r is the nominal annual rate, m is compounds per year and n is years, so m×n is the total number of periods and r/m is the rate per period;
  • FV = PV × e^(r×t): r is the nominal annual rate quoted on a continuous-compounding basis and t is years.

All amounts in this article are hypothetical teaching figures; when applying them to real products, state the currency, the rate convention and the compounding frequency.

Compounding, as OpenStax defines it, means later periods earn on interest already earned — interest on interest — with future value FV = PV × (1 + r)^n [1].

Conditions and limits

  1. Simple interest assumes no reinvestment. The textbook contrast is verbatim: "Simple interest applies to year 1 while compound interest or “interest on interest” applies to year 2."[1]. Which products actually use simple interest is not asserted here — this site retrieved no per-jurisdiction interest rules, so "short-term money-market instruments, some loans and legal interest" is the article's own illustration. What is checkable is that conventions vary by instrument: the official release footnotes some short-term series "Annualized using a 360-day year or bank interest." and others "On a discount basis."[3].
  2. Compound interest assumes reinvestment at the same rate. Early withdrawal, floating rates, taxes and fees change the result.
  3. A nominal rate must be read with its frequency. 12% annual and 12% monthly differ [2]; compare effective annual rates.
  4. Negative rates are possible. The formulas still work, but future value is below present value.
  5. Inflation, taxes and credit risk are outside the formula. Nominal amounts are not purchasing power.
  6. Continuous compounding is a mathematical limit. It is not every product’s convention.

Worked example

All figures are hypothetical.

Single sum. PV = 1,000, nominal annual rate r = 5%, t = 3 years:

  • Simple: FV = 1,000 × (1 + 0.05 × 3) = 1,000 × 1.15 = 1,150.
  • Compound: FV = 1,000 × 1.05³ = 1,157.63 (1.05³ = 1.157625).
  • Difference: 1,157.63 - 1,150 = 7.63, which is the interest earned on accrued interest in years two and three (2.50 plus 5.13).

Frequency. A nominal annual rate must be read together with its compounding frequency: at 12%, the EAR is exactly 12% when compounded annually and above 12% (about 12.68%) when compounded monthly, since the rate per period is 0.12/12 = 1%. The period-by-period comparison and full worked figures are in the interest-rate types and conventions article (FIN-012) and are not repeated here. The same “12%” is not the same annual rate.

Long horizon. 1,000 at 5% for 30 years: simple 2,500; compound 4,321.94. The gap widens with time.

Common misconceptions

  • Compounding guarantees high returns. No: returns can be negative, and fees and taxes erode them.
  • The same nominal rate means the same return. No: compounding frequency changes the effective rate.
  • Simple interest is always below compound interest. No: that usually holds for positive rates over more than one period; with less than one period or negative rates, do not apply it mechanically.
  • The annual rate is the true annual cost. No: a nominal rate must be read with its compounding frequency[2]; taking U.S. disclosure rules as the example, 12 CFR §1026.4 reads verbatim "The finance charge is the cost of consumer credit as a dollar amount. It includes any charge payable directly or indirectly by the consumer and imposed directly or indirectly by the creditor as an incident to or a condition of the extension of credit"[4], so a charge imposed as an incident to or condition of the credit belongs to the finance charge from which the annualized cost is derived — this site did not retrieve the equivalent Chinese or EU disclosure text, so the item illustrates one jurisdiction and does not claim the same treatment everywhere; and short-term rates are quoted on a 360-day or discount basis[3]. Each of the three changes what the same percentage actually costs.

A nominal rate must be read with its compounding frequency: 1.5% a month gives (1+0.015)^12 − 1, an effective annual rate of 19.56% in the OpenStax worked example [2].

Statement: separate rate, frequency, fees and horizon

Separate four columns: annual rate, compounding frequency, fees and horizon. For 1,000 yuan at 5% over three years, annual compounding gives 1,157.63 yuan and monthly compounding 1,161.47 yuan; frequency adds only 3.84 yuan. At the same 5% over 30 years, monthly compounding gives 4,467.74 yuan, and time turns the 3.84 yuan into 145.80 yuan.

Put fees on the same statement. A 1% annual fee leaves a 4% net rate and 1,124.86 yuan after three years, 32.77 yuan less than without the fee. That 32.77 yuan is already larger than the 3.84 yuan from monthly versus annual compounding. For small short-term accounts, fees matter more than frequency; over long horizons both compound.

Negative rates need their own line. At -5% over three years, simple interest gives 850.00 yuan and compound interest 857.38 yuan. The compound value is higher because each period charges interest on a shrinking base; it is not an improvement in return. When a product advertises 'compounding', ask for the rate sign, fees and horizon first.

Refutation: only compare further if the product beats the alternative by at least 30 basis points on the same horizon, frequency and fee basis. If it changes only frequency and hides fees, the comparison is invalid. The residual risk is early-withdrawal penalties and taxes, which the statement may not show.

Tax changes the ranking. If interest is taxed at 20% each year, a 5% pre-tax rate becomes 4% after tax, and the three-year value is 1,124.86 yuan, close to the 1% fee case. When tax and fees coexist, recompute the net rate year by year as (1+r×(1-t)-f); do not subtract two percentages directly.

Liquidity belongs on the statement too. Early withdrawal from a term deposit may earn the demand-deposit rate, a money-fund redemption has a settlement delay, and a wealth product's 'performance benchmark' is not a guaranteed return. Write horizon, fees, tax and liquidity on four lines before discussing compounding.

Boundary case: frequency, fees, and negative rates

Start with 1,000 at a 5% annual rate for three years. Annual compounding gives 1,000 × 1.05³ = 1,157.63; monthly compounding at 5%/12 gives 1,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)³⁶ = 1,161.47. The frequency adds only 3.84 because the horizon is short and the rate is low. At 30 years, annual compounding gives 4,321.94 and monthly compounding 4,467.74, a gap of 145.80.

Fees can reverse the advantage. A 1% annual fee leaves a 4% net rate, so the three-year value is 1,124.86, or 32.77 less than the no-fee case. That gap is larger than the 3.84 gained from monthly compounding. For a small short-term account, fees usually matter more than frequency; for a long-term account, both compound.

Negative rates also change the direction. At -5% for three years, simple interest gives 1,000 × (1 - 0.05 × 3) = 850.00, while compound interest gives 1,000 × 0.95³ = 857.38. Compounding is 7.38 higher because each period charges interest on a smaller base. That does not make a negative rate desirable; it shows that compounding follows the sign of the rate.

Case Calculation Terminal value
5%, annual, 3 years 1,000 × 1.05³ 1,157.63
5%, monthly, 3 years 1,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)³⁶ 1,161.47
5%, monthly, 30 years 1,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)³⁶⁰ 4,467.74
5%, 1% annual fee, 3 years 1,000 × 1.04³ 1,124.86
-5%, simple, 3 years 1,000 × (1 - 0.15) 850.00
-5%, compound, 3 years 1,000 × 0.95³ 857.38

The boundary is explicit: align horizon, frequency, and fees before comparing products; in the short run fees dominate, in the long run both compound; with negative rates, a higher compound value only reflects a shrinking base. A claim that “compounding makes you earn more” without fees and horizon is not a valid comparison.

Reading and review

Simple interest pays on principal only; compound interest pays on principal and accrued interest. More frequent compounding raises the effective annual rate. A 5% nominal rate compounded monthly gives an EAR near 5.12%; continuous compounding near 5.13%. Compare products by compounding frequency, fee timing and prepayment rules, not the headline annual rate. Time magnifies the difference: over 30 years a 1% fee can consume a large share of terminal value.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Which method charges interest only on the original principal? A. Compound; B. Simple; C. Continuous compounding; D. Effective annual rate.
  2. Calculation. For 2,000 at 6% over four years, what are the simple and compound future values? What is the difference?
  3. Conditions. Product A pays a 12% nominal annual rate compounded monthly; product B pays a 12.2% effective annual rate. Which is higher? Why can the two “12%” rates not be compared directly?
Suggested answers

  2. Simple 2,000 × (1 + 0.06 × 4) = 2,480; compound 2,000 × 1.06⁴ = 2,524.95; difference 44.95.
  3. A is higher: with the formula in this article, EAR = (1 + r/m)^m - 1 at r = 0.12 and m = 12, the effective annual rate is about 12.68%, above B’s 12.2%; a nominal rate without its frequency cannot be compared directly.
