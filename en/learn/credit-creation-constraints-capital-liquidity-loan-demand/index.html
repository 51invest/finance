Constraints on Credit Creation: Capital, Liquidity and Loan Demand

Banks do not simply 'take deposits and lend'; they create credit under three constraints: capital, liquidity and loan demand. This article uses a T-account and a capital-ratio example to explain why the same deposit lets one bank lend 90 yuan and another only 60.

Definition and intuition

Credit creation means banks create deposits when they lend. A 100-yuan loan adds 100 yuan to the borrower's deposit account: a loan on the asset side and a deposit on the liability side, without needing 100 yuan of deposits first.

Banks cannot create credit without limit. Three constraints bind: capital (loss absorption and capital adequacy), liquidity (withdrawals and settlement, liquidity coverage) and loan demand (whether firms and households want to borrow). Capital and liquidity are supply constraints; loan demand is a demand constraint. When both are tight, the credit gap is largest[1][2].

Capital adequacy = capital / risk-weighted assets. Higher risk weights mean less lending per unit of capital. Liquidity coverage = high-quality liquid assets / 30-day net outflow; it constrains a bank's ability to withstand withdrawals under stress.

T-account

Suppose a bank starts with 10 yuan of capital, 90 yuan of deposits and 100 yuan of cash. It makes an 80-yuan loan: assets gain 80 of loans and lose 80 of cash; liabilities gain 80 of deposits. The balance sheet becomes 80 loans, 20 cash, 170 deposits and 10 capital.

With a 100% risk weight, capital adequacy is 10/80 = 12.5%, above the 10% requirement, so the bank can lend more. Another 20 yuan of lending brings loans to 100 and capital adequacy to 10%, exactly at the limit; more lending breaches it.

Liquidity coverage also matters: if 30-day net outflow is 20 yuan, high-quality liquid assets must be at least 20; the 20 yuan of cash just covers it. Whichever constraint binds first sets the lending ceiling. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Formal expression and worked example

Two banks each have 100 yuan of deposits and 10 yuan of capital. Bank A has a 50% risk weight and a 10% capital requirement, so its theoretical loan ceiling is 10/(0.10×0.50)=200 yuan; Bank B has a 100% risk weight, so its ceiling is 10/(0.10×1.00)=100 yuan. With the same capital, A can lend more low-risk loans.

If A's loan demand is only 80 yuan, its 200-yuan supply ceiling is irrelevant; if B's demand is 150 yuan but capital supports only 100, the 50-yuan gap is a supply constraint. To judge a credit gap, read demand first, then capital and liquidity.

In a downturn, expected defaults rise and banks tighten standards; even with adequate capital, supply falls. That is the difference between a capital constraint (regulatory floor) and risk appetite (the bank's own choice)[3][4].

Conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: capital is not the only constraint. Liquidity coverage, leverage ratios, large-exposure limits and provisioning also restrict lending; different banks bind on different constraints.

Boundary 2: loan demand can disappear. If firms do not invest and households do not buy homes, banks cannot lend even with ample funds; monetary easing cannot force borrowing.

Boundary 3: shadow banking bypasses some constraints. Off-balance-sheet vehicles, wealth products and other channels relocate credit creation, but bring new maturity and liquidity risks.

Boundary 4: capital definitions differ. Common equity, additional Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital have different loss-absorption capacity; comparing banks on one ratio is not enough.

Common mistakes

Mistake 1: deposits come before loans. Lending creates deposits; capital and liquidity, not deposits, are the prior constraint.

Mistake 2: a higher capital ratio means more lending. High capital with weak loan demand may not lend more; risk weights and liquidity also bind.

Mistake 3: central-bank liquidity means bank lending. The central bank supplies reserves, but banks face capital, liquidity and risk appetite; monetary easing is not credit easing.

Deeper note: which constraint binds first when all three tighten

Capital, liquidity and loan demand are not parallel lines; they take turns as the binding constraint. In expansions, demand is strong and defaults low, so capital usually binds first; under stress, liquidity tightens first and banks with adequate capital still refuse to lend; in recessions, demand disappears and a high supply ceiling is irrelevant. To analyse a credit gap, first identify which line is active.

Capital has a numerator and a denominator. The denominator is risk-weighted assets: the same capital supports more loans when risk weights are low, and banks can 'save' capital by securitising loans or shifting to lower-risk assets. The numerator is the capital ratio: retained profits, issuance or dividend cuts add capital, but slowly. In crises both worsen at once and banks shrink balance sheets.

Liquidity is about maturity mismatch. LCR requires high-quality liquid assets to cover 30-day net outflows; NSFR requires stable funding for assets over one year. Deposit outflows, wholesale funding rollover stress or triggered off-balance-sheet commitments can tighten liquidity suddenly. The 2023 regional-bank problem was not insufficient capital but uninsured deposit outflows and bond losses that turned liquidity overnight.

Loan demand is the forgotten constraint. Bank surveys often show 'tighter standards' and 'weaker demand' together; if demand is the main driver, relaxing capital and liquidity may not restore credit. Japan in the 1990s and the euro area in the 2010s both had 'banks with money, firms not borrowing'.

Policy implication: capital instruments repair solvency, liquidity instruments buy time, and demand-side tools (fiscal transfers, guarantees, rate cuts) restore willingness to borrow. Mismatching the three produces 'liquidity injected, credit still flat'.

Measurement and policy sequencing

Credit gaps are judged by three cross-checks: the credit-to-GDP gap, bank lending-standard surveys, and the relative move in corporate bond versus loan rates. If credit growth lags nominal GDP, standards tighten and the bond market closes to low-rated issuers, supply constraints are active; if standards are unchanged and rates fall but credit stays weak, demand is the problem.

Look at capital quality, not just the ratio. Common equity tier 1, additional tier 1 and tier 2 have different loss-absorbing capacity; the same headline ratio with large deferred-tax assets or goodwill is weaker in truth. Compare CET1 and leverage ratios, not only total capital.

Liquidity metrics have maturity blind spots. LCR covers 30 days and NSFR one year, but a run can happen in hours; with concentrated or uninsured funding, a passing LCR can deteriorate fast under stress. Liquidity management needs concentration, collateral availability and intraday funding.

Policy sequencing: repair solvency first (capital injection, restructuring), then provide liquidity (discount window, repo facilities), then address demand (fiscal transfers, guarantees, rate cuts). Reversing the order sends liquidity to insolvent institutions or asks demand policy to work with unwilling banks.

A common misuse equates a reserve-requirement cut with credit expansion. A cut releases reserves, but with capital, risk appetite or demand constraints, credit need not grow; it lowers funding cost rather than creating loans directly.

Further reading

Bernanke's 1983 paper brought financial frictions into credit-contraction analysis: deteriorating bank asset quality raises external finance costs and amplifies investment cuts. Kashyap and Stein's work on bank capital and lending extends the capital constraint from regulation to market funding conditions: a undercapitalised bank may be constrained by funding markets even if it wants to lend.

Bernanke and Gertler's financial-accelerator model adds that falling collateral values raise risk premia, and the resulting credit contraction pushes collateral values down further, creating negative feedback. Read these works with the three constraints - capital, liquidity, demand - and each channel's crisis amplification becomes visible. One caution: these models describe mechanisms, not magnitudes; the size of amplification depends on the banking structure, deposit insurance and policy response in each episode, so calibrate before applying them to a specific country or crisis.

What would change the conclusion

Credit-creation conclusions depend on which constraint binds first:

  1. Capital binds first. If bank capital is short, ample reserves do not expand credit; the constraint is capital, not reserves.
  2. Liquidity binds first. If LCR/NSFR tighten, banks hold more high-quality liquid assets and lend less.
  3. Shadow banking bypasses. If credit is created through non-banks and off-balance vehicles, reserve requirements do not bind directly and the monitoring perimeter must widen.
