Rate Conventions and Credit Spreads: Different Labels for the Same Price of Money

The same 5% can be a nominal rate, a real rate, a coupon, a yield to maturity or an annual percentage rate. The difference is not a decimal point. Separate inflation, compounding frequency, credit and liquidity premia before comparing the cost of two funding options.

Definition

A nominal interest rate is the rate written in a contract or quote, before inflation. A real interest rate is the rate that raises purchasing power after inflation. The effective annual rate (EAR) converts compounding within the year into a comparable annual figure. A credit premium compensates default risk; a liquidity premium compensates an asset that is hard to sell or finance quickly[1].

This article does not ask which rate is “most real”. It separates the rate into three layers: purchasing power (nominal and real), time (maturity and compounding), and risk (credit and liquidity). Two rates are comparable only when all three layers match.

Intuition

A rate is like a price tag: the nominal rate is the number on the label, the real rate is the net content after removing the packaging, and the effective annual rate converts different package sizes into a price per kilogram. A 5% label can mean very different burdens under 2% inflation, 12 compounding periods and a 3% credit premium.

Compounding frequency is how often interest rolls back into principal. Once a year, 5% is 5%; monthly, the year-end amount is slightly more; the more frequent the roll, the higher the effective annual rate. The gap is small at short horizons but becomes real money over long periods and large balances[2].

When it applies

  • Before comparing two rates, align maturity, compounding, day-count basis and tax treatment.
  • The Fisher equation assumes inflation is the only change in purchasing power; tax, fees and default change the result again.
  • EAR is suitable for products in the same currency and maturity; cross-currency comparisons also need exchange rates and capital controls.
  • A credit premium can be priced only when default probability and recovery are estimable; a liquidity premium depends on market depth and financing arrangements.
  • The benchmark must match the asset’s maturity and cash flows; an overnight rate cannot price a thirty-year liability.

Nominal rate 5.0%, inflation 2.0%. The exact real rate is (1.05/1.02)−1=2.94%(1.05/1.02)-1=2.94%(1.05/1.02)−1=2.94%; the approximation gives 5.0%−2.0%=3.0%5.0%-2.0%=3.0%5.0%−2.0%=3.0%, about 0.06 percentage points too high. With monthly compounding, EAR is (1+5.0%/12)12−1=5.12%(1+5.0%/12)^{12}-1=5.12%(1+5.0%/12)12−1=5.12%.

Reading and review

The “rate” on one loan has at least three measures: nominal, real and effective annual. With 5% nominal and 3% inflation, the real rate is about 2%; monthly compounding lifts the EAR to about 5.12%; credit and liquidity premia can make the borrower pay far more than the benchmark. Compare quotes on one compounding frequency, fee and risk basis; the headline coupon hides compounding and fees. Common mistakes

“5% is 5%.” Nominal, real, effective annual and coupon rates are four conventions; 5% with different compounding is not directly comparable[2].

“The real rate is nominal minus inflation.” That is an approximation; the error grows with inflation and the rate level. The exact form is (1+i)/(1+π)−1(1+i)/(1+\pi)-1(1+i)/(1+π)−1.

“Credit premium is extra profit.” It must cover default loss first; if default probability is underestimated, the premium consumes principal[3].

The second line is the transmission from Compounding frequency and effective annual rate to Credit and liquidity premia: fees, tenor, liquidity or credit conditions change one cash flow first, then the result. Order the statement by date, not by percentage; cash arriving today and cash arriving in two days can be two different trades.

Boundary case: A 5% nominal rate is not the same price under different conventions

A 5% nominal rate with 2% inflation gives a Fisher real rate of about 2.94%. With a 20% tax and a 1% fee, net nominal is about 3.0% and the real rate about 1.03 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = 0.98%. Monthly compounding gives an effective annual rate of about 5.12%. A 3% credit spread adds three points to yield, but expected loss and recovery change the realised return.

The conclusion reverses when inflation or fees change: at 4% inflation, the same 5% leaves only about 0.96% real; if fees are charged on assets rather than returns, the drag is larger in a low-return environment. The boundary is that nominal, real, coupon, yield-to-maturity and annual-percentage labels are different. Comparing two funding costs requires aligning maturity, compounding, day-count, tax and credit assumptions first.
