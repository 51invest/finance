Client Asset Custody: Segregation, Rehypothecation and Institution Failure

Client assets are not the institution's own assets; custody is about segregation and traceability. If client assets are commingled, rehypothecated or lent to counterparties, they become general claims in a failure. The longer the custody chain, the harder the recovery.

Definition and intuition

Client assets are cash and securities that clients entrust to an institution for safekeeping, trading or management; they are not the institution's own assets. Custody is the service of holding and recording client assets; segregation separates client assets from the institution's own assets and from other clients; rehypothecation reuses client assets as collateral.

The purpose of segregation is not to prevent failure but to let clients recover their assets if the institution fails. If assets are commingled, clients become general creditors and queue with others.

The custody chain usually runs client-adviser-broker-custodian bank-central securities depository. Longer chains complicate account control, reconciliation and legal recovery; cross-border custody adds local insolvency law[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose client A gives 10 million yuan of securities to an adviser, who places them with a broker, who deposits them at a custodian bank. If the adviser commingles client assets with its own, A becomes a general creditor in the adviser's insolvency and may recover only 40%, or 4 million.

If the assets are segregated in a client account with complete names and beneficial-owner records, A can claim the securities and recover close to 100%, though after insolvency proceedings, custody fees and legal costs.

If the broker rehypothecates the securities to a counterparty, that counterparty can dispose of the collateral on default; A's claim ranks behind it. Deeper rehypothecation makes recovery harder. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three checks

Check 1: account segregation. Are client assets in separate accounts, titled in the client's name or with beneficiary records, and separate from own assets?

Check 2: rehypothecation authority. Does the contract allow lending, pledging or reuse of client assets? What are the scope, counterparties and substitute-asset terms?

Check 3: custody chain and reconciliation. Do the custodian bank, sub-custodian and depository accounts reconcile? Is there an independent audit and client statement?

Recovery in a failure depends on legal structure, account records and whether assets remain in the custody chain; not all 'client assets' enjoy equal protection[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: segregation is not insurance. It protects ownership, not value; market risk stays with the client.

Boundary 2: cash and securities differ. Client cash may be deposited at a bank and limited by deposit insurance; securities custody follows different rules.

Boundary 3: cross-border custody follows local law. Sub-custodian insolvency, exchange controls and asset freezes affect recovery.

Mistake 1: a large custodian is safer. Size does not imply good segregation; the chain, rehypothecation and audit quality matter.

Mistake 2: client assets are always off the balance sheet. Segregated assets should not count as own assets; commingling or rehypothecation changes their legal status.

Deeper note: legal ownership, commingling and rehypothecation

The core of client assets is not 'which account' but 'who owns them in law'. In custody, the client remains the owner and the custodian is only a keeper; if the custodian fails, client assets should not enter the insolvency estate. But that conclusion depends on local law, account structure and contract terms, and cannot be assumed.

Segregated versus omnibus: segregated accounts keep each client's assets apart and are easy to identify in insolvency; omnibus accounts pool several clients' assets and require records to allocate, and if records are incomplete clients may recover pro rata or even be treated as general creditors. Institutional clients often use omnibus to cut costs but need stronger reconciliation and records.

Rehypothecation is the custodian or broker lending or pledging client assets onward. It adds market liquidity and exposes client assets to counterparty risk. Contracts often cap it (say 140% overcollateralisation), but under stress collateral values fall and clients may not get assets back in time.

Lessons from Lehman and MF Global: client assets can be frozen for years in insolvency and clients recover only part; commingling, rehypothecation and cross-border legal differences complicate claims. When choosing a custodian, check the legal opinion on bankruptcy remoteness, account structure, rehypothecation policy and insurance.

Operational controls include daily reconciliation, independent custody, asset identification, corporate-action handling, cash sweeps and cross-border transfers. When controls fail, client assets can be misused or returned late.

Regulation usually requires segregation, no commingling with proprietary assets, periodic reporting and independent audit; rehypothecation needs disclosure, limits and client consent. Cross-border custody also depends on whether local law recognises client-asset priority.

Common misuses: treating 'a large custodian' as 'safe assets', ignoring bankruptcy remoteness; treating 'a balance in the account' as 'legally the client's', ignoring commingling and rehypothecation; treating a licence as 'asset insurance', ignoring cross-border legal differences.

Finally, client-asset protection is a three-part arrangement of law, operations and contract. Investors should ask: whose books hold the assets, who owns them in law, can they be rehypothecated, and how are they allocated in insolvency. If the four answers are unclear, custody risk has not been managed.

Stress scenarios: what happens when the custodian fails

Scenario one, custodian insolvency: if client assets are legally segregated and records are complete, they should be returned or transferred to a new custodian relatively quickly; if commingled or poorly recorded, clients may recover pro rata or be treated as general creditors. Return can take months or years.

Scenario two, rehypothecation counterparty defaults: client assets lent onward leave clients queuing as secured creditors; if collateral value is insufficient, recovery is partial. Contractual rehypothecation caps and overcollateralisation determine the loss.

Scenario three, cross-border custody: assets sit in another jurisdiction with different insolvency procedures, priorities and freezing rules; clients may need local counsel and more time. Confirm that local law recognises client-asset priority.

Response list: keep account structures and asset records, require independent custody and reconciliation, limit rehypothecation, buy insurance, prepare a backup custodian and rehearse transfers. Client-asset protection is ongoing operations, not a signed contract.

Due-diligence questions: whose books hold the assets, who owns them in law, can they be rehypothecated, and how are they allocated in insolvency. If the four answers are unclear, custody risk is not managed.

Key takeaways

  • Client-asset protection turns on legal ownership, not account location; bankruptcy remoteness depends on local law and contract.
  • Segregated accounts are easy to identify and return; omnibus accounts rely on records, and incomplete records mean pro-rata recovery.
  • Rehypothecation adds liquidity and exposes client assets to counterparty risk; caps and overcollateralisation determine losses.
  • Lehman and MF Global show client assets can be frozen for years, with cross-border custody adding complexity.
  • Operational controls include daily reconciliation, independent custody, asset tagging, corporate actions and cross-border transfers.
  • Four due-diligence questions: whose books, legal owner, rehypothecation, insolvency allocation.

What would change the conclusion

Client-asset protection depends on effective legal segregation:

  1. Segregation is legally enforceable. If client assets truly rank ahead and are not commingled in insolvency, custody protection holds.
  2. Rehypothecation is disclosed. If reuse, pledging and securities lending are disclosed and priced, the risk can be borne.
  3. The custodian is systemically important. If it is too big to fail, the resolution regime and bridge arrangements decide how fast assets move.
  4. Cross-border custody. If assets sit in several jurisdictions, insolvency law and asset freezes affect return speed.

Practical implication: client-asset protection depends on legal segregation, rehypothecation disclosure and the custodian's resolution framework; a contract saying 'segregated' does not guarantee quick return in insolvency.

Data note: check account title, legal jurisdiction and rehypothecation authority; master and sub-accounts are segregated to different degrees.

Scope note: stress-test custodian insolvency, cross-border freezes and rehypothecation chains; contractual segregation is only the first layer, and actual recovery depends on resolution speed and asset location.

Final note: legal title is not timely recovery; resolution speed, asset location and rehypothecation chains decide actual recovery.

Check: walk the insolvency waterfall and asset location before relying on the segregation clause.
