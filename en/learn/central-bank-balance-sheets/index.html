Central Bank Balance Sheets

A central bank balance sheet lists securities, lending and foreign reserves as assets and currency in circulation and bank reserves as liabilities; it records how the monetary base is created and shows why a central bank does not need deposits before expanding.

Definition

A central bank balance sheet records its assets, liabilities and capital at a point in time and follows assets = liabilities + capital. The liability side is mainly currency in circulation and bank reserves, which together make the monetary base; the asset side is mainly securities, lending to banks and foreign reserves.

Intuition

A commercial bank must fund its assets with deposits and other liabilities; a central bank does not need deposits before buying assets. When it buys a security, it simply credits the selling bank's reserve account, so assets and liabilities rise together; open market operations adjust the supply of reserve balances exactly this way[2]. Reserves are the central bank's own liability, so expansion is not limited by "cash on hand"; the real constraints are inflation, the exchange rate and credibility.

Formal expression

Securities 4,200 Currency in circulation 2,300
Lending to banks 600 Bank reserves 3,200
Foreign reserves 800 Government deposits 300
Other assets 400 Other liabilities 100
Total liabilities 5,900
Capital 100
Total assets 6,000 Total liabilities and capital 6,000

Assets are 6,000 = liabilities 5,900 + capital 100, all in RMB 100 million. The monetary base is currency 2,300 + reserves 3,200 = 5,500, or 5,500 ÷ 6,000 = 91.7% of assets. Buying 100 of securities raises securities and reserves by 100 each; selling has the opposite effect. The "capital" used here is the item FIN-017 defines as equity - assets minus liabilities - keeping the word customary on a central bank balance sheet. That is this article's terminology ruling dated 3 November 2026; it does not claim jurisdictions use the same disclosure wording, and it is not a judgement on whether any specific central bank is adequately capitalised.

Conditions and limits

  1. Domestic liabilities versus foreign assets. Domestic currency can be created, but foreign reserves cannot; sustained intervention at a fixed exchange rate can exhaust them.
  2. Negative capital does not stop operations at once. A domestic-currency central bank can still create liabilities, but persistent losses damage independence and credibility.
  3. Book value is not market value. Securities are carried at amortized cost or fair value; rising rates create unrealized losses that can exceed thin capital.
  4. Measurement differs. The Fed's H.4.1 Table 1 reports securities held and reserve balances weekly[1]; the ECB's annual balance sheet consolidates the Eurosystem national central banks and the ECB vis-à-vis third parties at year-end[3]. The two are not directly comparable.
  5. Reasons for expansion differ. Open market purchases[2], lending and FX intervention all enlarge the sheet, but their policy meanings differ; size alone does not reveal the stance.

Example and calculation

Using Table 1 (teaching assumptions, amounts in RMB 100 million), the central bank buys 100 of securities from a bank: assets rise from 6,000 to 6,100, liabilities from 5,900 to 6,000, and capital stays at 100; the monetary base rises from 5,500 to 5,600 (currency 2,300 plus reserves 3,300). The transaction is between the bank and the central bank; non-bank deposits do not rise.

If securities fall 5% in market value and are revalued to market, the loss is 4,200 × 5% = 210 and it falls on capital: capital drops from 100 to 100 - 210 = -110, assets become 5,790 while liabilities stay at 5,900, and 5,790 - 5,900 = -110 is capital. Before the loss, capital of 100 was only 100 ÷ 6,000 ≈ 1.7% of assets.

Common mistakes

  • Expanding the balance sheet is printing money. It creates reserves, but whether they become broad money depends on bank lending and public cash holdings.
  • A central bank can expand without limit. The nominal constraint is weak, but inflation, the exchange rate and credibility bind.
  • A larger balance sheet means looser policy. Size also reflects the economy, cash demand and institutions; asset composition and interest rates matter.

Knowledge-point review

central bank balance sheet

Definition: A central bank balance sheet lists assets such as government bonds, loans and foreign exchange, and liabilities such as currency, reserves and central-bank bills.

Mechanism: Asset purchases expand liabilities and base money, while sales or maturities contract them; foreign-exchange inflows, Treasury balances and rate tools change composition.

Example: For example, buying government bonds raises assets and banks' reserve liabilities, expanding base money.

Boundary: A central bank sheet is not a corporate one; it can operate with negative capital, asset quality and political constraints matter, and expansion does not guarantee inflation.

reserves

Definition: Reserves are banks' deposits at the central bank plus vault cash, used for settlement, reserve requirements and liquidity buffers.

Mechanism: They split into required and excess reserves; open-market operations, reserve ratios and rate corridors adjust quantity and price.

Example: For example, a bank must hold a fraction of deposits as reserves and can keep excess reserves at the central bank for interest or payments.

Boundary: Reserves are not free cash for lending; capital, credit demand and regulation also constrain, and reserve-ratio changes depend on the regime and liquidity environment.

settlement

Definition: Settlement is the final transfer of funds or securities that discharges obligations, usually through central-bank accounts or a central securities depository.

Mechanism: Finality, timing and counterparty risk shape payment-system stability; real-time gross, netting and intraday overdrafts are different arrangements.

Example: For example, a large interbank transfer settles individually in a central-bank real-time gross system and becomes irrevocable.

Boundary: Settlement is not clearing; clearing computes obligations while settlement moves assets, and outages, liquidity shortfalls and legal finality can cascade.

open-market operations

Definition: Open-market operations are central-bank purchases or sales of securities and repos to adjust reserves, short-term rates and liquidity.

Mechanism: Purchases or reverse repos inject funds, while sales or repos withdraw them; maturity, collateral and counterparties shape transmission.

Example: For example, a seven-day reverse repo injects short-term funds to primary dealers and lowers money-market rates.

Boundary: Open-market operations are not a universal fine-tuner; collateral scarcity, market segmentation and rate corridors limit transmission, and central banks may be forced to offset fiscal or FX flows.

standing facilities and operating framework

Definition: Standing facilities are central-bank lending or deposit windows that financial institutions may use actively, while the operating framework combines rate corridors, reserves and open-market operations.

Mechanism: A corridor sets a lending ceiling and deposit floor, with open-market operations managing scarcity; the framework determines short-rate stability.

Example: For example, a bank short of liquidity can borrow at the standing lending facility and park excess cash at the deposit facility, keeping market rates inside the corridor.

Boundary: The framework is not one tool; collateral, counterparties, Treasury accounts and FX intervention shift the corridor, and crises can move from price to quantity tools.

Data and institutional check: central bank balance sheet

Before using "central bank balance sheet" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns monetary base.

Third, the institutional boundary. reserves is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: central bank balance sheet

Learning "central bank balance sheet" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "monetary base": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "reserves" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of central bank balance sheet still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind monetary base reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "central bank balance sheet" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "monetary base" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. After the central bank buys securities, which item rises at the same time? A. Currency in circulation; B. Bank reserves; C. Government deposits; D. Bank capital.
  2. Calculation. A central bank has assets of 8,000, liabilities of 7,700 and capital of 300 (RMB 100 million). It sells 200 of securities and reserves fall by 200. What do assets and liabilities become, and what happens to the monetary base?
  3. Conditions. A country fixes its exchange rate and the central bank keeps buying domestic currency and selling foreign exchange. What limits its balance-sheet expansion?
Suggested answers
  1. B. Securities and reserves both rise; currency, government deposits and capital do not.
  2. Assets 8,000 - 200 = 7,800; liabilities 7,700 - 200 = 7,500; capital remains 300 (7,500 + 300 = 7,800). The monetary base falls by 200 because reserves do; the exercise gives no currency figure, so the level of the base cannot be computed.
  3. Foreign reserves. Domestic currency can be created, but sellable foreign exchange is limited; a fixed exchange rate commitment restricts intervention and can feed depreciation expectations.
  1. In 100 words, state the core metric in "FIN-005", including its denominator and unit.
  2. Write "central bank balance sheet" and "monetary base" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "reserves" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
  6. Write one falsifiable claim with an observable signal, a waiting period and a review metric.
