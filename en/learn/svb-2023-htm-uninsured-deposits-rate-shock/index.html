SVB 2023: HTM Securities, Uninsured Deposits and a Rate Shock

A bank with $209 billion of assets failed within two days. Put the timeline, deposit structure and securities portfolio together to see how a rate shock became a liquidity run through uninsured deposits.

Background and timeline

From 2020 to 2022, SVB’s total assets grew from under $60 billion to $209 billion, funded mainly by deposits from technology and venture-backed firms. Deposits gathered in the low-rate period were invested heavily in long-dated Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities. When rates rose sharply in 2022, the market value of those securities fell, but most were classified as held-to-maturity (HTM), so the losses did not run through current profit[4].

Deposits began to leave in the second quarter of 2022. On 8 March 2023, SVB announced a sale of available-for-sale securities with a loss of about $1.8 billion and a plan to raise $2 billion of capital. On 9 March, the shares fell about 60%, roughly $42 billion of deposits left in a day, and another $100 billion was queued for withdrawal.

On 10 March, the California regulator closed the bank and the FDIC became receiver[4]. On 12 March, the FDIC, the Federal Reserve and the Treasury invoked the systemic risk exception and protected all depositors, including uninsured ones. On 26 March, First-Citizens took over the bridge bank; the FDIC estimated the resolution cost at about $20 billion.

The event did not stop at one bank: Signature Bank was closed soon after, and the market began to reprice every bank with a high share of uninsured deposits and long securities duration[4].

Information available and actors

SVB’s customer base was concentrated: deposits came mainly from technology, life-science and venture-capital-backed firms, with balances far above the $250,000 insurance limit. At the end of 2022, about 94% of deposits were uninsured, so once confidence moved, most depositors had an incentive to run first[4]. Management faced a dilemma: selling securities would realise losses and expose a capital gap; not selling would fail to meet outflows.

The 8 March loss announcement and capital plan were meant to rebuild capital early, but to uninsured depositors they read as evidence that the bank was already impaired. The announcement itself changed the speed of the run. Regulators also faced a timing constraint.

If liquidity support had arrived before 9 March, the bank might not have had to sell securities at a discount; if only insured deposits were protected, uninsured depositors would run faster; if the systemic risk exception was invoked, deposits would stabilise in the short run but the cost would move to the deposit insurance fund and a special assessment on the industry.

Outcome and the number chain

The core numbers form a chain: $209 billion of assets, $175.4 billion of deposits, about 94% uninsured; roughly $42 billion of outflow in one day and another $100 billion queued; about $1.8 billion of realised securities losses and a $2 billion capital plan; about $20 billion of resolution cost[4]. The key is not any single number but the ratio and the speed. $42 billion was about 24% of deposits, and the queued $100 billion was about 57%; together they covered nearly all deposits.

The $1.8 billion loss was only 0.9% of assets, but relative to capital and the liquidity buffer it was enough to trigger a confidence collapse. Multiplying the uninsured share by securities duration gives a rough run-exposure indicator. A 94% uninsured share means almost every deposit can leave overnight; long duration means the bank loses more the more urgently it sells.

Only when both conditions hold does “assets look sufficient, cash does not” appear.

Mechanism

The mechanism has four steps. First, higher rates lower long-duration securities prices. Second, HTM accounting keeps the loss off the income statement and the capital ratio still looks adequate.

Third, deposit outflows force securities sales and turn the book loss into a realised loss. Fourth, the loss announcement triggers a coordinated run by uninsured depositors, and the outflow outpaces any asset sale[4]. This is not simple insolvency but a mismatch in liquidity and maturity between assets and liabilities.

The market value of assets may still exceed liabilities, but selling them within days brings a large discount; depositors do not need to prove eventual insolvency, only to believe others will run first. A self-fulfilling run can close a bank before fundamentals are exhausted.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, if deposits gathered in 2020–2021 had been invested in short-duration securities or hedged for rates, the price fall, realised loss and capital gap would all be smaller. Second, if deposit insurance had covered a larger share, or regulation had required large technology deposits to be diversified, the run incentive for uninsured depositors would fall. Third, if the 8 March capital plan had locked in investors before announcement, the market might have read it as “capital is already secured” rather than “the gap is exposed”.

Fourth, if regulators had provided full liquidity support on 9 March, the bank could have avoided distressed sales, but moral hazard and later political cost would rise. These counterfactuals are not retrospective blaming; they separate four variables: rate shock, liability structure, information timing and policy response. Change any one and the outcome can differ.

That is why “all banks would fail” or “regulators were simply wrong” cannot summarise the event.

Limits to generalization

What the case can show: with a high uninsured-deposit share and a long-duration securities portfolio, a rate shock can become a liquidity crisis quickly; announcements and policy response change run speed; the cost is not only shareholder loss but also the deposit insurance fund and industry assessment. What it cannot show: that every bank with high uninsured deposits would fail; that the $20 billion cost is the universal price of bank rescue; that SVB’s client structure applies to retail-funded banks; or that one episode settles the overall merits of HTM accounting.

Reproduction checklist

Reproduce by listing four dates: loss announcement, run day, closure, resolution. Then four numbers: assets, deposits, uninsured share, one-day outflow. Then three policy variables: accounting classification, deposit insurance coverage, systemic risk exception.

Finally write one counterfactual and one non-extrapolation boundary. Only with all four types of information can the case be reviewed; missing any one lets story replace mechanism[4]. Source note: institutional and mechanism background in [1][2][3]; case facts in [4].

Decision review

Separating decisions by actor reveals three different kinds of rationality. Shareholders and management preferred to delay loss recognition and buy time to raise capital; uninsured depositors preferred to move money first and leave queue risk to others; regulators preferred to avoid public rescue but had to protect depositors once systemic risk appeared. The three rationalities together closed the bank before its fundamentals were fully exhausted.

Reading the episode only as “management failure” misses the liability structure: the 94% uninsured share was not built in a day but resulted from years of serving technology clients. That structure was an advantage when rates were low and deposits flowed in, and a vulnerability when rates rose and deposits flowed out. Risk management is not asking afterwards “why not sell securities” but asking beforehand “if 30% of deposits leave in one day, do we have cash?” Accounting and capital come next.

HTM classification keeps the book capital ratio stable, but it changes neither the market value of the securities nor depositor confidence. Capital regulation that looks only at book values understates liquidity risk in a rate shock; regulation that looks only at market values forces loss recognition under stress. Both measures have uses; disclosure and stress tests must cover both.

Resolution is the last layer. The FDIC created a bridge bank, sold assets and estimated a cost of about $20 billion. That cost is not “the whole loss of rescuing the bank” but the net cost to the deposit insurance fund; shareholders and some creditors bore other losses.

Mixing resolution cost, shareholder loss and systemic risk produces the wrong policy conclusion.
