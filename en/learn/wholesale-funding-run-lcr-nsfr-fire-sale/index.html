Wholesale Funding Runs: LCR, NSFR and Fire Sales

Wholesale funding is not deposits, but it is more sensitive: repos, interbank certificates, commercial paper and bonds can stop rolling instantly under stress. LCR covers 30-day survival and NSFR the one-year structure; a bank can pass both and still run on collateral discounts and concentration.

Definition and intuition

Wholesale funding is non-deposit liabilities from interbank markets, money funds, insurers, corporates and institutional investors: repos, interbank certificates, commercial paper, bonds and securitisations. It is usually collateralised, short-dated and concentrated.

A run is creditors simultaneously redeeming or refusing to roll. Deposit runs are slowed by branches, withdrawal limits and insurance; wholesale runs can happen in hours: counterparties stop repo, certificates are not rolled and commercial paper finds no buyer.

LCR = high-quality liquid assets / 30-day net outflow, ensuring a 30-day survival horizon; NSFR = available stable funding / required stable funding, ensuring a stable one-year structure. Passing both does not eliminate run risk[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a bank has 100 billion yuan of wholesale funding, 30 billion maturing within seven days; high-quality liquid assets are 20 billion and 30-day net outflow is 25 billion, so LCR = 80%, below the 100% requirement.

If the 30 billion is not rolled, the bank must sell assets. A 5% discount costs 1.5 billion; a 15% discount costs 4.5 billion and may erode capital. Fire sales depress prices, impair other banks' collateral and create a negative feedback loop.

If NSFR is 95%, stable funding is insufficient; even with an adequate LCR, the long-term structure is fragile. The bank can shorten assets, issue longer debt or hold more HQLA, at the cost of yield. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three amplifiers

Amplifier 1: collateral discounts. Repo funding is collateralised by bonds; when bond prices fall, haircuts rise and the bank must post more collateral or repay early.

Amplifier 2: concentration. A few counterparties provide most funding; one exit creates a gap. Money funds, securities lenders and sovereign funds are especially sensitive.

Amplifier 3: accounting and ratings. A rating downgrade triggers contract terms, collateral calls and investor redemptions; fair-value accounting exposes losses earlier.

Regulation can require LCR/NSFR, limit concentration and provide standing repo, but cannot eliminate fire sales; banks must manage maturity and collateral buffers[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: LCR is a stress assumption, not a forecast. Net outflow is a regulatory scenario; real runs can be faster and more concentrated.

Boundary 2: HQLA also face discounts. Treasuries can trade at discounts under stress, and central-bank haircuts widen.

Boundary 3: deposit insurance changes speed. Insured deposits run more slowly; uninsured wholesale funding runs fast, and coverage above the limit still runs.

Mistake 1: an adequate LCR prevents runs. It only passes a 30-day stress test; concentration, collateral and confidence can still trigger a run.

Mistake 2: wholesale funding is more stable than deposits. It is short-dated and held by professional counterparties, who exit faster under stress.

Deeper note: the run timeline and the fire-sale spiral

A wholesale funding run is usually faster than a deposit run. Deposits are insured, branch-bound and withdrawal-limited; wholesale funding is institution-to-institution and can stop rolling within hours. First a downgrade or rumour, then counterparties demand larger haircuts or shorter terms, then repo fails to roll and commercial paper cannot be sold, then the bank is forced to sell assets.

LCR's 30-day assumptions can be optimistic in a real run. LCR assumes some deposits leave and some wholesale funding rolls; with concentrated counterparties and a high share of uninsured deposits, actual outflows can far exceed assumptions. LCR is a floor, not a stress forecast.

NSFR requires stable funding over one year and prevents over-reliance on short-term funding, but it cannot prevent assets from being sold at a discount under stress. A passing NSFR can still end in a fire sale if assets are illiquid.

The fire-sale spiral works like this: forced sales push prices down, collateral values fall, margin calls rise, more sales follow. 2008 structured investment vehicles and the March 2020 Treasury market both showed it. Destructive power depends on market depth, leverage and holder concentration.

The sequence of tools: private funding first (equity issuance, asset sales), then central-bank liquidity (discount window, repo), then resolution (receivership, bail-in). Reversing the order invites moral hazard; moving too slowly lets the run spread.

The 2023 regional-bank case shows that adequate capital with duration mismatch and concentrated uninsured deposits can still fail within days. The fix is not watching LCR alone but uninsured deposit share, bond losses, wholesale funding reliance and eligible collateral.

Monitoring indicators: wholesale funding share, weighted average maturity, counterparty concentration, uninsured deposit ratio, LCR/NSFR buffers, collateral haircuts, CDS and equity. Combine them; one alone misses the 'maturity mismatch plus concentration' mix.

Finally, wholesale funding is not 'bad': it lowers funding cost and deepens markets. The risk is too short a term, too concentrated counterparties and too illiquid assets. Regulation and bank management should target those three dimensions rather than banning wholesale funding.

Stress scenarios: from haircuts to receivership

Scenario one, haircuts rise: repo counterparties raise haircuts from 2% to 10%, so the bank must post more collateral or shrink repo. With insufficient eligible collateral it sells assets or turns to the central bank. LCR may still pass while actual liquidity has tightened.

Scenario two, wholesale funding stops rolling: commercial paper cannot be rolled and replacement funding is needed within days. If interbank markets are also tight, rates spike; backup credit lines may be drawn at the same time.

Scenario three, deposit outflows at the same time: uninsured deposits can move within hours in the social-media age; the bank faces wholesale and deposit outflows together, maximising fire-sale pressure. Silicon Valley Bank in 2023 was this scenario.

Policy sequence: central-bank liquidity first (discount window, repo), deposit insurance to stabilise deposits next, capital and resolution last. Talking about 'rescuing shareholders' first invites moral hazard and political resistance; delaying liquidity turns it into a systemic event.

Recovery plans should state in advance which assets can be sold, which liabilities extended, which businesses divested and when resolution starts. Decisions made under stress usually miss the window.

Key takeaways

  • A wholesale run unfolds as rating/rumour, wider haircuts, failed rollover and fire sales, within hours.
  • LCR's 30-day assumptions can be too optimistic, especially with high uninsured deposits and counterparty concentration.
  • NSFR covers one-year stable funding but cannot prevent asset discounts; passing it does not rule out fire sales.
  • The fire-sale spiral runs from sales to lower prices, lower collateral, margin calls and more sales; damage depends on market depth and leverage.
  • Policy order: private funding, central-bank liquidity, resolution; reversing it invites moral hazard or spreads the run.
  • Monitor wholesale share, maturity, concentration, uninsured deposits, LCR/NSFR buffers and collateral haircuts together.

Wholesale-funding run checklist

To judge a wholesale-funding run, do not stop at LCR and NSFR ratios. Split six questions:

  1. What are the liability maturities and collateral? Overnight repo, 30-day certificates and one-year bonds roll differently; haircuts and valuation frequency set run speed.
  2. How concentrated are counterparties? A few money funds, dealers or offshore institutions withdrawing can break refinancing; diversification matters more than the total.
  3. Are LCR outflow assumptions conservative? Stress scenarios may understate correlated withdrawals; check composition, haircuts and time to liquidate.
  4. Does NSFR hide a structural mismatch? A passing one-year stable-funding ratio does not mean overnight liabilities roll in stress.
  5. How fast is the fire-sale spiral? Price discount - margin call - forced sale - deeper discount can amplify losses within days.
  6. Are the central-bank and resolution sequence clear? Discount window, BTFP-type facilities, deposit insurance and resolution decide who absorbs losses first.

A funding run is a speed problem, not a capital-adequacy problem; a passing liquidity ratio can lose refinancing within hours.

What would change the conclusion

Wholesale-run conclusions depend on the quality of liquid assets:

  1. LCR buffers lose value in stress. If high-quality assets discount or become hard to sell, LCR overstates liquidity.
  2. Counterparties are insured deposits. If funding comes mainly from insured deposits, runs are slower than wholesale runs.
  3. Central-bank tools are broad enough. If the discount window and emergency facilities cover all eligible collateral, the fire-sale spiral is truncated.
