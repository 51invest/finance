Accrual Accounting

Accrual accounting recognizes revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash changes hands; it improves comparability across periods but relies on estimates, so profit and cash flow can diverge.

Definition

Accrual accounting recognizes revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, rather than waiting for cash to be received or paid. It contrasts with cash-basis accounting; financial statements prepared under US generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) or International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) are required to use the accrual basis.[1]

Intuition

A service is completed in December but cash arrives in January, so the economic result belongs to December. Wages are incurred in December but paid in January, so that cost also belongs to December. Accrual accounting places transactions in the period where their economic substance occurs, making profit comparable across periods. The price is a need for accruals, deferrals and estimates.

Formal expression

Revenue is recognized when goods or services are provided and the revenue is earned; expenses are recognized when incurred or matched with revenue.[1][2][3]

Accruals are items already earned or incurred but not yet settled in cash, such as accrued revenue and wages payable. Deferrals are items already settled in cash but not yet earned or incurred, such as unearned revenue and prepaid expenses. Adjusting entries reassign those receipts and payments to the period in which revenue is earned and expenses are incurred, so the books comply with the accrual basis.

Conditions and limits

  1. Reporting framework: this requirement binds only entities that adopt GAAP or IFRS - the page states verbatim "However, public or private companies using GAAP or IFRS must prepare their financial statements using the rules of accrual accounting." [1] and gives the basis verbatim as "Accrual-basis accounting prescribes that revenues and expenses must be recorded in the accounting period in which they were earned or incurred, no matter when cash receipts or payments occur." [1]. Companies outside that framework are not bound by the same sentence: firms that do not sell stock publicly are allowed verbatim to "Companies that do not sell stock publicly can use cash-basis instead of accrual-basis accounting for internal management purposes or because they are exempt from such requirements in agreements such as a bank loan." [1], and the cash method itself is described verbatim as "This method is most commonly used by small businesses that deal primarily in cash transactions." [1]. Tax reporting is not uniform either: verbatim "The Internal Revenue Service requires businesses to report using accrual-basis information when preparing tax returns.", and "companies with inventory must use accrual-based accounting for income tax purposes, though there are exceptions to the general rule" [1] - US tax reporting runs on accrual-basis information, inventory holders must use the accrual basis, and the general rule has exceptions.
  2. Matching: expenses should be recognized in the same period as the related revenue; costs that cannot be matched directly are recognized when incurred. The page writes verbatim "Using the accrual method, we match cash inflows and the outflows required to generate them. We call this the matching principle." [1] and attributes both recognition principles to the accrual method verbatim as "the revenue recognition principle and the expense recognition principle (also known as the matching principle)" [1].
  3. Estimates (this article's own list, not that page's claim): bad debts, returns, warranties, depreciation and impairment require estimates, and changes in estimates affect profit. The three retrieved pages (4.1, 4.2, 4.3) contain none of the word faces bad debt, uncollectible, warranty, depreciation, impairment, materiality or going concern once tags are stripped, so this list carries no citation marker.
  4. Materiality (this article's own): immaterial items may be simplified, but materiality cannot be used to hide a significant misstatement. The three retrieved pages (4.1, 4.2, 4.3) contain none of the word faces bad debt, uncollectible, warranty, depreciation, impairment, materiality or going concern once tags are stripped; the word "material" that does appear on the page belongs to the licence boilerplate and is not a materiality principle.
  5. Going concern (this article's own): accrual accounting assumes the business will continue operating, so assets are measured on a going-concern basis. The three retrieved pages (4.1, 4.2, 4.3) contain none of the word faces bad debt, uncollectible, warranty, depreciation, impairment, materiality or going concern once tags are stripped.
  6. Cash flow still matters: when profit is recognized before cash arrives, receivables and cash-flow risk rise.

Worked example

Teaching assumptions (not a real company; amounts in yuan): a service worth 10,000 is provided in December and collected in January; wages of 3,000 are incurred in December and paid in January.

Period Accrual revenue Accrual expense Net income Net cash flow
December 10,000 3,000 7,000 0
January 0 0 0 7,000
Total 10,000 3,000 7,000 7,000

In December the company recognizes revenue of 10,000 and wage expense of 3,000, for net income of 7,000; it also records a receivable of 10,000 and wages payable of 3,000. In January it collects the cash and pays the wages, reducing the receivable and payable without recognizing revenue or expense again. Total profit and total cash flow are the same over the two months, but their timing differs.

Common mistakes

Accrual accounting ignores cash. It still records cash receipts and payments, but also records accruals and deferrals so revenue and expense land in the right period.

Revenue is recognized when cash is received. A customer prepayment is a liability until the obligation is satisfied, not revenue.

Accrual profit is always more accurate. It depends on estimates and judgment and can be used to pull revenue forward or push expenses back.

Every small business must use accrual accounting. Tax rules and small-business reporting may allow cash basis; public financial statements generally require accrual.

Profit equals cash flow. Accruals make the two diverge in the short run, so both should be read together over time.

Boundary case: Accrual accounting puts 7,000 of profit in December and the cash in January

In December, revenue of 10,000 and wage expense of 3,000 produce net income of 7,000, but net cash flow is zero. In January, 10,000 is collected and 3,000 is paid, producing cash flow of 7,000 and profit of zero. If the customer pays only 8,000 and 2,000 becomes bad debt, December profit is still 7,000, but January records a 2,000 impairment and full-year profit falls to 5,000.

The conclusion reverses when collection is probable and the amount can be reliably estimated: December recognition is then appropriate. If consideration is variable or returns are large, both timing and amount must be adjusted. The boundary is that accrual accounting is not a licence to recognise revenue early; it requires matching, collectability and reviewable estimates. A gap between profit and cash flow is not automatically fraud, but it must be explained.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not company books.

The accrual-cash gap also depends on balance-sheet changes. Recognised revenue raises receivables, lifting profit before cash arrives; accrued expenses raise payables, lowering profit before cash leaves. Linking profit, working capital and cash shows whether the gap is normal timing or a signal of weak revenue quality or understated expenses.

Reading and review

Accrual accounting puts revenue and the costs incurred to earn it in the same period instead of following cash. Revenue recognition requires delivery and reliable measurement; matching pairs inventory cost, depreciation and warranty expense with revenue. Accruals therefore record timing differences: receivables, prepayments, payables and deferred revenue all change the mapping between profit and cash. Judge accrual quality by whether it reverses later and whether a real transaction supports it. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Under accrual accounting, when is revenue recognized?
  2. Calculation. In December a company earns 50,000 of revenue and collects 40,000; it incurs 30,000 of expenses and pays 25,000. What are accrual net income and net cash flow?
  3. Conditions. A company's credit sales are growing quickly, but it records no bad-debt allowance. What might be overstated, and what information should be added?
Suggested answers
  1. In the period when the goods or services are provided and the revenue is earned, not when cash is collected.
  2. Accrual net income = 50,000 - 30,000 = 20,000; net cash flow = 40,000 - 25,000 = 15,000.
  3. Revenue, net income and receivables may be overstated. Assess customer credit quality, aging and the bad-debt allowance, and use operating cash flow to judge collection quality.
