The Income Statement

The income statement summarizes revenue, expenses and profit over a period on an accrual basis; net income is not cash flow, and readers should separate gross profit, operating income and one-off items.

ModuleFIN-M11 · 三张报表
LevelFoundation
Read7 min · 11 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

The income statement summarizes a company's revenues, expenses, gains and losses over a period and arrives at net income or net loss. It answers whether the business was profitable during the period, not how much cash it held at the end.[1][2]

Intuition

A business first earns revenue by selling goods or services, then deducts the costs incurred to earn that revenue. Gross profit, operating income and net income are three different layers: gross profit deducts only product costs, operating income also deducts operating expenses, and net income also deducts interest and taxes. Each layer answers a different question.

Formal expression

The basic relationship is:

Revenue - Expenses = Net Income (or Net Loss)

Strictly, gains and losses also enter net income; the expression above is a shorthand that folds gains into revenue and losses into expenses, or shows them separately below.[2]

A multi-step presentation runs: revenue - cost of goods sold = gross profit; gross profit - operating expenses = operating income; operating income - interest expense = pre-tax income; pre-tax income - income tax = net income. The multi-step layout itself is also the article's own wording: the tag-stripped text of 2-1 and 2-3 contains no word forms gross profit, operating income, multi-step or cost of goods sold. Revenue and expenses are recognized on an accrual basis, and the page puts it verbatim: "In contrast, under accrual accounting the transactions are recorded when the transaction occurs, regardless of when the cash is received or paid."[2] Because the tag-stripped text of 2-1 and 2-3 returns no matching principle, unearned or performance obligation word forms, our phrase "in the period they are matched" is the article's wording rather than the page's.

Conditions and limits

  1. A period statement: the income statement covers a period, while the balance sheet is a point-in-time statement - the guide puts it verbatim, "Balance sheets show what a company owns and what it owes at a fixed point in time".[1]
  2. Accrual basis: the timing of cash does not drive recognition - verbatim, "In contrast, under accrual accounting the transactions are recorded when the transaction occurs, regardless of when the cash is received or paid."[2]; phrasing it as "recognized when earned and when incurred or matched" is the article's own wording (the tag-stripped text of 2-1 and 2-3 returns no matching principle, unearned or performance obligation word forms).
  3. Estimates and judgment: depreciation lives, bad-debt allowances, inventory costing and warranty provisions all change profit.
  4. Presentation choices: the page states verbatim "Both IFRS and US GAAP have the same elements as components of financial statements: assets, liabilities, equity, income, and expenses." and then "The definition of each of these elements is similar between IFRS and US GAAP, but there are some differences that can influence the value of the account or the placement of the account on the financial statements." [3]. This article therefore claims only that element definitions are similar while some differences affect an account's value and its placement, and that the reporting framework should be aligned before comparing; the earlier wording about differing minimum line items went beyond the page and has been removed.
  5. One-off items: asset disposals, restructuring and impairment can distort the current period; separate continuing operations from one-off effects.
  6. Net income is not cash flow or distributable cash; also examine the cash-flow statement and working-capital needs.

Calculation: 1,000,000 - 600,000 = 400,000; 400,000 - 150,000 = 250,000; 250,000 - 30,000 = 220,000; 220,000 - 55,000 = 165,000; 165,000 ÷ 100,000 = 1.65.

Common mistakes

Revenue is cash received. Credit sales are recognized when earned, even if cash arrives later.

Gross profit is net income. Gross profit still omits operating expenses, interest and taxes.

High net income means cash is available. Accrued revenue, inventory and capital spending can all drive profit and cash apart.

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) equals cash flow. It still ignores working capital, capital expenditure, interest and taxes.

Earnings per share is directly comparable across companies. Share structures and dilutive instruments differ, so adjustments are needed.

Knowledge-point review

balance sheet

Definition: A balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time, showing resources, financing structure and solvency.

Mechanism: Assets are ordered by liquidity and liabilities by maturity; current ratio, leverage and net assets describe short- and long-term financial position.

Example: For example, a firm holds cash, receivables, inventory and plant, financed by short-term debt, payables and equity.

Boundary: The balance sheet is a snapshot; book values reflect historical cost, impairment and off-sheet items and do not equal market value.

income statement

Definition: An income statement reports revenue, costs, expenses and profit over a period, showing operating performance.

Mechanism: Revenue minus costs and expenses gives operating profit, and interest and tax lead to net income; gross, operating and net margins capture different layers.

Example: For example, revenue of 10 million, costs of 6 million and expenses of 2.5 million leave about 1.5 million operating profit.

Boundary: Profit is not cash flow; accruals, one-off items and estimates affect quality, and revenue timing can pull income forward or push it back.

cash-flow statement

Definition: The cash-flow statement separates operating, investing and financing flows, explaining cash changes and testing profit quality.

Mechanism: Operating cash flow adjusts net income for non-cash items and working capital; investing covers capex and acquisitions, financing covers debt, equity and dividends.

Example: For example, profit grows while receivables surge, so operating cash flow may fall, signalling weaker collection.

Boundary: More cash flow is not always better; operating cash can come from stretching payables or asset sales, investing and financing outflows reflect strategy, and classification changes appearances.

financial statement articulation

Definition: Articulation means net income flows into equity, and opening cash plus net cash flow equals closing cash across the three statements.

Mechanism: Retained earnings, working capital, depreciation and financing link the statements; broken links reveal bookkeeping or model errors.

Example: For example, net income raises retained earnings, inventory absorbs operating cash, and capex raises investing outflow and fixed assets.

Boundary: Articulation is not simple addition; acquisitions, FX translation, policy changes and minority interests break naive ties, and models must handle them explicitly.

consolidation and notes

Definition: Consolidated statements present parent and subsidiaries as one economic entity, eliminating intra-group transactions and investments, with notes on scope, policies and risk.

Mechanism: Control determines consolidation; minority interests, goodwill and unrealised profit affect group figures, and notes add segments, related parties and contingencies.

Example: For example, a parent selling inventory to a subsidiary requires elimination of unrealised profit to avoid inflating group revenue and inventory.

Boundary: Consolidation is not simple addition; control boundaries, joint ventures, SPEs and cross-border standards leave off-sheet risk, and note readability matters.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Is the income statement a point-in-time statement or a period statement?
  2. Calculation. Revenue is 500,000 yuan, cost of goods sold 300,000 yuan, operating expenses 100,000 yuan, interest expense 20,000 yuan, and the tax rate 20%. Find operating income, pre-tax income and net income.
  3. Conditions. A company receives 1,000,000 yuan in cash in December for services to be delivered next year. Can it recognize revenue in December? Why?
Suggested answers
  1. A period statement.
  2. Operating income = 500,000 - 300,000 - 100,000 = 100,000; pre-tax income = 100,000 - 20,000 = 80,000; tax = 16,000; net income = 64,000.
  3. No. Under accrual accounting revenue is recognized when the transaction occurs, which the page states verbatim: "In contrast, under accrual accounting the transactions are recorded when the transaction occurs, regardless of when the cash is received or paid."[2] Recording the cash received up front as a liability and recognizing it later is this article's application of the newer revenue-recognition language - the tag-stripped text of 2-1 and 2-3 returns no matching principle, unearned or performance obligation word forms - so that terminology is not credited to these two pages. The cash inflow appears in the statement of cash flows, not in this period's income statement.
