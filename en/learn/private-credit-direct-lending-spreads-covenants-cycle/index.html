Private Credit and Direct Lending: Spreads, Covenants and the Cycle

Private credit is not a substitute for high-yield bonds but a chain from borrower to fund, bank facility and end investor. A spread must cover funding, fees, operations and expected loss before leverage turns it into an equity return.

Question and scope

Private credit is often summarised in one sentence: funds lend directly to firms that banks will not serve, and the yield is higher. That sentence omits three things: whose money, what covenant, and which part of the cycle. A private-credit fund is not a bank: it has no deposit insurance or central-bank discount window. It is not a high-yield bond market either: its loans are not quoted daily. It sits between the two, lending long-term floating-rate loans from long-term capital, monitoring borrowers through covenants and relationships, and maintaining liquidity through valuation and credit lines.

This article separates private credit into four layers: the intermediation chain, the spread, the covenant and the cycle. A 100-unit loan pool first shows the difference between a gross spread and an equity return; the covenant and valuation sections then explain why the same structure can amplify both return and risk. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not forecasts for any fund or market. The size and risk features of private credit are documented by the Federal Reserve [1][2], the global financial-stability view by the IMF [3], monetary and cross-border conditions by the BIS [4], and retail-product disclosure by the SEC [5].

The intermediation chain: four layers, not one loan

The first layer is the borrower. The typical private-credit borrower is a mid-sized firm: too large for a relationship loan from a bank, too small for public bond markets, or owned by a party that prefers to avoid public disclosure. It receives a floating-rate loan with a 5–7 year maturity and maintenance covenants.

The second layer is the private-credit fund. The fund raises long-term capital, lends to borrowers and earns spread, origination fees and management fees. Its assets are not priced daily; valuation relies on models, comparable transactions and borrower financials. This reduces short-term volatility and also reduces price discovery.

The third layer is bank facilities and warehouse funding. A fund may use a bank facility for working capital or leverage. The facility is not a deposit; its maturity is shorter and its draw conditions are tighter. There is a maturity mismatch: assets run 5–7 years, while the facility may be reviewed annually or quarterly.

The fourth layer is the end investor. Pensions, insurers, endowments, family offices and some retail money provide equity or debt. Pension liabilities are long and can tolerate illiquidity; retail liabilities are short and may force asset sales or redemption gates when investors withdraw.

Each layer converts short funding into long assets, or liquid funding into illiquid assets. The conversion is not inherently bad: a pension using long liabilities to support long loans is maturity matching; retail money using short liabilities to support long loans is the fragile part. When judging risk, start with the liability maturity on the funding side, not just the asset yield.

A spread is not a return

The “spread” on a private-credit loan is usually the contract rate minus a reference rate, such as SOFR + 300bp. But the fund's net return must deduct:

  • funding cost: the cost of fund borrowing or the facility;
  • origination and management fees: one-off and annual;
  • operating cost: due diligence, monitoring, legal, administrators;
  • expected credit loss: probability of default × loss given default;
  • undrawn commitments and cash drag: standby lines and uninvested capital.

So a “300bp gross spread” is not a “300bp net return”. If funding costs 6%, the loan yields 9%, fees and operations total 1% and expected loss is 1.4%, the net return on assets is only 1.8%. With five times asset/equity leverage, the equity return is about 9%; if expected loss doubles, the equity return approaches zero. Leverage magnifies net return, not gross spread.

Covenants: writing control into the contract

Private-credit covenants are thicker than public-bond covenants. Common terms include:

  • Leverage and coverage: net debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, fixed-charge coverage;
  • Restricted payments: dividends, acquisitions and additional debt need lender consent;
  • EBITDA definition: which add-backs, caps and whether “projected synergies” are allowed;
  • Collateral and guarantees: asset liens, receivables, equity pledges;
  • Information and access rights: monthly financials, budgets, management meetings;
  • Default and remedy: cross-default, acceleration, amendment and waiver.

The purpose of a covenant is not to guarantee no loss but to write information rights and bargaining rights in advance. When a borrower deteriorates, whether the lender can obtain data, restrict dividends and add collateral determines recovery. Covenants that are too loose discover loss late; covenants that are too tight push the borrower to other lenders or into default under stress. Covenant design is a trade-off, not “tighter is better”.

The covenant monitoring loop

A covenant is not finished at signing; it is a continuing loop. Terms are set at origination, financials are collected monthly, coverage is tested quarterly, and a breach leads to negotiation, amendment or exit. The speed of the loop determines how early a loss is found: if information rights allow only an annual audit, a borrower can run through cash for two quarters; if the lender can access systems at any time, the warning comes earlier but operating cost is higher. The loop must also state who looks, at what, and who decides after a trigger; otherwise the covenant is a static document.

A numerical example: a 100-unit loan pool

Assume a fund has 100 units of assets, 20 of equity and 80 of debt at a 6% cost. The asset yield is 9%, fees 0.5%, operating cost 0.5% and expected credit loss 1.4% (4% default probability × 35% loss given default).

Item Amount Note
Interest income 9.0 100 × 9%
Debt cost 4.8 80 × 6%
Fees 0.5 origination and management
Operating cost 0.5 diligence, monitoring, legal
Expected credit loss 1.4 4% × 35% × 100
Net income 1.8 9.0 − 4.8 − 0.5 − 0.5 − 1.4
Equity return 9.0% 1.8 / 20

Three scenarios:

  • Base: 4% default, 65% recovery, loss 1.4, net income 1.8, ROE 9.0%.
  • Downside: 8% default, 60% recovery, loss 3.2, net income 0, ROE 0%.
  • Upside: 2% default, 75% recovery, loss 0.5, net income 3.1, ROE 15.5%.

This table makes two points. First, most of the “high yield” in private credit compensates for illiquidity, covenant monitoring and credit risk; it is not a free premium. Second, leverage magnifies equity return non-linearly: when loss rises from 1.4 to 3.2, net income falls from 1.8 to 0 and ROE from 9% to 0%; if loss rises to 4.0, equity is eroded. Stress tests must test the loss distribution, not only the base spread.

The cycle: valuation, dry powder and redemptions

Private credit has three amplifiers:

  1. Valuation lag. Assets are not priced daily; valuation relies on models and financials. When market rates rise, fund NAV does not fall immediately, but new-loan spreads widen and the fair value of old loans falls. The lag is not misreporting, but it lets investors believe risk is unchanged at a turning point.
  2. Dry powder. Undrawn committed capital is a buffer in a credit crunch and a source of spread compression in a competitive market. With plenty of dry powder, funds have an incentive to loosen covenants to win deals; with little, a redemption forces asset sales or a halt in lending.
  3. Redemptions and lock-ups. Long-term funds have lock-ups; short-term retail products do not. Retail inflows expand the fund; redemptions make illiquid assets hard to sell, potentially triggering gates or discounts.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, private credit is not a bank substitute. Banks have deposit insurance, central-bank liquidity and regulatory capital; private-credit funds do not. Treating the two as the same intermediary understates run and liquidity risk.

Second, low volatility is not low risk. Model valuation smooths the NAV curve, but underlying cash flow, covenants and borrower operating risk remain. Measuring a long-dated illiquid asset by short-term volatility understates tail risk.

Third, covenants are not omnipotent. They can secure information and bargaining rights, but cannot turn a bad asset good; enforcement needs law, time and cost. A borrower may move assets or seek bankruptcy protection before a covenant trips.

Fourth, a wider spread is not automatically a buy signal. A wider spread may compensate for higher default probability and lower recovery; if losses rise faster than the spread, net income falls. Read the spread together with covenants, borrower cash flow and liability maturity.

One-page checklist

  • Draw the chain first: borrower, fund, bank facility, end investor; mark maturity and liquidity at each layer.
  • Deduct funding, fees, operations, expected loss and cash drag from the spread before looking at levered ROE.
  • Check covenants for leverage, coverage, restricted payments, EBITDA definition, information rights and remedies.
  • Stress-test at least base, downside and upside credit scenarios, not only the gross spread.
  • Ask how often assets are revalued, what the model assumes, and whether they can be sold in a redemption.
  • Label every teaching number as an assumption; never present it as fund performance or an official statistic.

Units check: spread, coupon and net return

Number Question it answers What it easily omits
Coupon What the borrower contractually pays each year Discount, fees and payment-in-kind
Spread How much compensation over a benchmark Default, recovery and restructuring
Net return What the loan pool actually earns after costs and losses Undrawn capital, valuation marks and liquidity

A spread is a price for risk, not the return an investor receives. It covers credit risk, illiquidity, covenant monitoring and locked capital, but only a cash-flow chain that includes coupon, discount, fees, default, recovery and exit valuation produces net return. Marketing “an 8% spread” treats the most favourable layer as the whole.

At review, ask which benchmark the spread uses and whether tenors match; how much coupon is payment-in-kind or deferred; whether the pool includes undrawn capital; whether default is gross or net of recovery; and whether valuation uses a transaction price, a model or the last financing round. Change any one of these and net return changes. Mixing spread, coupon and net return overstates return and understates liquidity risk at the same time.

Common misreadings

Misreading one: private credit yields more, so it is better than public debt. The higher yield compensates for illiquidity, monitoring and credit risk; after fees and losses, the net return may be close to public markets.

Misreading two: private credit has no daily quote, so the risk is low. Low volatility is a result of valuation, not the disappearance of risk; illiquid assets can trade at a discount under stress.

Misreading three: tighter covenants are safer. Too tight pushes borrowers away; too loose delays loss recognition. What matters is whether the covenant can be enforced and whether the lender has information rights.

Exercises

  1. Draw the intermediation chain of a private-credit fund and mark the maturity of assets and liabilities.
  2. Using the example, recompute net income and ROE if debt cost rises from 6% to 7% with everything else unchanged.
  3. If default probability rises from 4% to 6% and recovery falls from 65% to 60%, recompute expected loss and ROE.
  4. Explain why a “300bp gross spread” is not a “300bp net return”; list at least four deductions.
  5. Design an EBITDA definition and state the add-backs, caps and test convention.
  6. What does a restricted-payment covenant solve? Give an example of a borrower bypass.
  7. Why does valuation lag amplify the cycle? Use NAV, redemptions and new-loan spreads.
  8. How may a fund behave when dry powder is plentiful, and what happens to spreads and covenants?
  9. Compare banks and private-credit funds on liquidity support; give two key differences.
  10. Write a stress-test proposal for a private-credit fund, specifying scenario, loss and liquidity dimensions.
