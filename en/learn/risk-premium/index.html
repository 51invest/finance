Risk Premium

A risk premium is the expected return above the risk-free rate; it compensates non-diversifiable risk, not every kind of volatility.

ModuleFIN-M09 · 风险与金融定价原理
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

By the end of this article, you can separate a quoted spread, a risk premium, and a realized excess return, and use CAPM to calculate a required return instead of treating a high yield as a high premium.

Definition

A risk premium is the part of a risky asset’s expected return above the risk-free rate; it compensates for bearing risk[1][3]. The market risk premium is the expected return on the market portfolio minus the risk-free rate, and an asset’s risk premium is scaled to its systematic risk[3].

Intuition

Suppose that, in one currency, a one-year government bond yields 4.0% and a corporate bond of the same maturity yields 6.5%; this article takes the same-maturity government bond as the risk-free reference, while the textbook's usual proxy is the three-month US Treasury bill rate[1]. Part of the 2.5-point spread compensates default and liquidity risk[2], so only the remainder is closer to an expected premium. Equity risk premiums compensate market risk that cannot be diversified away, not firm-specific risk.

Formal expression

Asset risk premium:

RP_i = E[R_i] - R_f

Market risk premium:

MRP = E[R_m] - R_f

Capital asset pricing model (CAPM):

E[R_i] = R_f + β_i × MRP

Here E[R_i] is expected return, R_f is the risk-free rate, E[R_m] is the expected market return, and β_i = Cov(R_i, R_m) / Var(R_m) measures systematic risk: Cov and Var are covariance and variance, whose sample estimators and units are set out in the main article on covariance and correlation (RES-010), and the textbook defines beta as the slope of a regression on market returns, so this formula is the same slope written in different notation[3]. A quoted yield can be roughly split into the risk-free rate, credit, liquidity and term premiums[2]; this is an analytical framework, not an accounting identity.

Conditions and limits

  • The risk premium is expected, not realized; ex-post excess returns can be negative.
  • Match currency, horizon, tax, liquidity and default characteristics.
  • The risk-free rate is only low-default-risk; inflation, duration and reinvestment risk remain, so match its maturity to the cash flows.
  • Under the CAPM assumptions it is systematic risk that is compensated and diversifiable idiosyncratic risk that does not raise the required return[3]; this is a result of the model, not a claim about every market.
  • A quoted spread contains expected default losses and a liquidity compensation, and the textbook also lists the taxability of bond interest and the likelihood of a call among the factors behind yields[2] (a call feature is one kind of embedded option), so the spread usually exceeds the expected premium.
  • This article holds the market risk premium fixed only to work the example; in practice it varies with the economic regime and the estimation window, so it is not a constant. That scope note is this article’s own convention, not a sourced result.

Worked example

All numbers are hypothetical teaching assumptions; every rate is quoted in the same currency and on the same maturity basis, and MRP, required return and risk premium are annual percentages (differences in percentage points). Let R_f = 4.0% and E[R_m] = 9.0%, so MRP = 9.0% - 4.0% = 5.0%.

  • β = 0.8: E[R_i] = 4.0% + 0.8 × 5.0% = 8.0%; asset risk premium 4.0%.
  • β = 1.2: E[R_i] = 4.0% + 1.2 × 5.0% = 10.0%; asset risk premium 6.0%.

A same-maturity corporate bond yielding 6.5% over a 4.0% government bond has a 2.5-point spread. With 1.0 point of expected default loss, the remaining 1.5 points are closer to an expected excess return (before liquidity and taxes).

A higher beta raises the asset risk premium; this is a model relationship, not a guarantee about realized returns.

Common misconceptions

  • Risk premium equals historical excess return. No. It is an ex-ante expectation; history reflects luck and valuation changes.
  • The whole high-yield spread is a risk premium. No. It also contains expected default losses, liquidity, taxes and covenants.
  • Higher volatility always means a higher premium. Not necessarily. Systematic risk is what the model prices; diversifiable idiosyncratic risk earns no premium, and the definitions of diversification and idiosyncratic risk are in the main article on diversification and common risk (INV-017).
  • The risk-free rate is truly riskless. No. It usually means only very low default risk; inflation, duration and reinvestment risk remain.

Knowledge-point review

no-arbitrage

Definition: No-arbitrage means equivalent cash flows should not trade at different prices, since a zero-cost riskless profit would otherwise exist.

Mechanism: Arbitrageurs buy the cheap and sell the rich asset until prices converge; transaction costs, financing and short-sale limits can block full arbitrage.

Example: For example, if the same stock's cross-exchange gap exceeds costs, arbitrageurs buy low and sell high until it narrows.

Boundary: No-arbitrage is a pricing restriction, not a forecast; liquidity droughts, counterparty risk and constraints let deviations persist, and replication conditions matter.

law of one price

Definition: The law of one price says the same good should have one price in competitive markets without transport or trade barriers, or arbitrage removes differences.

Mechanism: Cross-market gaps reflect shipping, tariffs, exchange rates, quality and transaction costs; tradable and non-tradable goods converge differently.

Example: For example, the dollar price of identical gold in London and New York is usually close, and wider gaps attract arbitrage or hedging flows.

Boundary: The law does not hold for all goods; housing, services and regulated products cannot be shipped, and brands, taxes and local supply keep gaps.

risk premium

Definition: A risk premium is the extra expected return investors demand for holding a risky asset instead of a risk-free one.

Mechanism: It reflects risk size, correlation, liquidity, leverage and risk appetite; equity, credit and term premia have different sources.

Example: For example, stocks are expected to earn more than government bonds over time, partly compensating earnings uncertainty and drawdowns.

Boundary: A risk premium is not guaranteed return; expectations can fail, historical premia depend on samples and valuations, and low volatility can coexist with rich prices and weak future returns.

asset pricing and market efficiency

Definition: Asset pricing studies the link between risk and expected return, while market efficiency studies how quickly prices absorb information.

Mechanism: CAPM, multi-factor models and DCF offer different frameworks; weak, semi-strong and strong efficiency correspond to different information sets.

Example: For example, prices adjust quickly after an earnings release, suggesting public information is absorbed; persistent insider profits would contradict strong efficiency.

Boundary: Efficiency does not mean prices are always right; noise, arbitrage limits, behavioural biases and frictions create mispricing, and models can omit risk factors.

Exercises

  1. Concept. A government bond yields 4.0% and equity is expected to return 9.0%. What is the market risk premium?
  2. Calculation. R_f = 4.0%, MRP = 5.0%, β = 1.2. What are the required return and asset risk premium?
  3. Conditions. A corporate bond yields 6.5% and a same-maturity government bond 4.0%. Can the whole 2.5-point spread be called a risk premium?
Suggested answers
  1. 9.0%-4.0% = 5.0 percentage points.
  2. Required return 4.0% + 1.2 × 5.0% = 10.0%; risk premium 6.0%.
  3. No. It also contains expected default loss, liquidity, taxes and covenants; use the same maturity, currency and tax basis, and deduct expected losses before comparing.
