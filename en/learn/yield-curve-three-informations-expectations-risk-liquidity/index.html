Three Messages in the Yield Curve: Expectations, Risk and Liquidity

An inverted yield curve is often read as a recession signal, but the curve carries at least three messages: future rate expectations, term risk premium and liquidity premium. Separating them tells whether inversion reflects tight policy or risk repricing.

Definition and intuition

The yield curve plots yields across maturities. It carries at least three messages:

Expectations: long rates are the average of expected future short rates; if the market expects rate cuts, long rates can fall below short rates and the curve inverts.

Term premium: investors demand compensation for holding long bonds through repricing and inflation uncertainty; a higher premium steepens the curve.

Liquidity premium: long bonds differ in market depth, bid-ask spreads and collateral treatment; less liquidity means higher yield.

Mixing the three misreads 'expected cuts' as 'recession' or 'risk premium up' as 'policy easing'[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose the 2-year yield is 4.8%, the 10-year 4.2%, so the 10y−2y spread is −0.6 points and the curve is inverted.

Using the expectations hypothesis, if the expected average short rate over the next eight years is 3.8% and the term premium is 0.4%, the 10-year yield is about 3.8+0.4=4.2%; the 2-year 4.8% reflects high current policy and expected cuts. Inversion comes from 'high now, low later', not necessarily recession.

If the term premium rises from 0.4% to 1.2%, the 10-year yield rises to 5.0% even with unchanged rate expectations, and the curve steepens. Inversion ending may reflect higher risk premium, not better growth. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three signals

Recession signal: inversion persists for months alongside wider credit spreads, weaker PMI and higher jobless claims; the curve alone is insufficient.

Policy signal: the short end is anchored by policy; the long end falls on expected cuts. If inflation is high and the central bank is hawkish, the short end rises and inversion deepens.

Risk signal: long yields can rise from term or liquidity premium rather than growth expectations; the curve steepens but risk assets may not benefit.

Ask which end is moving: short-end moves are mostly policy; long-end moves can be expectations, risk or liquidity. The shape is the result; decomposing drivers is the analysis[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: term premium is unobservable. It is model-estimated; different models give different answers, and residuals are not facts.

Boundary 2: the term spread is not the only indicator. The 3-month/10-year spread, futures, credit spreads and bank lending surveys all add information.

Boundary 3: central-bank purchases compress the term premium. QE and QT change the curve; inversion may reflect the policy mix, not market expectations.

Mistake 1: inversion guarantees recession. It is a probability signal, not a guarantee; credit, employment and earnings matter.

Mistake 2: a steep curve means a strong economy. Steepness can come from a higher term premium, fiscal risk or inflation uncertainty, not growth.

Deeper note: split the curve into level, slope and curvature

Curve analysis should not fixate on the 10y-2y spread alone. A fuller approach splits the curve into three factors: level (parallel moves), slope (long-short spread) and curvature (the belly versus the wings). Level shifts usually reflect policy rates or inflation expectations; slope changes reflect term premia and the expected policy path; curvature changes often appear around policy turns or rising recession odds.

Term-premium estimation is the key. Academic and practitioner models such as ACM and KW decompose long yields into the average expected short rate and the term premium. If the 10-year yield rises 0.5 points but the model shows the premium up 0.7 and expected rates down 0.2, the move is mainly risk compensation, not growth optimism. Without the decomposition, 'risk repricing' is misread as 'overheating'.

Four curve shapes need separate readings: bear steepening (long end rises faster) usually means a higher term premium or inflation worry; bear flattening (short end rises faster) usually means central-bank hikes; bull steepening (short end falls faster) usually means rate-cut expectations; bull flattening (long end falls faster) usually means growth fear. The same word 'steeper' can mean opposite things.

Real versus nominal must be separated. If nominal yields rise while breakeven inflation falls, real rates rise, which is worse for growth and risk assets; if the rise is mostly inflation expectations, real rates may be unchanged. TIPS or inflation swaps are more reliable than the nominal curve alone.

Signals lag and are noisy. Inversions have led recessions by months to two years on average, but each cycle differs; timing on inversion alone can be early or late. Combine the curve with credit spreads, jobless claims, PMI and bank lending standards rather than using one spread as a switch.

Trading implications and signal limits

Curve trades typically express a view through duration-neutral portfolios: expect steepening, go long the short end and short the long end; expect flattening, the reverse. But the trade is relative value, not direction; if the curve steepens as expected while rates rise overall, the portfolio can still lose. Curve trades must manage duration, convexity and liquidity together.

The inversion-recession signal has preconditions. It needs a sustained inversion, rising real rates and wider credit spreads; if inversion comes from a compressed term premium, the signal is weaker. The US inverted in 2019 and recession came in 2020, but the 2022-2023 inversion persisted with strong growth, showing that a single indicator cannot be used mechanically.

Real rates explain growth better than the nominal curve. If the 10-year nominal rises 0.6 points with breakeven inflation up 0.4, the real rate rises only 0.2 and the growth drag is limited; if nominal rises 0.6 with unchanged inflation expectations, the real rise is 0.6 and the drag is stronger. Split nominal and real.

Liquidity premia amplify under stress. In a crisis, long-bond bid-ask spreads widen and collateral haircuts rise, pushing yields up; this is falling market depth, not better growth. Verify with volumes, spreads and repo haircuts rather than yields alone.

Signal limits: the curve sums market expectations, and expectations are wrong; policy, term premia and liquidity premia interfere; curve shapes across countries and maturities are not directly comparable. Treat the curve as a probability hint, not a certain forecast.

Further reading

Campbell and Shiller's term-structure work decomposes long yields into expected short rates and a term premium; the ACM model of Ang, Piazzesi and Wei provides an operational term-premium estimate. Local studies often use central-bank working papers and academic journals with different methods and data conventions; harmonise them before comparing.

Read with this article's three layers: expectations, term premium and liquidity premium. Any model explains only part; before calling the residual 'market sentiment', check the data and specification. A final caution: the term premium is an estimate, not an observable; when models disagree, report the range and assumptions rather than picking the number that fits the narrative. Backtest each model update instead of keeping only the version with the best hindsight fit, and state how much of the curve move remains unexplained.

What would change the conclusion

The three yield-curve signals depend on decomposition:

  1. Term premium is model-based. If the model is wrong, expectations and risk premium cannot be separated and the signal is overstated.
  2. Foreign demand dominates. If foreign central banks and institutions hold large shares, the curve reflects global term premium rather than domestic policy expectations.
  3. Yield-curve control. If the central bank pins a segment, the shape is administrative and not a market forecast.
  4. Inflation expectations de-anchor. If expectations stop being anchored, the meaning of steepening or inversion changes.

Practical implication: treat the curve as a bundle of information, not a forecasting machine: level for policy path, slope for cycle, curvature for tails; when they disagree, check liquidity premia and foreign demand first.

Data note: compare constant-maturity, same-liquidity buckets; on-the-run and off-the-run liquidity premia differ and distort the slope.

Scope note: use consistent maturity and liquidity buckets; curve segments of different maturities cannot be stitched into one signal.
