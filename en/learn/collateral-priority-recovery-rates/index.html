Collateral, Priority and Recovery Rates

How much a defaulted loan recovers depends less on the collateral written into the contract than on whether the security is enforceable, who ranks first, what disposal costs and who absorbs the shortfall.

Problem and scope

Credit analysis often treats "secured" as a synonym for "low risk." Reality is less tidy. A secured loan can fail at registration, valuation, enforcement or priority. An unsecured loan can recover more because the borrower's cash flow is strong and creditors are dispersed. Recovery is not a decorative contract term; it is the allocation of cash in a default state.

This article divides recovery into five layers: whether the security interest exists, what the collateral is worth, how high disposal costs are, who ranks first, and who absorbs the shortfall. A teaching example connects four common measures: collateral coverage, loan-to-value, loss given default and recovery rate. The final section covers the cases that most often break the arithmetic: double pledging, automatic stays, fraudulent transfers, cross-border insolvency and "rescuing the firm is worth more than liquidating it."

The five-layer structure of security

The first layer is the claim itself. A loan contract, bond indenture or trade receivable fixes principal, interest, fees and acceleration clauses. If the claim is invalid, security has nothing to attach to. The second layer is the security interest. Mortgage, pledge, lien, guarantee and credit enhancement have different legal effects: a mortgage typically targets specific assets, while a guarantee targets a third party's general estate. The third layer is registration and third-party effect. Without registration or possession, security may not defeat a good-faith purchaser or a later creditor. The fourth layer is enforcement and disposal. Judicial auction, out-of-court sale, portfolio sale and the speed of each determine how much book collateral becomes cash. The fifth layer is the priority waterfall. Secured claims, preferential claims, employee wages, taxes, insolvency expenses and secured restructuring debt rank in a legal order. That order determines how the same collateral recovers for different creditors.

If any layer breaks, "80% collateral coverage" can become "20% actual recovery." A secured loan therefore needs more than an appraisal: is the security enforceable, what does enforcement cost, and which other claims can jump the queue?

The priority waterfall

A typical liquidation waterfall runs: secured claims paid from collateral; insolvency expenses and employee claims in statutory order; taxes; unsecured preferential claims; ordinary unsecured claims; equity residual. Jurisdictions differ, and so do instruments within one jurisdiction: liquidation, reorganization and out-of-court settlement. An analyst should draw at least three waterfalls: liquidation, reorganization and negotiated disposal. Their recovery rates can differ widely.

A common error is to treat collateral value as recovery amount. The correct sequence is: disposal price minus disposal costs, taxes and priority expenses, giving distributable value; cap at the secured claim; only the remainder enters the unsecured pool. If collateral value is below the secured claim, the shortfall becomes an unsecured claim; the borrower does not automatically top it up.

Another error is to treat priority as full payment. Priority fixes queue position, not adequacy. Collateral worth 100, disposal cost 10 and priority expenses 5 give 85 distributable. If the secured claim is 120, the secured creditor receives 85 and the remaining 35 becomes an unsecured shortfall. Priority raises the probability of recovery; it does not eliminate insufficient value.

Recovery rate and loss given default

The recovery rate is the share of defaulted exposure eventually recovered; loss given default (LGD) is one minus recovery. Exposure, probability of default (PD) and LGD multiply into expected loss. If PD is 2% and LGD is 45%, expected loss is 0.9%. The credit spread on the loan must cover expected loss, capital charge and operating cost; otherwise larger volumes produce larger economic losses.

There are three recovery conventions: nominal recovery on contractual principal; economic recovery on present value at default, net of disposal time and discounting; ultimate recovery on actual cash flows after reorganization. The differences matter most in long restructurings. A 60% recovery promised three years from now is worth 47.6% today at an 8% discount rate. Comparing nominal recovery with current provisioning overstates the risk relief.

A numerical example

Take a 100 yuan loan with collateral appraised at 80, disposal cost 6, priority expenses 4, secured claim 60 and ordinary unsecured claim 40. Distributable value in liquidation is 80-6-4 = 70. The secured claim takes 60 and 10 enters the unsecured pool; the ordinary claim recovers 10/40 = 25%. Total recovery is 70/100 = 70%; the secured claim recovers 100%, the unsecured claim 25%.

If collateral falls to 50 while disposal cost stays 6 and priority expenses 4, distributable value is 40. The secured claim recovers 40/60 = 66.7%; the ordinary claim gets zero; total recovery is 40%. The same loan moves from 70% to 40% total recovery and from 30% to 60% LGD when collateral falls from 80 to 50. This is why collateral coverage and recovery rate are not the same number.

If the firm is reorganized rather than liquidated, the collateral is not sold and creditors receive 10 a year for five years under the plan at an 8% discount rate. The annuity present value is about 10×3.993 = 39.9, below the liquidation value of 70. Unless reorganization preserves firm value and raises total cash flow, "keeping the firm alive" need not recover more. The comparison must include time, discounting and execution risk.

A short workout case

Consider a 120 million exposure: 50 secured, 30 senior unsecured and 40 junior unsecured. Collateral is appraised at 70, disposal costs 7 and priority expenses 3, leaving 60 distributable. The secured claim takes 50 and 10 enters the unsecured pool of 70. Senior unsecured receives 10×30/70 = 4.29 and junior receives 10×40/70 = 5.71. Total recovery is 60/120 = 50%; secured recovery is 100%, senior unsecured 14.3% and junior unsecured 14.3% on a pro-rata basis. If the waterfall gives senior unsecured priority over junior, senior takes all 10 and junior gets zero. The same collateral value produces different recoveries depending on the inter-creditor agreement.

Now add time. Suppose the standstill lasts one month, disposal takes three months and cash arrives six months after default. At an 8% annual discount rate, the present value of 60 is 60/(1.08)^0.5 = 57.7. The nominal recovery is 50%, the economic recovery is 48.1%. If the workout takes two years, the present value falls to 51.1 and the economic recovery to 42.6%. This is why a 50% headline recovery can be much less in risk-adjusted terms.

The case also shows why "secured" is not a single category. The secured lender is paid first but is still exposed to collateral value and disposal cost. The senior unsecured lender may have a contractual priority that is worthless if the pool is empty. The junior lender bears the residual. A credit file that records only "secured/unsecured" loses the information that determines recovery.

Model and data risk

Recovery models often use a beta distribution fitted to historical recoveries. The fit can be good in-sample and fragile out-of-sample because default samples are small and clustered in recessions. A model trained on 2015–2019 may assign high recovery to collateral types that failed in 2008. The fix is not a more complex distribution; it is a scenario overlay: collateral price down 30%, disposal delayed 12 months, priority expenses up 50%, and a correlation between defaults and collateral values.

Data risk also comes from netting. If the exposure is measured gross of collateral and the recovery is measured net, the LGD can be double-counted. Define exposure, collateral, claim and recovery consistently, and reconcile to the legal agreement and the accounting ledger before modelling.

A third data risk is recovery timing. A 60% recovery that arrives in three years is not the same asset as a 60% recovery that arrives in three months: at an 8% discount rate, three months is worth about 58.8%, three years about 47.6%. The model should store both amount and timing, discount at the workout's funding cost, and report the sensitivity to a six-month delay. Without timing, a portfolio of "high recovery" loans can still run out of liquidity.

Finally, legal opinions are not data. A lawyer's letter saying "the security is valid" is a legal conclusion, not an observed recovery. It can support a scenario, but it cannot replace the collateral value, priority waterfall and disposal-cost inputs. Mixing legal comfort with quantitative recovery is one of the most common sources of overoptimistic LGD.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, security may be invalid. Missing registration, missing possession, lack of disposing power or a prior seizure can defeat priority. Cross-border deals add local registration and conflict-of-laws issues.

Second, automatic stays and reorganization can prevent immediate disposal. The stronger the insolvency protection, the longer the recovery and the lower its present value.

Third, fraudulent transfers and preferential payments can be clawed back. Selling assets cheaply to an affiliate, or paying one creditor ahead of others, may be reversed by the insolvency administrator. Recovery rises, but litigation cost also rises.

Fourth, cross-border insolvency has no single waterfall. Different national rules on security, priority and reorganization can produce different recoveries from the same assets in different courts.

Fifth, recovery samples are biased. Default samples contain only borrowers that defaulted and cannot represent the whole book. Recovery is overstated in booms and understated in crises. Stress testing with a historical average needs a crisis haircut.

Sixth, security is not free. Registration, appraisal, insurance, monitoring and disposal all cost money; over-reliance on collateral can squeeze borrower cash flow and raise default probability.

Additional boundary one: recovery is not one number. A nominal 60 delayed six months at 8% is worth about 57.7 today; a two-year disposal is worth less. Ratings and provisions need amount, time and uncertainty together, not collateral coverage alone.

Additional boundary two: collateral has a liquidation value and a going-concern value. With liquidation 80 versus book 100, overcollateralisation can be an accounting illusion; specialised equipment adds discounts and a longer disposal period. The more specific the asset, the fatter the recovery tail.

Additional boundary three: priority can be jumped. Employee arrears, taxes, insolvency expenses and an automatic stay can rank ahead of unsecured claims; cross-border cases also depend on local registration and recognition. One contract can produce two waterfalls in two countries.

Additional numerical counterexample: a 100 loan with collateral 80, disposal cost 6, priority expenses 4, secured claim 60 and unsecured claim 40 gives total recovery 70% and unsecured 25%. If collateral falls to 50, total recovery is 40%, secured 66.7% and unsecured 0. LGD jumps from 30% to 60%, so collateral relief is non-linear.

One-page checklist

  • Confirm the claim is valid, then whether security exists, is registered and defeats third parties.
  • Draw liquidation, reorganization and negotiated-disposal waterfalls and calculate distributable value for each.
  • State nominal, economic or ultimate recovery; discount long reorganizations.
  • LGD = 1 minus recovery; expected loss = PD × LGD; the credit spread must cover expected loss and capital cost.
  • Deduct disposal cost, taxes, priority expenses and waiting time from collateral value.
  • Check fraudulent transfers, preferential payments, automatic stays and cross-border rules.
  • Do not use one historical average recovery as a crisis scenario; stress at least a price fall and a recovery delay.

Common misreads

Misread one: a higher collateral ratio is safer. The ratio uses book value while recovery uses liquidation value; a 100 asset may sell for 60 in stress, and disposal cost, priority expenses and delay discount it further.

Misread two: recovery is simply one minus LGD. LGD is an economic measure including time, discounting and recovery cost; a 60% nominal recovery delayed two years at 8% may be worth half.

Misread three: registering priority once is permanent. Cross-border deals, moving collateral, lapsed registration and fraudulent transfers can reorder priority; every refinancing and restructuring should recheck registration and local counsel.

Misread four: specialised collateral is worth more. Specialised equipment may be valuable in normal operations but has few buyers, deep discounts and slow disposal in liquidation; recovery depends on the resale radius, not the accounting label.

Data conventions

Convention one: report nominal recovery, present-value recovery and LGD separately. Nominal is the contractual amount; present value discounts disposal time and uncertainty; LGD also subtracts disposal and priority costs. A 60 recovery six months out at 8% is about 57.7 today.

Convention two: state whether collateral is at liquidation or going-concern value. Book 100, liquidation 80 and LTV 80% looks covered, but disposal cost 6 and priority expenses 4 turn coverage into a shortfall; specialised equipment adds discounts and a longer disposal period.

Convention three: disclose the priority waterfall item by item. How much goes to secured claims, employee arrears, taxes, insolvency expenses and unsecured claims; how a stay affects order; which registration and recognition rules apply across borders. Without these, recovery cannot be recomputed.

Exercises

  1. A 100 loan has collateral 80, disposal cost 6, priority expenses 4, secured claim 60 and unsecured claim 40; compute total and unsecured recovery.
  2. Collateral falls to 50 with other terms unchanged; recompute secured and unsecured recovery.
  3. If the security interest is unregistered, how does the waterfall change? Give at least two consequences.
  4. Liquidation distributes 70; reorganization pays 10 a year for five years at 8%; compare present values.
  5. State the relation between LGD and recovery, and compute expected loss with PD 2% and LGD 45%.
  6. Why is a 60% nominal recovery not a 60% economic recovery? Give the time and discounting reasons.
  7. Explain how missing registration, an automatic stay and a fraudulent transfer each change recovery.
  8. Design a cross-border insolvency checklist with security, priority, stay and local legal opinion.
  9. With a six-month delay and 8% discount rate, what is the present value of a 60 recovery? Compare with immediate recovery.
  10. Collateral 100, disposal cost 10, priority expenses 5 and secured claim 120; how much does the secured creditor get and what is the shortfall?
  11. How can an unsecured creditor judge whether employee claims or taxes will jump ahead?
  12. Explain in one paragraph why 'secured is not low risk', with two counterexamples.
