Foundations of Digital Finance

Digital finance changes the technology layer of payments and settlement, but the liability, credit risk and legal rights of money still have to be identified item by item.

ModuleFIN-M16 · 数字金融基础
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

This article defines digital finance as the use of digital technology to provide payments, credit, investment, insurance and settlement. To distinguish liability, it uses three working definitions: a central bank digital currency (CBDC) is a digital form of central bank liability[4]; tokenised deposits represent commercial bank deposits on a distributed ledger; and a stablecoin is typically privately issued and claims to hold its price with reserve assets[3][4]. Their issuers, liabilities and settlement layers differ and cannot be conflated.

Intuition

When you scan a code to pay, what changes is how the payment instruction travels; which bank holds the money, who bears credit risk and how final settlement occurs are separate layers. Technology can speed the front end, but it does not automatically remove bank credit risk, settlement risk or the user's legal rights.

Formal expression

Payment innovation

Payment innovation shortens authorisation, clearing and settlement, lowers cost and expands coverage. Evaluation must separate front-end experience, back-end clearing, final settlement and dispute handling; failure at any layer feels like “the money did not arrive”.

E-money

E-money is usually the issuer's liability to the holder, redeemable at par; it depends on the issuer's credit and safeguarding arrangements and is not a central bank liability. Regulation focuses on client-money protection, redemption rights and AML.

Central bank digital currency

A CBDC is a direct central bank liability, possibly retail or wholesale. The BIS central-bank report stresses that CBDC design must not compromise monetary or financial stability and involves choices about operations, access, privacy and cross-border arrangements[1]. Retail CBDC raises privacy, offline payment, commercial-bank disintermediation and operating-architecture questions; wholesale CBDC is more about cross-border and settlement experiments. Design choices change where risk sits.

Tokenised deposits

Tokenised deposits move commercial bank deposits onto a programmable ledger; the liability remains a bank deposit. They may improve settlement efficiency and composability, but credit risk, deposit insurance and the regulatory perimeter generally still follow deposit treatment.

Fintech and infrastructure

Fintech embeds technology in financial processes; success often depends on infrastructure: identity, clearing, data standards, cybersecurity and legal certainty. Technology is one layer. Crypto assets may also raise securities-law, custody, disclosure and fraud issues; regulatory treatment depends on structure and facts[2].

Conditions and limits

  1. Discussing digital money requires issuer, liability, redemption rights and settlement finality; talking only about the “coin” confuses risks.
  2. Payment innovation's efficiency depends on back-end clearing and settlement; a fast front end is not fast finality.
  3. CBDC design involves privacy, programmability and the commercial-bank role; designs are not interchangeable.
  4. Stablecoin stability depends on reserve quality, audit and redemption; the FSB principle calls for same activity, same risk, same regulation, and stablecoin arrangements also need reserve and redemption requirements[3]. “Stable” is a promise, not a guarantee of value; holders still bear issuer, reserve and redemption risks.

Worked example

Suppose a 1,000 yuan merchant payment follows this flow; the numbers are teaching assumptions.

Step Participant What happens
1 Payer Initiates a 1,000 yuan payment in a wallet or bank app
2 Payment provider Verifies identity and debits the payer
3 Receiving bank Credits the merchant account, possibly instant or next day
4 Clearing system Nets interbank positions
5 Settlement Final transfer of funds and reconciliation

The front end may show “paid” at step 2 while interbank steps 4–5 are incomplete. If the provider fails or clearing breaks, the merchant may face reversal or delay. Digital-finance evaluation must separate user experience from final settlement.

Common mistakes

CBDC is cryptocurrency. A CBDC is a central bank liability[4] backed by the state; most crypto assets lack the same issuer and redemption promise.

A stablecoin is a bank deposit. A stablecoin is usually a private liability relying on reserves and redemption; it does not automatically receive deposit insurance or central bank support.

Knowledge-point review

payment innovation

Definition: Payment innovation changes initiation, clearing and settlement through new technology, channels and business models, such as mobile, fast payments and open banking.

Mechanism: It cuts friction and expands access but brings fraud, privacy, operational risk and arbitrage; interoperability and finality matter.

Example: For example, QR payments let small merchants collect cheaply, while account theft and refund disputes require rules.

Boundary: Innovation is not automatically inclusive; digital divides, fees and network effects exclude vulnerable users, and instant settlement can aid fraud.

e-money

Definition: E-money is a stored monetary claim on an issuer, usable for payments and usually separated from bank deposits.

Mechanism: Issuers back liabilities with safeguarding assets or bank deposits and need licences, segregation and redemption rights; technology and merchant networks drive use.

Example: For example, a prepaid card or wallet balance buys goods, and the issuer must hold matching safe assets and allow redemption.

Boundary: E-money is not a bank deposit or central-bank money; issuer credit, safeguarding quality and insolvency separation determine safety, and anonymity and cross-border use raise compliance risk.

central bank digital currency

Definition: A CBDC is a digital liability issued directly by the central bank to the public or institutions, distinct from commercial-bank deposits.

Mechanism: Retail CBDC can improve payment efficiency and inclusion, wholesale CBDC serves interbank settlement, and design must balance privacy, offline use, remuneration and stability.

Example: For example, a retail CBDC wallet is recorded at the central bank and transfers bypass commercial banks, which may trigger deposit shifts.

Boundary: CBDC is not cryptocurrency; central issuance, legal status and programmability are policy choices, and interest, limits and privacy shape adoption and bank intermediation.

tokenised deposits

Definition: Tokenised deposits record commercial-bank deposits on a distributed ledger while remaining bank liabilities.

Mechanism: They keep deposit credit and regulatory attributes and enable programmable payments and atomic settlement, but need bank books, identity and legal finality.

Example: For example, a firm tokenises deposits to pay automatically on supply-chain conditions, while the bank remains responsible for redemption and compliance.

Boundary: Tokenisation is not issuing new money; outages, double spending, privacy and cross-chain gaps create risk, and legal certainty matters more than the label.

fintech and infrastructure

Definition: Fintech uses data, algorithms, cloud and distributed ledgers to reshape payments, credit, investment and insurance; infrastructure is the systems and rules beneath.

Mechanism: Technology cuts costs and barriers but concentrates data, algorithm and cloud risk; licensing, cybersecurity, interoperability and consumer protection determine sustainability.

Example: For example, a lending platform scores small firms with alternative data, expanding credit but requiring bias and data-source review.

Boundary: Fintech is not a regulatory vacuum; technology neutrality, systemic importance and outsourcing still need oversight, and innovation must be balanced against stability.
