Cost of Capital

The cost of capital is the return investors require to supply funds and the hurdle rate for projects; the weighted average cost of capital combines after-tax debt and equity costs at market-value weights and applies only to comparable-risk projects.

Definition

The cost of capital is the return investors require to supply debt or equity financing, and the minimum return a company must expect on a new project financed with those funds. It is both the investor's required return and the company's hurdle rate.

Intuition

Creditors demand interest; shareholders demand dividends or price appreciation. These demands make up the price of financing. A company blends its sources in proportion to arrive at an overall minimum return. If a project earns less than that blended rate, shareholder value falls after financing.

Formal expression

For a company without preferred stock, the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is:

WACC = D% × rd × (1 - T) + E% × re

Here D% and E% are the shares of debt and equity in the company's market value, rd is the pre-tax cost of debt, T is the tax rate, and re is the cost of equity. The after-tax cost of debt is rd(1-T). Weights should use market values, not book values.[1]

Conditions and limits

  1. Use marginal costs: the current yield to maturity on new debt, not the coupon on old debt.[2]
  2. Use market-value weights; book weights can diverge from the actual financing structure.[1]
  3. The project's risk should be close to the company's overall risk; divisions with different risk need risk-adjusted rates.
  4. Assume the tax rate, capital structure and debt cost are broadly stable over the project's life; recompute the weights and WACC when they are not.
  5. The value of the interest tax shield depends on the tax rate and the company's ability to use the deduction.
  6. WACC is an estimate built on assumptions, not an observable market price.[3]

Worked example

A hypothetical company (not a real business) has debt with a market value of 400 million yuan and equity with a market value of 600 million yuan, for total capital of 1 billion yuan. Its pre-tax cost of debt is 6%, the tax rate is 25%, and the cost of equity is 9%.

Item Value (100 million yuan)
Market value of debt 4
Market value of equity 6
Total capital 10
Debt weight 40%
Equity weight 60%
Pre-tax cost of debt 6%
After-tax cost of debt 4.5%
Cost of equity 9%
WACC 7.2%

WACC = 40% × 4.5% + 60% × 9% = 1.8% + 5.4% = 7.2%. The 9% cost of equity can be derived from the capital asset pricing model (CAPM): risk-free rate 3% + beta 1.2 × market risk premium 5% = 9%.[2][3] The 7.2% rate applies only to projects with risk similar to the existing business and the same financing mix.

Common mistakes

The cost of capital is a cost already paid. No. It is an opportunity cost: the return investors require.

The cost of debt is the coupon rate. An old coupon may be stale; use the current refinancing rate or yield to maturity.[2]

A lower WACC is always better. More leverage can lower WACC while raising financial risk.

One WACC fits every project. Riskier projects need higher discount rates.

Knowledge-point review

capital budgeting

Definition: Capital budgeting screens long-term projects and allocates capital, using NPV, IRR and payback among other tools.

Mechanism: Projects are judged on incremental cash flow, risk and financing capacity; sunk costs are excluded, while opportunity costs and salvage values are included.

Example: For example, a firm compares a new line with an upgrade by discounting incremental cash flows and choosing the higher NPV within risk limits.

Boundary: Capital budgeting is not precise forecasting; long horizons, terminal values and discount rates are uncertain, and bias and budget games distort projects.

sources of finance

Definition: Corporate funding includes retained earnings, bank loans, bonds, equity, leases and supply-chain finance, with different maturity and cost.

Mechanism: Internal funds are cheaper but limited by profit; external funds carry interest, dilution and control constraints, and pecking order reflects information asymmetry.

Example: For example, a growing firm retains earnings and borrows long-term to avoid early equity dilution, while stable cash flow supports more debt.

Boundary: More funding is not always better; excess debt raises distress risk, equity dilutes control, and market windows and covenants limit choices.

cost of capital

Definition: Cost of capital is the minimum return investors require; equity cost reflects equity risk and debt cost reflects after-tax interest and tax shields.

Mechanism: WACC weights debt and equity by market value; a project riskier than the firm average should use a higher discount rate.

Example: For example, a firm with an 8 percent WACC uses 10 percent for a riskier expansion.

Boundary: Cost of capital is not historical accounting cost; beta, market risk premium and debt cost are disputed, and low rates and leverage can temporarily lower WACC while raising risk.

capital structure

Definition: Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity, affecting tax shields, distress risk, control and financial flexibility.

Mechanism: Trade-off theory weighs tax benefits against financial distress; pecking order prefers internal funds, and market timing affects issuance.

Example: For example, a mature utility can carry more debt for tax shields, while a startup relies more on equity and retained earnings.

Boundary: There is no single optimum; industry, asset collateral, cash-flow volatility and institutions matter, and over-optimisation can ignore agency and crisis risk.

agency and financing constraints

Definition: Agency arises when managers, shareholders and creditors have conflicting goals, requiring monitoring and incentives; financing constraints block investment due to information, collateral or cash shortfalls.

Mechanism: Debt covenants, equity pay, boards and disclosure mitigate agency; cash buffers, credit lines and relationship finance ease constraints.

Example: For example, a high-growth firm lacking collateral is denied a bank loan and must skip projects or accept costly equity.

Boundary: Agency and constraints are distinct; excessive monitoring can stifle innovation, excess cash can invite waste, and subsidies can weaken market discipline.

Data and institutional check: cost of capital

Before using "cost of capital" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns weighted average cost of capital.

Third, the institutional boundary. capital structure is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: cost of capital

Learning "cost of capital" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "weighted average cost of capital": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "capital structure" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of cost of capital still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind weighted average cost of capital reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "cost of capital" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "weighted average cost of capital" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. A company's old bonds carry a 4% coupon, but new bonds of the same maturity would pay 7%. Which debt cost belongs in WACC?
  2. Calculation. Debt has a market value of 300 million yuan and equity 700 million yuan. The pre-tax cost of debt is 5%, the tax rate is 20%, and the cost of equity is 11%. Calculate WACC.
  3. Conditions. A company uses one WACC for a low-risk utility division and a high-risk new venture. What could go wrong?
Suggested answers
  1. Use the 7% pre-tax cost. The cost of capital is the current refinancing rate, not the historical coupon.
  2. After-tax cost of debt = 5% × (1-20%) = 4%; WACC = 30% × 4% + 70% × 11% = 1.2% + 7.7% = 8.9%.
  3. The divisions have different risks. A single WACC overstates the hurdle for the safe division and understates it for the risky one, so the company may reject sound projects or accept too much risk. Use risk-adjusted rates.
