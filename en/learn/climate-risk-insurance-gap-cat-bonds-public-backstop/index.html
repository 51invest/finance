Climate Risk and the Insurance Gap: Cat Bonds, Reinsurance and Public Backstops

Climate losses are growing, but insurance and reinsurance cover only part of them. Cat bonds transfer tail risk to capital markets, and public backstops take the last layer, each with basis and incentive costs.

The question and its scope

Climate risk is not simply "more extreme weather". It is a fatter loss distribution: the probability of a large event rises in a region, and the tail of a single event gets longer. Insurance can carry part of the loss, reinsurance another part, capital markets another part through catastrophe bonds, and households, firms and governments carry the rest. The part that is not insured is the protection gap.

This article discusses four layers: how to measure the gap, how insurance and reinsurance are layered, how cat bonds are priced and triggered, and when a public backstop is justified. The numbers are teaching examples, not a real transaction.

The protection gap: more than "no insurance"

There are at least three ledgers. The economic gap is total loss minus claims paid. The insurance gap is the part of insurable loss that was not covered. The fiscal gap is what government ultimately pays without having pre-funded it. The numerators and denominators differ; one "gap ratio" cannot summarise them.

Four forces create the gap. First, risk may be uninsurable or hard to price: climate change makes historical loss data a poor guide to the future. Second, capacity is limited: reinsurance capital is consumed after a catastrophe and prices rise. Third, affordability: low-income households may know the risk but cannot buy full cover. Fourth, incentives: post-disaster government aid can reduce the willingness to insure ex ante, creating a free option.

A larger gap is not always worse, and a smaller gap is not always better. A large gap means recovery depends on temporary fiscal aid and household savings; a small gap may mean heavy subsidies or mandates that suppress the risk signal. The goal is to transmit the price of risk while protecting the most vulnerable, not to reach "zero gap".

Layering risk: from deductibles to public backstops

A climate loss is usually handled in layers. The bottom is the policyholder's deductible and retention; the next layer is the primary insurer's policy payout; the next is reinsurance; the next is cat bonds or other capital-market tools; the top is government or international aid.

The economics is that different parties carry losses of different probability. A deductible makes the policyholder carry frequent small losses and avoids the administrative cost of tiny claims. Reinsurance carries low-frequency, large losses and protects the primary insurer's solvency. Cat bonds sell tail risk to institutional investors. A public backstop carries the extreme layer that the market will not insure but society cannot ignore.

Each layer has a cost. A high deductible can push households into a cash-flow crisis after a small event. Expensive reinsurance makes primary insurers withdraw or raise premiums. Cat bonds carry basis risk. A public backstop can crowd out private insurance. Layering does not remove risk; it allocates risk to those with the best capacity, information and incentive to manage it.

Cat bonds: selling tail risk to capital markets

A catastrophe bond is an insurance-linked security. Investors pay principal, which the issuer holds in a collateral account. If the specified catastrophe does not trigger, investors receive coupon payments and their principal back; if it triggers, part or all of the principal is written down to pay claims.

The key parameters are the trigger, the attachment point, the exhaustion point and the expected loss. Triggers can be industry loss, an index, a parameter (magnitude, wind speed, rainfall) or indemnity. Parameter triggers settle quickly and transparently, but carry basis risk: the parameter does not map exactly to the actual loss.

Suppose a $100 million cat bond with a three-year term, a coupon of SOFR plus 4%, and an expected annual loss of 2.5%, or $2.5 million per year. The investor's required return is roughly the risk-free rate plus a risk premium and a liquidity premium minus expected loss. If expected loss is underestimated, the coupon looks high while the true risk is larger; if the trigger is set too high, the bond may never pay and the layer is empty.

Cat bonds expand capacity by connecting insurance risk to capital markets. They also bring basis risk, model risk, liquidity risk and issuance cost. They are not "cheaper insurance"; they are another way to carry tail risk.

Reinsurance and the pricing cycle

Reinsurance is insurance for insurers. A reinsurer takes losses above a retention in exchange for a premium. Reinsurance prices cycle: capital falls after a catastrophe and prices rise; when capital is replenished, prices fall. The cycle passes through to primary premiums and underwriting terms.

Reinsurance pricing has three components: expected loss, cost of capital and expenses. Expected loss is the long-run average claim; the cost of capital is the return required on the capital held against tail risk; expenses cover underwriting, administration and distribution. If expected loss is 100, capital cost 30 and expenses 15, the gross premium is at least 145; a 12% return on capital raises the capital charge.

Reinsurance is not unlimited. One large event can consume capital at several reinsurers at once, reducing subsequent capacity. Sidecars, cat funds and collateralised reinsurance exist to replenish capacity when capital is tight. Their common feature is that capital arrives quickly and leaves quickly: pricing is high after an event and low before one.

Public backstops: who pays the last bill

Public backstops take several forms: government disaster funds, sovereign insurance, parametric insurance, post-disaster aid and tax relief. They aim to prevent the fiscal budget from being overwhelmed after a disaster, but their incentive effects differ.

An ex ante fund requires annual contributions. It is prepared before the event and does not compete with the budget afterwards, but a large fund sits idle and is politically easy to divert. Ex ante insurance pays a premium and transfers risk to the market; prices are transparent, but premiums can be high and coverage limited. Post-disaster aid is flexible but weakens the incentive to insure and can widen the deficit.

A public backstop must answer three questions: who is eligible, what triggers payment, and where the money comes from. A backstop without clear rules becomes a political bargain after the event; rules that are too strict may exclude the people who need help most. Parametric insurance can pay quickly, but basis risk can send money to those whose parameter triggered without much loss, or miss those with large losses but no trigger.

Climate risk: why the gap may widen

Climate change widens the gap through three channels. First, frequency and severity: the tail of heat, flood, wildfire and storm losses rises, and historical pricing models fail. Second, insurance retreat: in some high-risk areas, insurers decline renewal, narrow coverage or raise prices sharply, creating "insurance deserts". Third, affordability: premiums rise faster than incomes, low-income households exit, and the gap moves from "uninsured" to "unaffordable".

Adaptation can lower physical risk, but adaptation costs money and has distributional effects. Sea walls, drainage, firebreaks and building codes reduce losses, but often benefit property owners; tenants and low-income communities may be excluded. Insurance can embed adaptation incentives: discounts for flood-proofed homes, higher premiums or restricted cover for new high-risk construction.

Attribution is another difficulty. A single event cannot be attributed simply to climate change, but the change in the loss distribution can be estimated statistically. Insurance pricing needs future probabilities, not a statement about whether this event was caused by climate change. The two questions must not be confused.

A numerical example: one reinsurance layer and one cat bond

Suppose a region has a 1% annual probability of a large catastrophe and a 20 billion economic loss. The primary insurer retains 2 billion; reinsurance covers 2 to 8 billion; a cat bond covers 8 to 12 billion; the public backstop covers above 12 billion.

Expected loss in the reinsurance layer is 0.01×(8−2)=0.060.01\times(8-2)=0.060.01×(8−2)=0.06 billion; in the bond layer, 0.01×(12−8)=0.040.01\times(12-8)=0.040.01×(12−8)=0.04 billion. If the reinsurance loading is 1.5 times expected loss, the premium is about 0.06×1.5=0.090.06\times1.5=0.090.06×1.5=0.09 billion. If the bond pays a risk-free rate plus 4% on 10 billion, the annual coupon is about 0.4 billion, while expected write-down is only 0.04 billion. The investor carries a small probability of full loss, not a stable annual loss.

The key is not the premium level but whether the layer actually pays after the event. If the bond trigger is set at 15 billion while reinsurance stops at 8 billion, the 8–15 billion range is unprotected. If the trigger is parametric and the actual loss is 13 billion but the parameter is not met, the bond may not pay. Every gap in the layer diagram is a hole that households, firms or government must fill after the event.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, insurance is not mitigation. It transfers loss from after to before the event but does not necessarily reduce total loss; mitigation needs building codes, land use and early warning. Second, cat bonds are not a cure-all. They expand capacity, but basis risk can misalign payouts and needs. Third, public backstops are not free. Government bears the last loss, ultimately paid by taxpayers or future budgets. Fourth, attribution is not pricing. A single-event attribution study does not become a premium; pricing needs a probability distribution. Fifth, cross-country comparison needs care. Insurance penetration, government roles, building codes and hazard types differ, so gap ratios are not directly comparable.

Definitions: five fields that must not be mixed

Field Meaning Common error
Economic loss Total loss after the event Confusing it with insurance payout
Insurance gap Uninsured part of insurable loss Treating it as total loss
Reinsurance layer Range carried by the reinsurer Ignoring attachment and exhaustion
Basis risk Gap between parametric payout and actual loss Treating it as credit risk
Public backstop Last layer carried by government Treating it as free money

Common misreadings

Misreading one: insurance means no gap. Coverage limits, deductibles, caps and insurer solvency all leave gaps.

Misreading two: a high cat-bond coupon means safety. A high coupon may reflect underestimated tail risk; read the trigger structure and model assumptions.

Misreading three: a government guarantee stabilises the market by itself. Unfunded ex post aid weakens ex ante insurance and competes with other budget priorities.

Misreading four: adaptation removes risk. Adaptation lowers probability and loss but cannot eliminate tail risk; insurance and backstops remain necessary.

Misreading five: parametric insurance is always better. Parametric triggers settle quickly but basis risk can leave the actual victims underpaid.
