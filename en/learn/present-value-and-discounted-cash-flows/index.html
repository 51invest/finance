Present Value and Discounted Cash Flows

Present value converts future cash flows to today at a discount rate, and discounted cash flow sums those present values; the rate, timing and cash-flow assumptions jointly determine the result.

ModuleFIN-M08 · 时间价值
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Present value (PV) is the value today of a future cash flow at an appropriate discount rate. Discounted cash flows (DCF) discounts each flow and sums them; net present value (NPV) is that sum minus the initial investment. The discount rate prices time and risk and must be chosen.

Intuition

105 in one year is worth 100 today at 5% (currency assumed; state it when using real figures), because 100 invested for one year becomes 105. A project costing 1,000 today and returning 300, 400 and 500 over three years must discount all three flows first.

Formal expression

Single-period and multi-period present value:

PV = CF_t / (1 + r)^t
PV = Σ_{t=1..n} CF_t / (1 + r)^t

Net present value:

NPV = Σ_{t=1..n} CF_t / (1 + r)^t - CF_0 (the initial investment CF_0 occurs at t = 0)

Ordinary annuity:

PV = C × [1 - (1 + r)^(-n)] / r

Here CF_t is the cash flow at t in currency (inflows positive, outflows negative), r is the discount rate per period as a decimal, n is periods and C is a level cash flow. CF_0 is the initial investment entered as a positive cost, which is why the formula subtracts it; if the sign convention is applied to every flow, the t = 0 entry is -CF_0 and the two writings give the same result. Match nominal flows with nominal rates and real flows with real rates; align periods and timing. All amounts here are teaching assumptions; state the currency and units when applying this to real projects. This article writes NPV as the sum of present values minus CF_0 (CF_0 carried as a positive cost), keeping the t = 0 negative-sign form only as an equivalent note; that convention is this article's editorial ruling dated 3 November 2026, and whether it becomes a site-wide convention is to be settled by an article-by-article check before publication.

Present value is the reverse of future value: 127.63 received five years from now at 5% is worth 100 today, and unless the rate is negative a present value is smaller than its future value (OpenStax) [1].

Conditions and limits

  1. Cash flows and rate must match. Nominal or real, pre-tax or after-tax, currency and period must align.
  2. The formulas carry one r, so the rate is assumed flat across periods. This is the same basic assumption as in the time value of money article (FIN-009); when a term structure or a changing rate applies, each period needs its own rate, which the examples here do not cover.
  3. Timing must be explicit. End-of-period and beginning-of-period flows differ by one period; an annuity due equals an ordinary annuity times (1 + r).
  4. The rate reflects risk and opportunity cost. A risk-free rate on risky flows overstates present value.
  5. Negative rates can make PV exceed FV. The formula works, but the intuition changes.
  6. DCF is sensitive to long-horizon assumptions. Terminal value, growth and capital expenditure can dominate.
  7. With uncertain cash flows, a single-scenario NPV is conditional. Use scenarios or sensitivity analysis.

An ordinary annuity can be discounted in one step, PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)^(−n)] / r, with an annuity due multiplied by (1 + r); uneven cash flows cannot use the annuity formula and are discounted period by period, as in the textbook case of five uneven withdrawals at 6% [2][3].

Worked example

All figures are hypothetical teaching amounts; state the currency when applying them to real products.

Single sum. Discounting reverses compounding: 1,157.63 received in three years at r = 5% gives PV = 1,157.63 / 1.05³ ≈ 1,000 (1.05³ = 1.157625; the rounded future value reverses to 1,000.0043, hence the approximation sign) [1]. The growth path of the same amount is the worked example in the time value of money article (FIN-009) and is not repeated here — only the discounting conclusion is used.

Unequal flows. CF_1 = 300, CF_2 = 400, CF_3 = 500, r = 5%:

PV = 300/1.05 + 400/1.05² + 500/1.05³ = 285.71 + 362.81 + 431.92 = 1,080.44.

With an initial investment CF_0 = 1,000 (a positive cost), NPV = 1,080.44 - 1,000 = 80.44, positive. The 1,080.44 is the sum of the present values after each is rounded to fen; summing the unrounded values gives 1,080.448, which rounds to 1,080.45, so the 0.01 difference comes from per-item rounding.

Sensitivity. At 4%, PV = 1,102.78 and NPV = 102.78; at 6%, PV = 1,058.83 and NPV = 58.83. A higher rate lowers PV.

Annuity. 100 at the end of each year for three years at 5% has a present value of about 272.32; the period-by-period check belongs to the time value of money article (FIN-009) and is not repeated, so only the ordinary-versus-due difference (exercise 3) is drawn from it here [2].

Common misconceptions

  • A higher discount rate raises present value. No: the denominator grows, so PV falls.
  • Present value is book value or market price. No: it is calculated from stated cash flows and a rate.
  • One rate fits every cash flow. No: risk, timing, currency and taxes may require different rates.
  • Positive NPV guarantees a profit. No: it meets the required return only under the assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept. All else equal, a higher discount rate makes present value: A. rise; B. fall; C. unchanged; D. indeterminate.
  2. Calculation. What is the PV of 200 in year 1 and 300 in year 2 at 5%?
  3. Conditions. A three-year annuity pays 100 at the beginning of each year at 5%. Is its present value higher or lower than an ordinary annuity, and why?
Suggested answers
  1. B. A higher rate raises the denominator and lowers PV.
  2. 200/1.05 + 300/1.05² = 190.48 + 272.11 = 462.59.
  3. Higher: the annuity due equals the ordinary annuity times (1 + 0.05), so 272.32 × 1.05 ≈ 285.94, because each flow arrives one period earlier.
