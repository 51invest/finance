Insurance Pricing and Pension Liabilities: The Ledger of Risk Sharing

Insurance does not abolish risk; it pools many small-probability, large-loss exposures into a predictable pool. Premium must cover expected claims, expenses and capital, while reserves must cover future benefits. Pensions separate current contributions from future promises; a funding gap is eventually closed by contributions, investment returns or public money.

Definition

Insurance pools many similar but largely independent exposures and exchanges premiums for a promise to pay affected members. The risk premium is the present value of expected claims; the expense loading covers acquisition, underwriting, administration and capital; reserves are obligations for claims already incurred but not settled; reinsurance transfers part of the tail risk to another insurer[1][2].

A pension liability is the present value of promised future benefits. Pay-as-you-go systems use current contributions to pay current retirees; funded systems invest contributions. Both can run gaps, but the timing and legal bearer of the gap differ[3].

Intuition

Insurance is a shared water tank: everyone pours in a little, and whoever has a fire draws from it. The tank must be large enough, but not so large that nobody wants to maintain it. Premiums too low drain the tank; premiums too high drive members away. Actuarial work turns “how often, how large, how much buffer” into checkable numbers.

A pension is a bill stretched across decades: contribute while working, receive after retiring. The system must answer three questions: where is the money now, is the return assumption credible, and who pays if it falls short. Separating these questions shows that a pension gap is an intertemporal allocation, not a moral verdict.

When it applies

  • Risks in the pool should be relatively independent; otherwise one systemic event damages every member at once.
  • Loss probability and size must be estimable; new or extreme tail risks need thicker capital or reinsurance.
  • Reserve assumptions must separate reported, incurred-but-not-reported and future business; one discount rate cannot cover all.
  • The pension discount rate must match benefit timing and risk; a high-return assumption lowers the liability on paper and pushes the gap into the future.
  • Reinsurance reduces one insurer’s tail risk but adds counterparty risk; capital and reinsurance are layers, not substitutes.

Example and calculation

Suppose 1000 similar policies, each with a 2% loss probability and a 50-unit loss, fixed cost 0.2 units per policy and a 20% expense loading. Expected claims per policy are 2%×50=1.02%×50=1.02%×50=1.0 unit; the risk premium is (1.0+0.2)/(1−20%)=1.5(1.0+0.2)/(1-20%)=1.5(1.0+0.2)/(1−20%)=1.5 units.

If the actual claims rate rises to 3%, expected claims per policy become 1.5 units; the old 1.5 premium covers claims but leaves nothing for expenses and capital. If reinsurance cedes 30% of claims, the insurer’s retained tail falls, but it pays a reinsurance premium; net capital relief need not fall proportionally.

Pension example: present value of future benefits 100, present value of future contributions 70, current assets 20; the gap is 100−70−20=10100-70-20=10100−70−20=10. If the return assumption falls from 4% to 2%, the benefit present value may rise to 110 and the gap to 20. The assets did not “disappear”; the discount rate and the promise horizon changed the ledger.

Common mistakes

“Insurance is gambling.” Insurance handles estimable probabilities and correlations; gambling creates new risk. The only shared feature is uncertainty[1].

“Reserves are profit.” Reserves are liabilities, not distributable profit. Releasing reserves raises current profit but shrinks the buffer for future claims.

“A pension gap can be filled by a higher return assumption.” A higher assumption changes the present value on paper, not the future return. More risk can make the gap appear later and larger[3].
