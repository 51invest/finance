Financial Stability

Financial stability is the system's ability to keep payments, saving, borrowing and investment working through a shock without cascading failures, not the absence of volatility.

ModuleFIN-M17 · 金融稳定
LevelFoundation
Read5 min · 12 sections

Definition

Financial stability is a state in which the financial system provides households and businesses with crucial services—paying, saving, borrowing and investing—in good times and bad, without cascading failures[3]. This article defines systemic risk as the risk that a failure of one institution or market spreads through balance sheets, collateral, common investors or payment networks and disrupts services at scale. Stability is not the absence of volatility; it is volatility that does not stop the system.

Intuition

A bank with insufficient capital after loan losses faces depositor withdrawals; to raise cash it sells assets at a discount, prices fall, other banks holding similar assets lose value, and more depositors run. One institution's problem becomes a system problem through common assets and confidence.

Formal expression

Systemic risk

Systemic risk comes from size, interconnectedness, substitutability and common exposure. “Too big to fail” is one case; payment systems, clearing houses, funds and collateral markets can also be transmission nodes.

Runs

A bank run is concentrated withdrawal driven by doubt about solvency. Banks fund long-term assets with short-term liabilities and are inherently run-prone; deposit insurance, central bank liquidity and asset discounts change the speed.

Contagion

Contagion spreads through direct exposures, common collateral, common investors, information asymmetry and payment interruptions. Assessment must map networks and common exposures, not only individual capital ratios.

Leverage

Leverage magnifies the effect of asset-price moves on capital. Highly levered institutions sell assets under stress, potentially triggering a discount spiral; leverage, maturity structure and collateral haircuts determine fragility.

Regulatory capital and macroprudential policy

Regulatory capital requires banks to absorb losses with own funds, lowering failure probability[1]; the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) requires enough high-quality liquid assets to meet short-term stress[2]. Macroprudential tools—countercyclical buffers, loan-to-income caps and stress tests—target the system rather than one institution[4].

Conditions and limits

  1. Stability assessment must separate solvency from liquidity: a capitalised bank with no liquidity can still run.
  2. Stress tests depend on scenarios and correlation assumptions; benign assumptions understate tail losses.
  3. Macroprudential tools can be procyclical; timing and exit need pre-design.
  4. Cross-border institutions divide responsibility between home and host; coordination failure is itself a risk.

Worked example

Suppose a bank has assets of 100, liabilities of 95 and capital of 5, a 5% capital ratio. An 8 yuan loan loss cuts assets to 92 while liabilities stay at 95, so capital becomes -3. These are teaching assumptions.

Item Before shock After shock
Assets 100 92
Liabilities 95 95
Capital 5 -3
Capital/assets 5% -3.3%

A loss equal to 8% of assets wipes out a 5% capital ratio and turns it negative. If the bank must sell assets, discounts cut capital further; if depositors withdraw at the same time, liquidity pressure may arrive before solvency failure. Capital and liquidity are two different lines of defence.

Common mistakes

Financial stability means no market volatility. Stability means services do not stop; price volatility is normal, cascading failure is the concern.

Capital adequacy equals safety. Capital absorbs losses, liquidity handles runs; both are needed and both depend on asset quality and maturity structure.

Knowledge-point review

systemic risk

Definition: Systemic risk is the danger that a shock propagates through institutions, markets and infrastructure and threatens the financial system's functions.

Mechanism: Size, interconnectedness, substitutability and complexity define systemic importance; leverage, maturity mismatch and common exposures amplify shocks.

Example: For example, failure of a large clearing bank could halt countless payments and trades, hitting the real economy.

Boundary: Systemic risk is not the sum of individual risks; linkages and confidence feedback amplify nonlinearly, and warning indicators can lag.

bank runs

Definition: A bank run is a mass withdrawal by depositors or investors fearing insolvency, becoming self-fulfilling.

Mechanism: Runs force asset fire sales, drain liquidity and spread confidence loss; deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort and liquidity rules can stop them.

Example: For example, a rumour of losses brings depositors to withdraw, the bank sells bonds at a loss, and the loss feeds more withdrawals.

Boundary: Runs are not limited to banks; money funds, shadow banks and stablecoins can face redemptions, and insurance and rescue create moral hazard.

contagion

Definition: Contagion is the spread of risk beyond direct exposures across institutions, markets or countries.

Mechanism: It travels through balance sheets, common assets, information asymmetry and confidence; cross-border and derivative chains accelerate it.

Example: For example, a forced fund sale depresses prices, hurting holders of similar assets and triggering deleveraging.

Boundary: Contagion is not inevitable; firewalls, capital buffers and diversification limit it, but correlations and liquidity can jump under stress.

leverage

Definition: Leverage uses borrowed funds or derivatives to magnify asset exposure, scaling gains and losses.

Mechanism: It arises through debt, repos, margin and off-sheet structures; haircuts and margin calls force deleveraging under stress.

Example: For example, 10 yuan of equity borrowing 90 to hold 100 of assets loses all capital if assets fall 10 percent.

Boundary: Leverage is not simply risk-taking; moderate leverage can raise returns and liquidity, but procyclical deleveraging amplifies systemic risk, and accounting leverage can hide derivative exposure.

regulatory capital and macroprudential policy

Definition: Regulatory capital is loss-absorbing capacity required by supervisors; macroprudential policy uses buffers, loan-to-value limits and liquidity tools to contain systemic risk.

Mechanism: Capital is risk-weighted, and countercyclical buffers build in booms and release in stress; macroprudential policy watches aggregate credit and asset prices.

Example: For example, a hot housing market may face higher loan-to-value limits and a countercyclical capital buffer to curb leverage.

Boundary: Tighter regulation is not always better; risk weights, models and cross-border coordination can fail, and capital rules may push risk into shadow banking.
