Ample Reserves and the Floor System: How the Corridor Works

Under an ample-reserves framework, the central bank no longer fine-tunes rates through scarce reserves and open-market operations; it sets a floor with the reserve rate and ON RRP rate, letting market rates run inside a corridor. This article uses a reserve supply-demand table to explain why the floor system needs reverse repos and when it fails.

Definition and intuition

An ample-reserves framework means banks hold reserves well above the minimum, so short rates are no longer set by reserve scarcity but by a corridor. The floor is usually the reserve rate (IORB) or the overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) rate; the ceiling is a standing repo or lending facility.

Under a scarce-reserves framework, the central bank fine-tunes reserve supply through open-market operations and lets the repo rate fluctuate around a target; under an ample framework, reserves are abundant and the quantity tool loses sensitivity, so the central bank manages the price.

The floor system does not freeze rates; it keeps market rates inside the corridor. The floor supports non-bank lending, the ceiling caps bank funding cost, and arbitrage keeps rates from drifting too far[1][2]. The Eurosystem balance sheet offers a comparison: reserves, Treasury deposits and bond portfolios differ, but the corridor logic is similar[3].

Supply and demand

Let bank reserves R = 300 billion yuan, minimum demand R_min = 250 billion, and ON RRP RRP = 50 billion. Reserves are 50 billion above demand, and non-banks park idle cash at ON RRP to earn the floor rate.

When Treasury issuance or higher reserve demand drains R, RRP is absorbed first; once RRP hits zero, reserves approach R_min and the repo rate moves toward the ceiling. If R falls below R_min, banks must borrow reserves from each other and rates can break the ceiling.

The floor has two supports: IORB for bank reserves and ON RRP for non-bank cash. If ON RRP is zero, non-banks lose their shelter and short rates can fall below the floor; if the ceiling facility carries stigma, rates can break above it. From a liquidity-regulation view, the LCR also requires banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets, which shapes their reliance on corridor facilities[4]. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose the corridor floor is 4.50%, the ceiling 4.75%, and the repo rate 4.55%. Treasury issuance drains 80 billion yuan, 40 billion from reserves and 40 billion from ON RRP; RRP falls to 10 billion and R to 260 billion.

Reserves remain 10 billion above R_min, and the repo rate rises to 4.62%, still inside the corridor. Another 30 billion drain takes R to 230 billion, 20 billion below R_min; the repo rate may rise to 4.80% and break the ceiling. The central bank must slow issuance, activate standing repo, or lower reserve demand.

The floor system is not an automatic stabiliser; it requires daily monitoring of reserve distribution, Treasury deposits and quarter-end settlement. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: reserves are unevenly distributed. Aggregate abundance does not mean every bank has enough; small banks, foreign banks and quarter-end settlement can run short.

Boundary 2: ON RRP counterparties are limited. Only eligible money funds can use it; too narrow a list lets non-bank cash push rates below the floor.

Boundary 3: ceiling stigma. Banks may avoid the standing facility to avoid appearing weak, preferring expensive funding and letting rates break the ceiling.

Mistake 1: ample reserves mean printing money. Reserves are liabilities, not free funds; they correspond to assets such as bonds and loans.

Mistake 2: the floor system needs no open-market operations. The central bank still adjusts supply, runs repos and monitors distribution; only the frequency and tools change.

Deeper note: the corridor's boundary is distribution, not the total

The common mistake in an ample-reserves framework is watching the total and ignoring distribution. An ample total does not mean every bank can borrow when needed; if reserves sit with a few large banks, smaller banks may still scramble at quarter-end or under stress, pushing repo rates toward the ceiling. Judge the corridor by total, distribution and collateral together.

Quarter-end and tax dates stress-test the corridor. Banks hoard reserves to meet reporting and settlement needs, while higher Treasury balances drain reserves from the system. ON RRP balances may fall first, then repo rates rise. A central bank that watches only the policy rate and ignores quarter-end funding can get an 'ample but tight' mismatch.

Tool stigma can break the ceiling. A standing repo facility should cap rates, but banks fearing a 'funding stress' label may prefer paying up in the interbank market; the ceiling exists and rates still overshoot. Normal use, anonymity and clear disclosure rules reduce stigma.

ON RRP is not an unlimited floor. When non-banks lack eligible counterparties or collateral, idle cash may move to the interbank market and push rates down; when ON RRP balances are exhausted, only IORB remains, and since only banks earn IORB directly, non-bank cash can fall below the floor.

Balance-sheet runoff changes how the corridor works. As reserves move from 'ample' back to 'scarce', open-market operations and reserve demand matter again and the central bank may need active management. That is a framework switch, not a failure; communicate it early or markets will read a technical adjustment as a policy turn.

Diagnostics and framework switching

Corridor diagnostics track four quantities: reserves minus minimum demand, the distribution of reserves across banks (approximated by intraday overdrafts and dispersion in interbank rates), ON RRP balances, and use of the ceiling facility. The corridor is stable only when all four are healthy; watching one invites misdiagnosis.

Rate dispersion is the direct signal of distribution problems. If the standard deviation of overnight interbank rates widens, some institutions are scrambling; if the repo-IORB spread widens, reserves are no longer 'ample'. Central banks can monitor dispersion daily rather than waiting for quarter-end.

Framework switching must distinguish technical adjustment from policy change. Reserves falling under runoff and ON RRP exhaustion are technical; if the central bank resumes active open-market operations, markets may read it as tightening. Communication should state that the aim is to preserve the corridor, not to change the policy stance.

Compared with a scarce framework: the scarce framework relies on forecasting reserve demand and frequent operations - large errors but a direct signal; the ample framework relies on the corridor and facilities - fewer operations but dependence on distribution and tool credibility. Neither is inherently better; match the framework to balance-sheet size, regulation and market structure.

Common misuses: reading a fall in ON RRP as 'abundant liquidity' when cash may simply move to the Treasury or money funds; treating IORB as a hard floor when non-banks cannot earn it directly.

Further reading

Borio and colleagues at the BIS stress that corridor and reserve-system choices must match the financial structure; Ihrig and co-authors at the Federal Reserve compare scarce and ample reserve frameworks, noting that in an ample framework the 'quantity' signal of open-market operations weakens and communication and facility design matter more.

Focus on three questions: which facilities set the floor and ceiling, whether their use carries stigma, and how reserve distribution is monitored. Apply these to any country's framework and you can judge whether its corridor is nominal or actually effective. One caveat: framework comparisons often assume similar banking structures; where non-bank intermediation is large, the effective floor may depend on facilities open to non-banks, not only on IORB, and on how those facilities are priced relative to money-market alternatives.

What would change the conclusion

The ample-reserves corridor depends on stable reserve demand:

  1. Reserve demand is unstable. If regulation, payments or bank behaviour make demand hard to forecast, the corridor rate de-anchors.
  2. Money funds move to ON RRP. If non-banks park cash at the central-bank facility, interbank rates are diverted and the corridor narrows.
  3. Treasury issuance drains reserves. If heavy issuance and tax dates cut reserves quickly, scarcity returns.
