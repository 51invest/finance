Sources of Corporate Finance

Companies fund assets with internal retained earnings or external debt and equity; the sources differ in claims, cost, control and maturity, and the choice should fit the target capital structure.

Definition

Sources of corporate finance are all the channels through which a company obtains money to pay for its assets. They divide into internal financing (retained earnings) and external financing (debt, common stock, preferred stock, convertible debt and similar instruments). The right-hand side of the balance sheet is the list of sources: assets = liabilities + equity.[1]

Intuition

Every equipment purchase forces the question "where does the money come from?" The easiest answer is to keep some profit. If that is not enough, the company borrows or issues shares. Retained earnings require no negotiation, but shareholders give up a dividend; debt requires scheduled interest; new shares hand over part of the ownership. Each source carries a different price, risk and control consequence.

Formal expression

Let ΔA be new investment, RE the retained earnings available, ΔD new debt and ΔE newly issued equity:

ΔA = ΔD + RE + ΔE

If the company keeps a target debt weight wD, then ΔD = wD × ΔA. The equity requirement is (1 - wD) × ΔA; whatever remains after RE must be raised by issuing shares. This is the symbolic form of what that page works through numerically, verbatim: "If the company has a capital structure of 25% debt and 75% equity and wants to maintain that capital structure, it must increase its debt by $250,000 to balance the increase in equity." [2], together with the balancing requirement, verbatim: "Also, note that the weights for debt, preferred stock, and common stock in the capital structure sum to 100%. The company must finance 100% of its assets." [2]. The formula itself is our restatement of that example.

Conditions and limits

  1. Retained earnings are not free money: shareholders require a return on them, and that opportunity cost belongs in the cost of equity.[2]
  2. Debt carries fixed interest and a prior claim; weak cash flow raises default risk.
  3. New shares dilute existing owners and can involve issuance costs.[2]
  4. Internal funds are limited by net income and payout policy; a loss-making company cannot rely on retained earnings.
  5. External channels depend on firm size, disclosure and market access. Not every company can make a public offering; exempt offerings (such as Regulation D, Regulation A and crowdfunding) and registered public offerings are different routes.[3]
  6. The choice also changes control, maturity and tax treatment, so the nominal interest rate alone is a poor guide.

Worked example

A hypothetical company (not a real business) plans 500 million yuan of new investment with a 40% target debt weight.

Item Value (millions of yuan)
New investment ΔA 500
Target debt weight wD 40%
New debt ΔD 200
Equity requirement 300
Retained earnings available RE 180
New equity to issue ΔE 120

ΔD = 40% × 500 = 200; equity requirement = 60% × 500 = 300; 300 - 180 = 120. The three pieces sum to 200 + 180 + 120 = 500, so the debt weight stays at 40%. If all of the new financing were debt, the debt weight of the increment would be 100%, moving the company away from its target.

Common mistakes

Retained earnings are free. They are not. The money could have been paid out as dividends, so shareholders give up current cash and must be compensated.[2]

Equity has no cost because it never has to be repaid. Equity holders hold the residual claim and normally demand a higher return than lenders - the page states the ordering verbatim: "Preferred shareholders will require a higher rate of return than debt holders, 9.17%, because they are later in the order of claimants. Common shareholders are the residual claimants, standing at the end of the line to receive payment." and "Thus, common shareholders have the greatest amount of risk and require the highest rate of return." [2]. (The 9.17% inside that quotation is the preferred-stock figure from the page's own worked example, not a number used in this article.)

Sources and capital structure are the same decision. The source is the instrument used; capital structure is the debt-and-equity mix that results, covered in FIN-022.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "FIN-021". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "sources of corporate finance". Column two states the transmission channel of "internal financing and retained earnings". Column three states the institutional boundary of "debt and equity financing". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "internal financing and retained earnings" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. debt and equity financing is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: A 40% target debt weight can still be funded mostly from retained earnings

A company plans 500 million of new investment with a 40% target debt weight, requiring 200 of debt and 300 of equity. Retained earnings provide 180, so 120 of new equity is needed; the three sources sum to 500. If retained earnings are 300, the firm uses 200 debt, 300 retained earnings and no new equity, while the debt weight stays at 40%. If the target falls to 20%, it needs 100 debt and 400 equity; after 180 of retained earnings, it still needs 220 of new equity, increasing dilution. If it actually borrows 300, uses 180 retained and issues 20, the new financing is 60% debt and drifts from the target.

The conclusion reverses when issuance costs or control matter more than the weight: with a 5% equity issuance cost and family owners unwilling to dilute, the firm may temporarily borrow more and accept the deviation. The boundary is that financing choice jointly optimises claims, cost, maturity and control. Looking only at the debt weight mistakes a source mix for a capital-structure target.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a financing plan.

The financing sequence is also shaped by information and signalling. An equity issue may be read as a sign that shares are overvalued, while debt may signal management confidence; the same money sends different messages depending on source. Choosing a instrument requires weighing issuance cost, dilution, covenants and market reaction together.

Reading and review

Corporate financing is not a matter of choosing whichever source looks cheapest; cash flow, asset form and control rights set the order. Retained earnings are cheapest because they avoid issuance costs and new disclosure; debt brings a tax shield but adds fixed claims and financial distress cost; equity dilutes control but absorbs losses. Asset-light firms with stable cash flow lean on internal funds and debt; asset-heavy firms with uncertain growth rely more on equity. Judge capital structure by collateral, cash-flow predictability and the urgency of growth options, not tax rates alone. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. A company buys equipment with undistributed profit. Is this internal or external financing?
  2. Calculation. New investment is 300 million yuan, the target debt weight is 30%, and retained earnings available are 150 million yuan. How much new equity must be issued?
  3. Conditions. Why is it hard for a loss-making start-up to expand using retained earnings?
Suggested answers
  1. Internal financing. The funds come from retained earnings, with no new debt or shares.
  2. New debt = 30% × 300 = 90; equity requirement = 210; new equity = 210 - 150 = 60.
  3. Retained earnings come from net income, and a company with continuing losses has none to retain. It must use external debt or equity, and outside investors will usually demand a higher return or stricter terms.
