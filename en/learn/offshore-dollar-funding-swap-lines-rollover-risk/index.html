Offshore Dollar Funding: Swaps, FX Swaps and Rollover Risk

Offshore institutions need dollars but lack U.S. deposit insurance and the discount window; they obtain dollars through FX swaps, offshore deposits and central-bank swap lines. When dollars tighten, swap basis widens and rollover becomes difficult, transmitting stress back onshore.

Definition and intuition

Offshore dollars are dollar-denominated deposits and claims circulating outside the United States. They are not created directly by the Fed but by commercial banks, FX swaps and offshore branches.

Offshore institutions obtain dollars through three channels: FX swaps (exchange local currency for dollars and agree to reverse later), offshore dollar deposits (non-U.S. banks take dollar deposits), and central-bank dollar swap lines (the Fed lends dollars to other central banks, which on-lend to local banks).

The key vulnerability is maturity mismatch: short liabilities fund long assets and must be rolled over. When dollar funding tightens, rollover costs rise, institutions sell assets and stress transmits back onshore[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a European bank swaps euros for 100 million dollars for three months. If the spot and swap cost is 0.3% annualised, funding costs 0.3%; if dollars tighten and the swap points widen, the annualised cost rises to 1.2%, 0.9 points above the onshore dollar rate.

That 0.9 point is the swap basis: offshore dollars are more expensive than onshore dollars. If the bank's assets yield 1.0% and funding costs 1.2%, it loses 200,000 dollars a year per 100 million and must cut lending or sell assets.

If a central-bank swap line is available, the bank can swap local currency for dollars near the policy rate and compress the basis. Swap lines are crisis tools, not daily funding sources. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three risks

Risk 1: rollover. A three-month swap matures; if the dollar market closes, the institution cannot roll funding and must sell assets.

Risk 2: FX and collateral. Local-currency depreciation makes swap counterparties demand more collateral, raising funding costs further.

Risk 3: contagion. Offshore dollar funds, non-U.S. banks and emerging-market borrowers hold overlapping exposures; deleveraging by one triggers chain selling.

Central-bank swaps, standing repo and regulatory liquidity requirements can ease but not eliminate maturity mismatch; the offshore dollar system remains a weak link in global finance[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: offshore dollars are not fake dollars. They are commercial-bank liabilities subject to issuer and host-country regulation, not direct Fed liabilities.

Boundary 2: swap lines have political and size limits. Not every central bank has Fed access; size, collateral and tenor are limited.

Boundary 3: offshore markets serve trade and investment. They are not only speculative; trade settlement, cross-border loans and bond issuance depend on them.

Mistake 1: a reserve currency means cheap funding forever. Offshore dollar premia can spike in crises; reserve status is not unlimited liquidity.

Mistake 2: swap lines solve everything. They provide liquidity, not solvency or collateral discounts; banks still need capital and maturity management.

Deeper note: cross-currency basis and the rollover ladder

The pricing core of offshore dollar funding is the cross-currency basis. A European bank needing dollars in New York can borrow directly in the dollar money market or borrow euros and swap them into dollars. The implied dollar cost equals the euro rate plus the swap cost; when direct dollar funding tightens, the cross-currency basis widens and borrowing euros to swap becomes more expensive. The basis is not an abstract indicator; it enters the bank's funding cost directly.

Three funding channels need separate reading. First, dollar commercial paper and repo: short-term and sensitive to sentiment. Second, the FX swap market: flexible but constrained by counterparty limits and collateral. Third, central-bank swap lines: cheap but available only to eligible institutions. Under stress the first two tighten and the third backstops, but with stigma and limits.

The rollover ladder is the basic risk tool. List dollar liabilities maturing in the next 30, 90 and 365 days by date, marking the amount to roll, the rate and counterparty concentration. If maturities on some days exceed liquid assets, or counterparties are concentrated, rollover risk is high. 2008 and March 2020 both showed 'sound assets, frozen liabilities'.

Swap lines have boundaries. They relieve offshore dollar shortages but cannot fix solvency; they move liquidity risk from banks to the central bank's balance sheet and may let institutions delay deleveraging. Users may also be marked as 'needing help', creating stigma.

Monitoring indicators include the dollar Libor/SOFR-OIS spread, the cross-currency basis, the maturity structure of commercial paper outstanding, FX swap volumes, and bank CDS and equity. One indicator misleads; a combination separates technical tightness from a credit crisis.

Policy implications: lengthen and diversify dollar funding, reduce reliance on short-term wholesale funding; under stress use FX swaps and repo before central-bank facilities; supervise money funds and foreign banks' dollar liabilities because they amplify the offshore dollar chain.

Common misuses: treating swap lines as unlimited dollars, ignoring limits, eligible collateral and stigma; treating a wider cross-currency basis only as an arbitrage, ignoring the funding stress it reflects; treating offshore dollar shortages as a purely US problem, ignoring global dollar linkage.

Finally, offshore dollar risk is not 'whether dollars exist' but 'whether dollar liabilities can be rolled at tolerable cost when needed'. Read the balance sheet, the rollover ladder and counterparty concentration rather than a single rate.

Stress scenarios: three levels of dollar funding shock

Mild shock: dollar rates rise 50 basis points and the cross-currency basis widens 20. Banks can still roll liabilities but at higher cost; extend some maturities to lock in funding and avoid using swap lines.

Moderate shock: commercial paper closes to some issuers and repo haircuts rise 10 points. Banks need replacement funding within 30 days or must sell liquid assets; if liquidity is insufficient, turn to central-bank or swap facilities. Distinguish a liquidity problem from a solvency problem; the latter cannot be funded away.

Severe shock: several institutions stop rolling at once, the basis widens beyond 100 points and FX swap counterparties refuse. Banks depend on central-bank facilities and collateral; insufficient collateral forces fire sales. Policy focus shifts to contagion, not single-firm profit.

Trigger indicators: dollar Libor/SOFR spread, cross-currency basis, shortening commercial paper tenor, repo haircuts and counterparty CDS. All four worsening means it is not technical tightness.

Preparedness list: maturity ladder, collateral inventory, counterparty limits, backup facilities, stress tests and recovery plans. Prepare in calm times or pay the highest price under stress.

Key takeaways

  • The marginal cost of offshore dollar funding is the cross-currency basis, not a single dollar rate.
  • The three channels - money market, FX swaps and central-bank facilities - tighten in sequence, and facilities have limits and stigma.
  • The rollover ladder lists maturing amounts, rates and counterparty concentration by date, the basic tool for rollover risk.
  • Swap lines solve liquidity, not solvency; users may be marked by the market.
  • Monitor spreads, basis, CP tenor, repo haircuts and counterparty CDS together; one indicator misleads.

What would change the conclusion

Offshore-dollar conclusions depend on swap lines and basis:

  1. Larger central-bank swaps. If facilities are ample and quickly usable, rollover risk falls and rate shocks are buffered.
  2. Narrower basis. If the cross-currency basis normalises, hedging costs fall and dollar funding pressure eases.
  3. Natural dollar assets. If borrowers have dollar revenues or assets, currency mismatch is smaller than headline liabilities.
  4. Non-bank intermediation. If shadow banks supply dollars, risk moves from banks to less-regulated entities.
  5. Global dollar shortage. If dollars are scarce everywhere in a crisis, swaps and reserves cannot cover all borrowers.

Practical implication: offshore-dollar risk depends on swap availability, basis and borrowers' natural dollar assets; headline external debt alone misstates rollover pressure.

Data note: consolidate on- and off-balance dollar liabilities by remaining maturity; parent-only foreign bonds miss SPVs and repo.

Scope note: separate bank, non-bank and corporate borrowers; their dollar funding channels and run speeds differ, and merging hides risk. Also distinguish drawn liabilities from committed lines, which get drawn in stress.

Final note: separate drawn debt from committed lines; undrawn facilities get drawn in stress.
