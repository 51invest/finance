Custody and Asset Rights

Custody hands safekeeping, settlement and record-keeping to a specialist institution, but it does not by itself change beneficial ownership; segregation and the ability to exercise rights depend on the account structure, the agreement and applicable law.

Definition

Custody is the holding of client funds and securities for safekeeping, normally by a qualified custodian that handles settlement, corporate actions and account records. Asset rights are the beneficial owner’s rights to income, voting, transfer and claims. Under the U.S. SEC custody rule for investment advisers the test is broader: holding client funds or securities directly or indirectly, or having any authority to obtain possession of them, is custody [1]. Serving as general partner or trustee usually counts as custody, while limited authority to move assets between a client’s own accounts on the client’s written instruction may not [2]. Custody changes who holds and controls assets, usually not who beneficially owns them; ownership follows the account agreement, registration and applicable law.

Intuition

Securities are often held in street name - registered in the name of the broker or a nominee - while the client remains the beneficial owner, and dividends, votes and corporate actions pass along the custody chain. This paragraph is our general description of a common holding structure: the registered SEC staff FAQ returns 0 hits for the word face street name across its 66,093 characters, and no source read this round defines street name. If client assets are commingled with the custodian's own assets, insolvency recovery depends on local law and the account structure.

Formal expression

The chain runs from the client to the investment manager (instructions), the qualified custodian and then the sub-custodian or central securities depository. Key variables are the account name, authority and segregation of client assets. Reconciliation:

Q_end = Q_begin + Q_buy - Q_sell + Q_action
V = Q × P

where Q is units (shares or other holdings), P is the price per unit in a stated currency (for example, yuan per share), V is market value in that same currency and Q_action covers split and reverse-split adjustments.

Conditions and limits

  1. The custodian is not the owner. The client is the beneficial owner; the custodian holds the assets in the client’s name or as agent or trustee [1].
  2. Qualified custodians are limited. Under the U.S. SEC rule a qualified custodian is a bank or savings association whose deposits are insured by the FDIC, a registered broker-dealer holding the assets in customer accounts, a registered futures commission merchant holding the assets in customer accounts (only for client funds and futures-related securities), or a foreign financial institution that customarily holds financial assets for clients and keeps client assets segregated from its own [1].
  3. Segregation is conditional. Client assets must be kept in a separate account holding only that client’s assets, or in an account holding only the adviser’s clients’ assets opened in the adviser’s name as agent or trustee [1]; omnibus accounts, sub-custody and cross-border arrangements make checks harder.
  4. Rights travel through a chain. Dividends, votes and corporate actions face cut-off times, fees and nominee rules - an empirical generalisation by this article, and the registered SEC staff FAQ returns no corporate action or cut-off word face.
  5. Rehypothecation and lending change rights. Margin and securities lending can turn ownership into a contractual claim, and statements plus independent verification do not by themselves guarantee that the assets exist - both sentences are this article's general framing (the staff FAQ returns 0 hits for repledg, securities lending and guarantee). The rule binds only advisers registered with, or required to be registered with, the U.S. SEC, and it excludes certain situations - for example the account of a registered investment company, mutual fund shares held through the fund's transfer agent, and certain privately offered securities [1].

Worked example

Hypothetical teaching example; share counts are shares and money amounts are in yuan (assumed CNY). A client holds 1,000 shares, buys 200, sells 100, and a 2-for-1 split (each share becomes two) follows.

  • Before the split: 1,000 + 200 - 100 = 1,100 shares; after: 1,100 × 2 = 2,200 shares.
  • Post-split dividend of 0.50 yuan per share: 2,200 × 0.50 = 1,100 yuan.
  • A statement still showing 1,100 shares differs by 2,200 - 1,100 = 1,100 shares and should be reconciled to transactions and corporate actions.

Under the assumed one-share-one-vote rule, 2,200 shares carry 2,200 votes; preferred shares usually have no vote but receive fixed dividends first [3].

Common misconceptions

  • Custody means selling the assets. No: beneficial ownership usually remains with the client.
  • The broker holds the shares, so the client has no rights. No: the client is still the beneficial owner.
  • Custody removes risk. No: segregation, rehypothecation, sub-custody and cross-border law still matter.

Exercises

  1. Concept. A broker holds a client’s shares in street name. Which statement is most accurate? A. The client loses ownership; B. The client is the beneficial owner and the broker is the nominee; C. The broker gains voting rights; D. The shares become the broker’s assets.
  2. Calculation. Starting with 1,000 shares, buying 200 and selling 100, how many shares remain after a 2-for-1 split? What dividend is due if the post-split dividend is 0.50 yuan per share?
  3. Conditions. A manager may deduct its fee directly from the custody account. Does that create custody under the SEC rule? Does the fee exception remove all duties?
Suggested answers
  1. B. The client keeps beneficial ownership; votes normally follow client instructions.
  2. 1,000 + 200 - 100 = 1,100 shares; after the split 1,100 × 2 = 2,200 shares; dividend 2,200 × 0.50 = 1,100 yuan.
  3. Yes. An arrangement that lets the adviser withdraw client funds or securities from the custodian on instruction is custody [1]. The fee-deduction exception waives only the annual independent verification, not the qualified-custodian or client account statement requirements [1].
