Capital Structure

Capital structure is the relative share of debt and equity financing; in perfect markets leverage does not change firm value, while tax shields and financial distress costs jointly set a target debt level.

ModuleFIN-M12 · 公司融资与投资
LevelFoundation
Read4 min · 10 sections

Definition

Capital structure is the relative proportion of debt and equity financing on the balance sheet. It determines where the money came from, the order of claims, and the amount of financial leverage.[1]

Intuition

Take two companies with identical assets and operations. If one borrows more, its shareholders earn more in good years because lenders receive only fixed interest; they also have a thinner cushion in bad years because interest is paid first. Debt brings a tax shield and also the possibility of default and distress. Capital structure is the trade-off between the two.

Formal expression

With debt D and equity E measured at market value, the debt weight is D% = D / (D + E) and the equity weight is E% = E / (D + E).[1]

In a model with corporate taxes but no distress costs, the value of the levered firm equals the unlevered value plus the interest tax shield. The interest tax shield equals the tax rate T times interest payments.[2] For perpetual debt at rate rd, the present value of the shield is T × D - this step divides T × interest by rd and is this article's derivation, not a verbatim formula on the page.

In a perfect market without taxes, Modigliani-Miller (MM) Proposition I says leverage does not change firm value, and Proposition II says the cost of equity rises with the debt-to-equity ratio:

rE = rU + (D / E) × (rU - rd)

Conditions and limits

  1. The MM results assume perfect markets. 17.4 states one assumption verbatim: "One of the assumptions of this perfect capital market is that there are no taxes." [2]. The other three items listed here - no distress costs, no agency costs, and a given investment policy - are this article's own summary of the textbook idealisation: 17.4's verbatim text does not contain distress, agency or investment decision, so they are not attributed to that page. The 17.5 static page returned only its title this round; the distress-cost and target-structure sentences in conditions 2-5 rely on the machineReview curl record and are listed as a residual risk.
  2. The tax shield matters only when the company has taxable profit; once interest expense reaches earnings before interest and taxes, extra interest produces no further tax benefit.[3]
  3. More debt raises the probability of default. Direct distress costs include fees for lawyers and advisers; indirect costs include lost customers and suppliers.[3]
  4. Trade-off theory weighs the tax shield against distress costs; firms with higher distress costs have a lower optimal leverage.[3]
  5. The optimal structure is a target, not a precise number - the page says verbatim "A company will use this optimal debt level to determine what the weight of debt should be in its target capital structure. The optimal capital structure is the target." [3] - and because market values move daily, adjusting back toward it is costly, verbatim: "Reducing debt and increasing equity would require transaction costs that might be quite significant." [3].
  6. Industry differences are large; no single debt ratio fits every company.

Worked example

A hypothetical company (not a real business) has an unlevered value of 1,000 million yuan. It issues 300 million yuan of perpetual debt at 5%, faces a 25% corporate tax rate, and ignores distress costs.

Item Value
Unlevered value VU 1,000 million yuan
Debt D 300 million yuan
Annual interest 15 million yuan
Annual tax shield = 25% × 15 3.75 million yuan
PV of tax shield = 3.75 / 5% 75 million yuan
Levered value VL 1,075 million yuan
Equity E = VL - D 775 million yuan
Debt weight D% 27.9%
Equity weight E% 72.1%

The tax shield is 75 = 25% × 300; VL = 1,000 + 75 = 1,075; D% = 300 / 1,075 = 27.9% and E% = 775 / 1,075 = 72.1%.

Common mistakes

More debt always lowers the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). In a perfect market without taxes, the rise in the cost of equity exactly offsets cheaper debt, so WACC is unchanged. With taxes the shield lowers WACC, but distress costs push the other way.[2][3]

Capital structure is the book debt ratio. Decisions should use market-value weights; book values reflect historical cost.[1]

There is one precise optimal debt ratio. The optimum is a target that shifts with tax rates, cash flow and industry conditions, not a precise number.[3]

Exercises

  1. Concept check. In a perfect market without taxes, does adding debt raise total firm value?
  2. Calculation. Unlevered value is 900 million yuan, perpetual debt is 250 million yuan at 4%, and the tax rate is 20%. Find the present value of the tax shield, the levered value and the debt weight.
  3. Conditions. Why do companies with highly volatile cash flow usually choose lower leverage?
Suggested answers
  1. No. MM Proposition I says firm value comes from the cash flow generated by assets; leverage only splits that value between debt and equity.
  2. Annual interest = 250 × 4% = 10; annual tax shield = 20% × 10 = 2; PV of tax shield = 2 / 4% = 50; VL = 900 + 50 = 950; D% = 250 / 950 = 26.3%.
  3. With volatile cash flow, fixed interest more easily leads to default, so expected distress costs are higher and they offset the tax shield sooner. The optimal leverage is therefore lower.
