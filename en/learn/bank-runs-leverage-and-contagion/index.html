Bank Runs, Leverage and Contagion: A Financial-Stability Stress Test

Financial stability is not the absence of volatility; it is the system’s ability to keep payments and funding going under stress. A run is a liquidity problem, leverage amplifies losses, and contagion turns one failure into a network event. A stress test separates the three layers and puts them back on one balance sheet.

Definition

Financial stability is a state in which the financial system keeps providing payments, credit and risk transfer under stress. A run is simultaneous withdrawal by depositors or short-term creditors; leverage is the degree of debt financing; contagion is the transmission of one institution’s loss through balance sheets, common assets or confidence[1].

The liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) divides high-quality liquid assets by 30-day stressed net outflows; capital buffers absorb losses, not liquidity[2].

Intuition

A bank is a long bridge held up by short ladders: deposits are the ladders, loans are the bridge. In normal times the ladders are enough; when everyone pulls them away at once, the bridge remains but nobody can cross. Leverage raises the bridge’s pivot: a small asset fall becomes a large equity fall.

Contagion is not “everyone becomes bad”; it is “balance sheets are connected”. One institution sells assets at a discount, depressing prices of similar assets; price falls hurt other institutions’ capital; damaged capital forces more sales. That loop is more dangerous than a single loss.

When it applies

  • LCR outflow assumptions must cover uninsured deposits, interbank funding and off-balance-sheet commitments; retail deposits alone understate stress.
  • Leverage must use a consistent risk-weighted or exposure basis; different measures are not directly comparable.
  • Fire-sale discounts depend on market depth and asset homogeneity; liquid assets have small discounts, homogeneous assets large ones in stress.
  • Contagion channels include interbank lending, derivatives, common assets and confidence; looking at one institution misses network exposure.
  • Capital and liquidity buffers are not substitutes: capital absorbs losses, liquidity buys time.
  • Stress scenarios need feedback loops; they cannot assume asset prices and funding conditions stay fixed at once.

Data and institutional check

Before comparing "Runs and liquidity coverage" with "Leverage and fire sales", check four things: whether units and denominators match; whether nominal and real, adjusted and unadjusted series have been reconciled; whether the sample is bound by one legal and accounting regime; and whether an independent source can reproduce the order of magnitude.

These checks are not formalism. The common failure is not an arithmetic error but dividing two numbers with different definitions and hiding the mismatch behind a tidy correlation. Every teaching number here is labelled as an assumption; formal citation follows the original source.

Three counterexamples

  1. Counterexample 1. Use capital adequacy ratios alone to judge safety: liquidity coverage ratios and uninsured deposit ratios may precede capital exhaustion. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  2. Counterexample 2. Assume that all depositors wait rationally at the same time: once a queuing signal appears, running first becomes the individual optimal, but the collective result is a run. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  3. Counterexample 3. Equating bailouts to free: The systemic risk exception protects depositors, but resolution costs go back to the deposit insurance fund and industry assessments. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.

All three point to the same thing: the model is a conditional statement, not an unconditional forecast. State the conditions and the reader knows when to use it and when to stop.

Decision checklist

  • Scope: Which entity, market and period, and does it match the source sample?
  • Measure: Level, ratio or index, and what are the denominator and base period?
  • Counterfactual: Without the event or policy, what is the most defensible alternative?
  • Sensitivity: Which single assumption, if changed, flips the conclusion?
  • Replication: Can an independent source reproduce the order of magnitude, and how is the gap explained?

The checklist does not replace judgement, but it stops an abstract conclusion from being pasted into a different setting.

Common mistakes

“Adequate capital prevents runs.” Capital is a loss buffer, not cash; with concentrated short-term liabilities, a well-capitalised bank can still run out of liquidity[2].

“High LCR is safe.” LCR depends on outflow assumptions; uninsured deposits and interbank funding can leave faster than the model.

“Contagion is only between banks.” Common assets, derivatives, money funds and cross-border links transmit shocks; network structure determines the path[3].

Deeper note: the run timeline and the policy sequence

A run is not instantaneous; it has an observable timeline. Stage 1 is an information shock: loss announcements, downgrades or interbank defaults make creditors reassess. Stage 2 is short-term funding flight: repos are not rolled, certificates find no buyers and uninsured deposits leave. Stage 3 is asset sales: the bank sells its most liquid assets, prices fall and discounts widen. Stage 4 is contagion: other banks hold similar assets, their capital is impaired and they sell too.

Policy tools map to stages. Deposit insurance reduces retail run incentives but does not cover wholesale funding or offshore deposits; a central-bank lender of last resort provides liquidity for 'good assets that cannot be sold now' but does not fix solvency; capital injection and orderly resolution handle insolvency; redemption suspension and capital controls stop the bleeding but damage confidence and long-run market access.

Leverage amplifies every stage. With 100 of assets and 10 of equity, leverage is 10; a 5% asset fall cuts equity 50%. At 20x, a 5% fall wipes equity out. Capital ratios, leverage ratios and LCR limit leverage and maturity mismatch but cannot remove run incentives; transparency and debt-structure management matter too.

A counter-intuitive point: the most liquid assets are sold first. Under stress banks sell Treasuries rather than loans because Treasuries settle quickly; but heavy sales depress Treasury prices and impair other banks' high-quality liquid assets. This is the liquidity illusion: salable in normal times, unsalable when everyone sells[3][4].

Evidence grades: who can check this claim

Sort the sentences into three layers. The first is what the source states directly: definitions, dates, units and institutional terms that anyone can check. The second is the author's calculation from the source, which must keep the intermediate values and assumptions. The third is extrapolation to a new setting, which can only be labelled a scenario, never a fact.

For "Runs and liquidity coverage", the source can usually support what it is, who publishes it and how it is measured. It cannot automatically support "the same happens in another market". Pull out the third-layer sentences and the reader can see where the risk sits.

For replication, give one person the raw numbers and assumptions and ask them to reach the same order of magnitude. If they cannot, the article is missing a step, not the reader.

Timeline and actors: a traceable chain

Every mechanism needs a timeline. Write what was published first, which price moved first and which institution reacted first. If the order is wrong, the causal story turns an outcome into a cause.

Then write the actors: who bears the loss, who receives the gain and who can change the rule. Replace collective nouns such as "the market", "investors" and "regulators" with identifiable institutions and accounts before discussing incentives.

Finally write the information set: what decision-makers knew on the day, and when they knew it. A link that is obvious after the event may have been invisible at the time. Skip this step and an ex-post explanation is dressed up as an ex-ante necessity.

Orders of magnitude: rule out arithmetic error first

Before the mechanism, run three checks. First, convert ratios into absolute amounts: 5% of 1,000 and 5% of a trillion are different objects. Second, convert annualised figures into period values: multiplying a quarterly number by four amplifies seasonality and one-offs. Third, convert nominal into real: high inflation can hide a real contraction behind nominal growth.

None of these needs a complex model, only paper and a calculator. They block the common failure of a plausible story with a wrong order of magnitude. Teaching numbers in the article are labelled assumptions; formal citation follows the date, unit and footnote of the source.

Compress the conclusion into three lines

Line one is the conclusion: one sentence with the entity, direction and condition. Line two is the condition: which assumption must hold. Line three is the counterevidence: what data would make you abandon it.

If the three lines cannot be written, the article is still wrapped in background, significance and implications rather than a testable judgement. Hand it to a reader who disagrees and ask them to attack only lines two and three; that is more useful than asking them to repeat the whole text.

This is the house style: checkable facts first, conditional judgement second, and the unknown left on the table rather than covered by a longer explanation.

Exercises

  1. Leverage question: assets 120, equity 10. What is leverage? If assets fall 4%, what is the approximate equity loss?
  2. LCR question: HQLA 18, 30-day stressed outflows 24. What is LCR? If outflows rise to 30, what is the gap?
  3. Contagion question: why does one bank’s discounted sale of similar assets hurt another bank’s capital? List two feedback channels.
  4. Timeline question: what are the four stages of a run, and where do deposit insurance and the lender of last resort work best?
  5. Deeper question: assets 100, equity 5. What is leverage? If assets fall 4%, how much does equity fall, and what does it mean for regulatory capital?
  1. Decision case. Take a real situation you know; list three data checks and one counterexample required before deciding.
  2. Concept check. Explain "Runs and liquidity coverage" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.

  2. Counterfactual. If Leverage and fire sales moved in the opposite direction, which intermediate quantity changes first and what happens to the conclusion?
  3. Data check. Take one number, find its denominator and base period; if the source omits them, say how you would handle the gap.
