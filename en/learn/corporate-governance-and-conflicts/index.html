Corporate Governance and Conflicts of Interest

Corporate governance allocates rights and accountability among shareholders, the board, management and stakeholders; independent directors, committees, related-party disclosure and voting constrain conflicts of interest but do not eliminate agency costs.

Definition

Corporate governance is the set of relationships between a company's shareholders, board, management and other stakeholders, and the structure and systems through which the company is directed, its objectives are set and performance is monitored.[1] A conflict of interest arises when a decision-maker holds competing interests that may compromise the fiduciary or agency duties owed to others.

Intuition

Managers may favor expansion and higher pay while shareholders want returns; a controlling shareholder may shift value through related-party deals. Governance mechanisms require those with decision power to disclose information, submit to review, and step aside when their own interests are at stake.

Formal expression

Independent-director share = independent directors / board seats.

In the United States, a reporting company must describe transactions since the start of its last fiscal year, or proposed transactions, involving more than $120,000 in which a related person has a direct or indirect material interest, and a series of similar transactions is aggregated; Item 404(a) reads "Describe any transaction, since the beginning of the registrant's last fiscal year, or any currently proposed transaction, in which the registrant was or is to be a participant and the amount involved exceeds $120,000, and in which any related person had or will have a direct or indirect material interest."[2] A smaller reporting company uses a lower threshold; see condition 2. A company must also identify its independent directors and, where it has audit, nominating or compensation committees, identify members who do not meet the committee independence standards.[3]

Conditions and limits

  1. Independence is determined under the applicable independence standards.[3] Formal independence is not the same as the absence of conflict in fact (this article's judgment).
  2. The $120,000 threshold applies to Item 404(a) disclosure by U.S. reporting companies - the rule reads "the amount involved exceeds $120,000". A smaller reporting company uses the lesser of $120,000 or 1% of the average of its total assets at year end for the last two completed fiscal years.[2] Below the applicable threshold, that item is not triggered, though other rules or voluntary disclosure may still apply.
  3. Related-party transactions include debt, guarantees and leases, not only purchases of goods, and a series of similar transactions is aggregated: Instruction 2 states "For purposes of paragraph (a) of this Item, a transaction includes, but is not limited to, any financial transaction, arrangement or relationship (including any indebtedness or guarantee of indebtedness) or any series of similar transactions, arrangements or relationships." and Instruction 3.a begins "In the case of any lease or other transaction providing for periodic payments or installments, the aggregate amount of all periodic payments or installments due on or after the beginning of the registrant's last fiscal year"[2]
  4. The G20/OECD Principles set governance objectives and suggest various means of achieving them - the page states "They seek to identify objectives and suggest various means for achieving them, typically involving elements of legislation, regulation, listing rules, self-regulatory arrangements, contractual undertakings, voluntary commitments and business practices." - and their stated reach is "The Principles focus on publicly traded companies, both financial and non-financial."[1]
  5. Governance mechanisms reduce but do not eliminate agency costs; a controlling shareholder can still dominate votes.
  6. Disclosure does not prove a transaction is fair; review by independent directors and abstention from voting matter as well.

Worked example

A hypothetical company (not a real business; assumed not to be a smaller reporting company) has 9 board seats, 6 independent directors and a 3-member audit committee. It enters a related-party transaction of $150,000.

Item Value
Board seats 9
Independent directors 6
Independent-director share 66.7%
Audit committee members 3
Related-party transaction $150,000
Item 404 disclosure threshold (non-SRC) $120,000
Above threshold Yes

The independent share is 6 / 9 = 66.7%. The $150,000 transaction exceeds the $120,000 threshold and, assuming the related person has a material interest, the company must disclose the related person, that person's relationship with the company, the transaction amount and the person's interest.[2]

Common mistakes

Governance is just compliance paperwork. Governance is the allocation of decision rights, accountability and disclosure; documents are only its form.

Independent directors prevent problems. Independence reduces conflicts but does not guarantee good judgment or effective monitoring.

A related-party transaction is fraud. Such transactions can be legitimate and necessary; what matters is disclosure, pricing and approval procedures.

Boundary case: Six of nine independent directors versus four of nine

A board has nine seats and six independent directors, a 66.7% independence ratio. A related-party transaction of 150,000 dollars exceeds the 120,000 disclosure threshold and requires disclosure of the related person, relationship and amount. With only four independent directors, the ratio is 44.4%; the audit committee still has three members, but approval may depend on fewer independent voices and the conflict buffer is thinner. If the amount were 100,000, below the threshold, Item 404 disclosure might not be required, though other rules could still apply.

The conclusion reverses when the transaction is ordinary-course and at market terms: even below the threshold, a related-party deal can harm shareholders through tunnelling; and a high independence ratio does not guarantee effective oversight if directors lack industry knowledge. The boundary is that governance structure and disclosure thresholds are procedural constraints, not proof of substantive fairness. Independence, committee composition, transaction size and voting rights must be read together.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not legal advice.

Governance also depends on information flow and incentives. Whether independent directors receive complete information in time, whether the audit committee can hire advisers independently and whether related parties recuse themselves matter more than seat ratios; if pay and reputation are tied to short-term results, independence weakens.

Reading and review

Corporate governance is about who has authority to decide, who bears the consequences and how conflicts are detected. Board independence, related-party approval, shareholder votes and audit committees separate decision power from oversight. Judge quality by related-party disclosure, whether independent directors can block, and whether a controller can divert resources, not by board size. The governance discount prices the probability of cash-flow diversion, not a “good governance” label. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. Do identifying independent directors and disclosing related-party transactions address the same problem?
  2. Calculation. A company has 11 board seats and 7 independent directors. What is the independent share? If a related-party transaction is $100,000, is Item 404(a) triggered?
  3. Conditions. Why use a disclosure threshold instead of banning related-party transactions outright?
Suggested answers
  1. They are different disclosures with a related purpose: independence disclosure describes directors' ties to management, while related-party disclosure describes a specific transaction and the related person's interest.
  2. The share is 7 / 11 = 63.6%. If the company is not a smaller reporting company, $100,000 is below the $120,000 threshold, so Item 404(a) is not triggered; if it is a smaller reporting company, compare $120,000 with 1% of average total assets for the last two completed fiscal years and use the lower figure. In either case, other rules may still apply.
  3. A blanket ban would block normal business arrangements and add cost; disclosure plus review lets investors assess fairness. A threshold lowers the reporting burden, but similar transactions must be aggregated to prevent splitting to avoid disclosure.
