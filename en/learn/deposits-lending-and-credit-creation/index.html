Deposits, Lending and Credit Creation

When a bank makes a loan it credits an equal deposit to the borrower, so money is created; credit creation is limited by reserves, capital, liquidity and loan demand, and the money multiplier is an upper bound rather than a forecast.

Definition

Credit creation is the process by which a bank making a loan credits an equal deposit to the borrower's account, raising broad money. A bank does not have to wait for an equal deposit before it lends; the loan itself creates a spendable deposit[1].

Intuition

When a bank approves a loan, it does not move cash out of a vault; it writes a deposit into the borrower's account. Once the borrower pays another party, the money circulates, and when the loan is repaid the deposit shrinks and the money disappears. Total deposits across the banking system can therefore be several times the reserves behind them.

Formal expression

Let the reserve ratio be r and new reserves in the banking system be R. The deposit ceiling is D = R ÷ r and the money multiplier is m = 1 ÷ r[1]. The formula assumes that the public holds no extra cash and that banks keep no excess reserves; if either assumption fails, the actual expansion falls short of the ceiling. With r = 10% and R = 100 yuan, the ceiling is 1,000 yuan, round by round as follows:

Conditions and limits

  1. Loan demand and credit standards. The ECB Bank Lending Survey measures both the supply of and the demand for loans to enterprises and households[3]; without creditworthy borrowers, reserves do not turn into credit automatically.
  2. Capital and profitability (the article’s own reasoning). Defaults eroding equity, and a capital-short bank having to shrink assets, follow from the teaching arithmetic in this article and FIN-003 (a loss larger than equity leaves negative net worth); they are not stated by any registered source. Neither OpenStax 27.4 nor the Federal Reserve page retrieved here contains the wording capital adequacy or shrink assets (asserted on the whole-page superset), so this bullet carries no source marker and is not a statistical finding.
  3. Currency drain and excess reserves. When the public holds more cash and banks hold more reserves than required, the actual expansion is smaller.
  4. Reserve ratios are not a fixed gate. The Federal Reserve Board sets reserve ratios on transaction accounts, nonpersonal time deposits and Eurocurrency liabilities; net transaction accounts have been zero since March 26, 2020, while nonpersonal time deposits and Eurocurrency liabilities have been zero since December 27, 1990[2]; creation is still limited by capital, liquidity and demand.
  5. A single bank is constrained (settlement is the article’s own reasoning). After lending, a bank settles with other banks and must borrow or shrink assets if it lacks settlement funds — no sentence says this on either page retrieved here (27.4 scores zero for clearing, settlement funds and must settle; the Federal Reserve page scores zero for settlement funds and must settle, and its only settlement/Clearinghouse hits are payment-service and navigation names), so it is the article's own inference. The textbook does analyse one bank on its own, verbatim "Money Creation by a Single Bank Start with a hypothetical bank called Singleton Bank."[1], while the multiplier logic is written for the system: verbatim "If all banks loan out their excess reserves, the money supply will expand. In a multi-bank system, institutions determine the amount of money that the system can create by using the money multiplier."[1]. So "loans create deposits" describes the system, not any single bank acting alone.

Example and calculation

With R = 100 yuan and r = 10%, the ceiling is 1,000 yuan. If creditworthy loan demand totals only 600 yuan, actual new deposits are 600 yuan, required reserves are 600 × 10% = 60 yuan, so 40 of the 100 yuan of reserves stay as excess reserves and the system comes 400 yuan short of the 1,000 yuan ceiling in Table 1. The multiplier is a ceiling, not a forecast.

Common mistakes

  • Banks lend out depositors' money. The loan and the deposit appear together and disappear together on repayment; a bank is not simply passing deposits through.
  • The multiplier predicts the money supply precisely. It is an upper bound shaped by demand, drain, capital and regulation, and actual outcomes are usually lower.
  • Credit creation is printing money. The new deposit is a bank liability that must be serviced with interest and repaid in principal.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. A bank approves a 50,000 yuan loan and credits the borrower's account. How do the bank's assets and liabilities change?
  2. Calculation. The reserve ratio is 20% and the banking system gains 80 yuan of new reserves. What are the money multiplier and the deposit ceiling?
  3. Conditions. A country has a zero reserve ratio. Can its banks create deposits without limit? Explain.
Suggested answers
  1. Loans rise by 50,000 yuan (assets) and deposits rise by 50,000 yuan (liabilities); equity is unchanged and the money stock rises at the same time.
  2. The multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5; the ceiling is 80 × 5 = 400 yuan, consistent with 80 ÷ 0.20 = 400.
  3. No. A zero reserve ratio relaxes only one constraint; the limits this article lists — capital, liquidity, settlement funds, creditworthy loan demand and credit standards — still constrain expansion, and loan defaults consume equity (those statements are the article’s own reasoning, see conditions 2 and 5).
