Stablecoin Reserves, Pegs and Runs: Is It a Bank?

A stablecoin promises one coin for one dollar and holds reserves to defend the peg. It compresses deposit-reserve-redemption into an on-chain liability but lacks deposit insurance and a lender of last resort; reserve quality, maturity mismatch and redemption speed decide whether it runs.

Definition and intuition

A stablecoin is a crypto token that tries to peg to a fiat currency, usually the dollar. It promises one token for one dollar and holds reserves to defend the peg. Reserves can be bank deposits, short Treasuries, commercial paper, repos or crypto assets.

Its balance sheet resembles a bank: liabilities are redeemable tokens, assets are reserves. But it usually lacks deposit insurance, a discount window and a lender of last resort; capital and liquidity requirements are also weaker.

The peg relies on three designs: full reserves (1:1 assets), over-collateralisation (assets exceed liabilities), and algorithmic adjustment (protocol and token incentives). Full reserves are the most stable; algorithmic stablecoins de-peg most easily under stress[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose a stablecoin issues 10 billion dollars, backed by 6 billion of short Treasuries, 3 billion of bank deposits and 1 billion of commercial paper. If confidence breaks and 3 billion is redeemed in one day, the issuer must convert assets quickly.

Treasuries can settle T+1 but may face a 1% haircut; bank deposits can be withdrawn immediately; commercial paper may be unsellable or discounted 5% under stress. If the reserves realise only 2.97 billion, coverage falls from 100% to 99.7% and the peg starts to slip.

If 6 billion is redeemed, the issuer must sell longer assets, discounts widen and a self-reinforcing run may start. The '1:1' promise holds only in normal markets; under stress, reserve liquidity and maturity set the discount. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Bank analogy

Like a bank: short liabilities, long assets, redeemable, confidence-driven; asset discounts transmit runs.

Unlike a bank: no deposit insurance, no central-bank lender of last resort, inconsistent reserve disclosure and audits, and fragmented cross-border regulation.

Like a money-market fund: a 1-dollar peg, short assets and redemption-driven liquidity risk; but money funds face SEC rules while stablecoin regulation is still forming.

Stablecoin risk is not only 'code bugs'; reserve quality, maturity mismatch, redemption rights and regulatory coverage matter equally[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: a peg is not risk-free. The 1:1 promise depends on reserve authenticity and liquidity; audits, disclosure and custody determine credibility.

Boundary 2: algorithmic stablecoins are not deposits. They rely on arbitrage and incentives, not full reserves; stress can trigger a death spiral.

Boundary 3: regulation differs widely. Reserve, redemption and licensing rules vary by jurisdiction; cross-border use creates arbitrage.

Mistake 1: a stablecoin is a digital dollar. It is private liability, not a Fed liability; credit and liquidity risk remain.

Mistake 2: enough reserves prevent runs. Runs depend on redemption speed and asset liquidity; ample but slow reserves can still de-peg.

Deeper note: reserve structure, runs and the quasi-bank boundary

A stablecoin issuer's balance sheet resembles a narrow bank: liabilities are tokens in circulation, assets are reserves (cash, short Treasuries, repo, bank deposits). Whether the peg holds depends on whether reserve value, liquidity and maturity match redemption speed. If reserves include long bonds or low-rated commercial paper, rate rises cut market value and redemption pressure forces sales at a discount.

Run triggers are not only a price de-peg. When users doubt reserves, they redeem at once; if the issuer sets thresholds, delays or limits, doubt becomes self-fulfilling. Algorithmic stablecoins have no hard reserves and rely on arbitrage and confidence; once confidence breaks, the death spiral is faster than a bank run.

Key differences from bank deposits: stablecoins usually have no deposit insurance and no lender of last resort; issuers are not subject to bank-style capital and liquidity rules, and reserve disclosure and audit scope are limited. It is not a deposit, yet performs deposit-like liquidity functions - the quasi-bank risk.

Reserve quality has four dimensions: the share of cash and overnight assets, Treasury duration, repo counterparties, and concentration of bank deposits. Short Treasuries are stable in value but still subject to haircuts; repo may not roll under stress; bank deposits can fail with the bank. 'Diversified' reserves are not automatically 'safe'.

Redemption mechanics are the core stress test. T+0, no threshold and no fee sounds user-friendly but leaves the issuer no buffer; T+1, limits or fees slow runs but weaken peg credibility. Design trades off stability against redeemability.

Regulatory trends require reserve assets, disclosure, capital buffers and redemption rules; regulation cannot eliminate runs, only reduce probability. Issuers also need liquidity backstops, stress tests and recovery plans.

Common misuses: equating 'pegged to the dollar' with 'equal to the dollar', ignoring issuer and reserve risk; equating '100% reserved' with 'absolutely safe', ignoring duration and liquidity; treating algorithmic arbitrage as 'risk-free', ignoring tail risk.

Finally, stablecoin stability is the joint result of promise, reserves and redemption mechanics, not code or slogans. Reserve composition, duration, redemption terms and governance matter more than the daily peg price.

Stress scenarios: three stages of a de-peg

Stage one, discount appears: the market sees long reserve duration or a fall in some asset value and tokens trade at a 1-2% discount. Arbitrageurs should buy the discount and redeem, but if redemption has thresholds or delays the chain breaks and the discount widens.

Stage two, redemption queue: users redeem en masse and the issuer sells reserves. Short Treasuries suffer limited haircuts; long bonds or commercial paper realise losses and weaken reserves further. Redemption limits or fees may then amplify panic.

Stage three, confidence collapse: the peg breaks 5%, payment venues refuse the token and a death spiral begins. Without deposit insurance, a lender of last resort or outside capital, the issuer restructures or fails. Algorithmic stablecoins usually move faster because arbitrage depends on collateral value.

Buffer design: short reserves, overcollateralisation, liquidity backstops, transparent disclosure and tiered redemption. Buffers reduce probability, not eliminate runs; the key is market confidence that reserves cover stress redemptions.

Regulatory stress tests should assume rates up 200 basis points, 20% of tokens redeemed in one day and a 10% haircut on one reserve asset. If the issuer cannot answer whether the peg survives all three, it should not call itself stable.

Key takeaways

  • A stablecoin issuer is a narrow bank: tokens are liabilities, reserves are assets, and the peg depends on reserve value, liquidity and maturity matching.
  • Runs do not start only at a price de-peg; redemption thresholds, delays and limits make doubt self-fulfilling.
  • The key differences from bank deposits are no deposit insurance, no lender of last resort and weaker capital and liquidity rules.
  • Reserve quality depends on cash share, Treasury duration, repo counterparties and bank-deposit concentration; '100% reserved' is not absolute safety.
  • Redemption mechanics trade stability against redeemability; T+0 with no threshold amplifies runs.
  • Regulation reduces probability, not eliminates runs; issuers still need liquidity backstops and recovery plans.

What would change the conclusion

The stablecoin-as-bank analogy depends on reserves and redemption rules:

  1. Reserves are short-term Treasuries. Credit risk is low, but discounts and liquidity can still gap in stress.
  2. Redemption gates. If limits or delays are imposed, runs slow but confidence discounts rise.
  3. Bank-like regulation. If stablecoins face bank capital and liquidity rules, disintermediation and run transmission change.
  4. Payments use expands. If stablecoins become daily payment tools, deposit substitution and money velocity affect bank funding.
  5. Algorithmic designs. If reserves are insufficient and rely on algorithms, death-spiral risk in stress is far higher than full reserve.

Practical implication: stablecoin stability depends on reserve composition, redemption rules and the regulator; full reserve lowers credit risk but does not remove discounts, runs or settlement breaks. Treating it as a bank deposit misses the absence of a lender of last resort and deposit insurance.

Data note: check reserve maturity, custodian and valuation frequency; short Treasuries are not instant cash, and discounts and settlement delays count.

Scope note: disclose reserve composition, maturity and custodian daily; otherwise holders cannot verify the peg and discounts widen in an information vacuum. Distinguish redemption suspension from insolvency, which allocate losses differently.

Final note: a peg is a promise about reserves, not the price itself; disclosure frequency and reserve quality decide whether the promise can be verified.

Check: verify reserve composition and redemption terms every day; the peg is a promise, not a price.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Reserves and redemption" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. the institutional boundary is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet.
