Introduction to Asset Pricing Models

Asset pricing models map systematic risk to a required return, which is then used to discount cash flows; CAPM is the simplest one-factor benchmark.

Definition

An asset pricing model maps an asset’s systematic risk to an expected return, which is then used to discount future cash flows — this article’s own summary. The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) prices systematic risk only; the textbook reads verbatim "The CAPM focuses on systematic risk, rather than a stock’s individual risk, because firm-specific risk can be eliminated through diversification."[3], so this article treats it as the benchmark built on a single risk factor and measures an asset’s sensitivity to the market with beta, defined on the same page verbatim as "The systematic risk of a particular stock is measured by how much the stock moves with the market. The measure of how much a stock moves with the market is known as its beta."[3]. The risk of an individual asset is, verbatim, "This uncertainty about what the return will be is referred to as risk.", and its most common measure is the standard deviation of returns — "The most commonly used measure of volatility of returns in finance is the standard deviation of the returns."[1].

Intuition

Two projects each expect to pay 108 yuan (CNY) one year from now, but one has beta 0.8 and the other 1.2. With a 4.0% risk-free rate and a 5.0% market risk premium, the first requires a return of 8.0% and the second 10.0%. Their values today are 100.00 yuan and 98.18 yuan. For the same future cash flow, higher systematic risk means a lower price today.

Formal expression

CAPM:

E[R_i] = R_f + β_i × (E[R_m] - R_f)

Beta:

β_i = Cov(R_i, R_m) / Var(R_m)

The display above is the CAPM equation the textbook gives; its text layer reads "Re=Rf + Beta × (Rm - Rf)" (the math markup repeats the same formula twice and this article quotes one occurrence)[3], with the variables defined verbatim "where Re is the expected return of the asset, Rf is the risk-free rate of return, and Rm is the expected return of the market."[3] — the page sets the subscripts as Re, Rf and Rm, while this article uses E[R_i], R_f and E[R_m] for the same quantities. Here E[R_i] is the asset's expected return, R_f the risk-free rate, E[R_m] the expected return of the market portfolio and E[R_m] - R_f the market risk premium (MRP). Writing beta as Cov over Var is a definition this article supplies: that section defines beta only as how much a stock moves with the market[3] and never uses covariance or variance. Returns and rates are percentages over the horizon used here (one year), beta is a ratio and so carries no unit, and cash flows and prices are in yuan. One-period pricing:

P_0 = E[CF_1] / (1 + E[R_i])

Multi-period valuation discounts each expected cash flow at its own required return; when the horizon or the risk changes, do not apply one discount rate to all of them. In equilibrium, expected and required returns coincide.

Conditions and limits

The next three bullets are assumptions this article lists, not the wording of section 15.3, which never uses assumption, homogeneous, frictionless or mean-variance (asserted against the whole page). Cite a verbatim source for an assumptions list in a valuation report or paper:

  • Investors are risk averse, care only about mean and variance, and decide over one period.
  • Investors share the same expectations for expected returns, variances and covariances.
  • Unlimited risk-free borrowing and lending, frictionless markets, and a market portfolio containing all tradable assets.
  • CAPM prices only systematic risk [3]; diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk, and the systematic kind stays, verbatim "This systematic risk cannot be eliminated through diversification."[2].
  • Beta and expected returns are not directly observable; estimates from historical data carry sampling error and instability.
  • CAPM is a benchmark, not a precise forecast; multifactor models add other risk factors.

Worked example

All numbers are hypothetical teaching assumptions; amounts are in yuan (CNY) and returns are percentages over one year. Let R_f = 4.0%, the market risk premium MRP = 5.0%, and the expected cash flow one year out E[CF_1] = 108 yuan.

  • β = 0.8: E[R] = 4.0% + 0.8 × 5.0% = 8.0%; P_0 = 108 yuan / 1.08 = 100.00 yuan.
  • β = 1.2: E[R] = 4.0% + 1.2 × 5.0% = 10.0%; P_0 = 108 yuan / 1.10 = 98.18 yuan.

If both trade at 100.00 yuan, the beta-1.2 asset offers an expected return of 8.0% (108 yuan / 100.00 yuan - 1 = 8.0%), below its 10.0% required return, so the model values it at 98.18 yuan; under the model's assumptions it is relatively overvalued. This conclusion depends on the beta, MRP and cash-flow estimates, is not a certain fact, and is not investment advice.

Common misconceptions

  • High beta always delivers high returns. No. It raises the required return, and the realized return can still fall short — verbatim "However, no investment is a sure thing. The return may not be what the investor was expecting."[1].
  • CAPM gives precise prices. No. It relies on unobservable expectations and the market portfolio, and input errors pass into prices.
  • Diversification eliminates all risk. No. It reduces idiosyncratic risk; systematic risk remains [2].
  • Beta is a permanent constant. No. It varies with sample period, market proxy and leverage.

Boundary case: beta and the market risk premium move together

The article's baseline is R_f = 4.0%, MRP = 5.0%, E[CF_1] = 108. At beta 1.2 the required return is 10.0% and the price is 108 / 1.10 = 98.18; at beta 0.8 the required return is 8.0% and the price is 100.00. With the same cash flow, the higher-beta asset is cheaper.

Change MRP from 5.0% to 3.0%. At beta 1.2 the required return becomes 4.0% + 1.2 × 3.0% = 7.6%, and the price 108 / 1.076 = 100.37; at beta 0.8 the required return is 6.4% and the price 101.50. Both get more expensive, but the high-beta asset gains more. If MRP rises to 7.0%, beta 1.2 requires 12.4% and the price is 96.09; beta 0.8 requires 9.6% and the price is 98.54. High beta is more sensitive to MRP.

MRP Beta 0.8 required Beta 0.8 price Beta 1.2 required Beta 1.2 price
3.0% 6.4% 101.50 7.6% 100.37
5.0% 8.0% 100.00 10.0% 98.18
7.0% 9.6% 98.54 12.4% 96.09

The boundaries: beta is not a permanent constant and moves with the sample window, market proxy, and leverage; MRP is an expectation, not an observation. If beta is estimated at 0.9 instead of 1.2, required returns and prices reorder; if beta is estimated over 60 months while leverage changes, financial risk enters the estimate. CAPM gives a conditional ranking, not an exact price. If prices barely move when inputs change, check whether beta and MRP were treated as constants before calling the model accurate.

Reading and review

An asset-pricing model is not a formula that computes future returns; it decomposes return into compensated risk. CAPM measures sensitivity to the market portfolio with beta, and the security market line writes expected return as the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium. It needs strong assumptions: an observable market portfolio, no frictions and priceable risk. In practice beta changes with the estimation window and the market portfolio is hard to observe fully. Report beta's interval, the source of the premium and the unexplained residual instead of treating a line as truth. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
