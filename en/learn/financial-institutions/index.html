Financial Institutions

Financial institutions differ not by sign but by balance sheet, maturity transformation, revenue source and regulatory perimeter.

ModuleFIN-M06 · 金融机构
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Financial institutions are organizations that take deposits, make loans, manage assets, underwrite risk, operate payment and settlement systems or provide other financial services. They fall into depository and nondepository groups and differ not by name but by balance sheet, maturity transformation, revenue source and regulatory perimeter[2][3]. Understanding an institution starts with what it owns, whom it owes and what it charges for.

Intuition

A bank funds loans with deposits and earns a spread; an asset manager runs money for clients and earns a management fee; an insurer collects premiums, pays claims later and holds reserves; a clearing house holds no customer deposits but bears counterparty risk through margin and default funds. Their revenue and risk structures are entirely different.

Formal expression

Banks

Banks fund themselves with deposits, wholesale funding and capital, and hold loans, securities and reserves. Lending expands the bank balance sheet and creates deposit money, which disappears as the loan is repaid[1]. Their core function is maturity transformation and credit creation; their core risks are runs, credit losses and liquidity mismatch.

Securities firms

Securities firms underwrite, broker, trade for their own account and make markets. Revenue comes from commissions, underwriting fees, spreads and investment gains; risks from inventory, leverage, client default and market swings.

Asset management

Asset management runs client portfolios under fiduciary duty, earning management and performance fees. Client assets usually sit off the manager's balance sheet, but duties of loyalty, care, suitability and conflict-of-interest management remain[4].

Insurance and pensions

Insurance manages risk through underwriting, reserves and reinsurance; pensions accumulate long-term liabilities through contributions and investment[3]. Both rely on long-horizon liabilities and actuarial assumptions; they differ in risk pools and benefit promises.

Trading and clearing

Trading and clearing institutions operate exchanges, central counterparties (CCPs) and settlement systems. A CCP manages counterparty risk through multilateral netting, margin and default waterfalls; margin should cover potential exposure over the close-out interval and be stress-tested regularly[5].

Shadow banking

Shadow banking refers to institutions and activities that perform credit intermediation, maturity transformation or leverage outside traditional bank supervision[6]. It is not a synonym for illegal; the risks come from opacity, run vulnerability and regulatory arbitrage, not the label.

Conditions and limits

  1. Classification must follow legal entities and functions, not licence names; one group may run several businesses.
  2. Balance-sheet analysis must separate on- from off-balance-sheet and client from own assets; mixing them misstates risk.
  3. Clearing risk management depends on margin models, collateral liquidity and default funds; changing stress assumptions changes conclusions.
  4. The shadow-banking boundary moves with regulation; calling every non-bank activity shadow banking loses precision.

Worked example

Suppose bank A and asset manager B have the following simplified structures; the numbers are teaching assumptions.

Item Bank A Asset manager B
Own assets Loans 90, reserves 10 Own cash 5
Client assets None 200 under management
Main liabilities Deposits 100 No client deposits; fiduciary duty
Main revenue Net interest margin Management fee
Main risk Runs, credit loss Fiduciary duty, conflicts

Bank A funds 90 of loans with 100 of deposits, so risk sits on its balance sheet; asset manager B runs 200 of client assets, so risk sits more in fiduciary and governance duties. Both are financial institutions, but applying the same capital-adequacy tool to both would mismatch risk and instrument.

Common mistakes

All financial institutions are banks. Banks, brokers, asset managers, insurers, pensions and clearing houses have different balance sheets and risk structures, and different regulatory tools.

Shadow banking means illegal finance. It describes credit-intermediation functions outside the supervisory perimeter; legality depends on the activity and jurisdiction.

Knowledge-point review

banks

Definition: Banks take deposits, make loans, provide payments and perform maturity transformation as regulated financial intermediaries.

Mechanism: They earn spreads, fees and capital-market income; capital, liquidity and deposit insurance constrain risk.

Example: For example, a commercial bank gathers household deposits, writes mortgages and lends working capital to firms.

Boundary: Banking is not one business; wholesale, retail and investment banking carry different risks, and shadow and digital banks shift boundaries.

securities firms

Definition: Securities firms handle brokerage, underwriting, proprietary trading, research and asset management, linking issuers and investors.

Mechanism: Brokerage earns commissions, underwriting earns spreads and proprietary desks trade capital; net capital, client-asset segregation and suitability rules constrain conduct.

Example: For example, an investment bank underwrites an equity issue and runs the roadshow, while its brokerage arm executes client trades.

Boundary: Securities firms are not pure pipelines; proprietary trading, market making and asset management create conflicts, and leverage and liquidity mismatch amplify risk.

asset management

Definition: Asset management invests client money under a mandate, charging management and performance fees.

Mechanism: It pursues returns through portfolios, research and trading; fiduciary duty, valuation, disclosure and liquidity matching protect investors.

Example: For example, a mutual fund pools retail money in stocks and bonds, issues redeemable units at net asset value and discloses daily.

Boundary: Asset management is not a bank deposit; NAV swings, redemption pressure and strategy capacity can lose money, and manager incentives may mismatch client horizons.

insurance

Definition: Insurance pools premiums to pay specified claims, converting uncertain individual losses into predictable costs.

Mechanism: Insurers underwrite, price, invest and reserve; the law of large numbers, adverse selection, moral hazard and capital constrain sustainability.

Example: For example, health insurance pools many premiums to pay for the few with major illness.

Boundary: Insurance is not a deposit or guaranteed return; exclusions, waiting periods, deductibles and solvency affect claims, and the underwriting cycle can produce losses.

pensions

Definition: Pensions provide retirement income through pay-as-you-go, funded or hybrid arrangements.

Mechanism: Contributions, investment returns, longevity and demographics determine sustainability; DB promises benefits while DC shifts investment risk to individuals.

Example: For example, an employer DC plan contributes a share of wages to funds, and the account balance determines retirement income.

Boundary: Pensions are not simple saving; eligibility, portability, fees, longevity risk and intergenerational fairness matter, and fund failure hits members.

trading and clearing infrastructure

Definition: Trading and clearing infrastructure provides matching, registry, clearing and settlement services as market utilities.

Mechanism: Exchanges, central counterparties and depositories manage risk through rules, margin and default funds; access and governance shape competition.

Example: For example, a stock exchange matches orders and a central counterparty becomes counterparty to every trade, cutting bilateral credit risk.

Boundary: Infrastructure is not neutral; monopoly, outages, governance conflicts and interconnectedness create single points of failure, and cross-border coordination is incomplete.

shadow banking

Definition: Shadow banking covers lightly regulated credit intermediation and maturity transformation, such as money funds, trust loans, securitisation and repos.

Mechanism: It fills bank credit gaps and adds leverage and mismatch but lacks deposit insurance and routine central-bank support.

Example: For example, a money fund invests in short-term paper and offers deposit-like liquidity, though its NAV can fluctuate.

Boundary: Shadow banking is not necessarily illegal; it results from regulatory boundaries, and runs, rollover risk and implicit guarantees create systemic risk that is hard to monitor.
