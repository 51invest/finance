Regulation, Law and Ethics

Financial regulation protects enforceable rights and market integrity; ethics addresses judgment where the rulebook runs out.

ModuleFIN-M18 · 监管法律与伦理
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

Financial regulation sets rules, disclosure, capital, conduct and resolution requirements for institutions and markets. Financial law and ethics asks whose rights those rules protect and how practitioners should judge when the rulebook runs out. Bank prudential regulation centres on capital and stable funding[1]; securities regulation aims at investor protection, fair, efficient and transparent markets, and reduced systemic risk[3]; custody of client assets has separate rules[2]; professional ethics requires putting client interests above personal interests[4]. Regulation is the floor; ethics is the judgment above the floor.

Intuition

A broker recommends a product and earns a sales commission while the client believes the recommendation is based on suitability. A conflict exists. Disclosure can reduce misunderstanding, but if the commission still rewards high-fee products, disclosure alone may not change behavior.

Formal expression

Financial rights

This article lists financial rights as ownership of client money, redemption, information, fair treatment and compensation. Rules must map to concrete rights: who holds them, how to exercise them and how to remedy breach; this is the article's analytical framework.

Disclosure

Disclosure requires issuers, intermediaries or product providers to publish key information and reduce asymmetry. Its effectiveness depends on comprehensibility, comparability, timeliness and investors' capacity to use it.

Market integrity

Market integrity prohibits fraud, manipulation, insider trading and misrepresentation[3]. It protects the credibility of markets as pricing and allocation mechanisms, not merely individual losses.

Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest arises when multiple interests may lead a party to harm one of them. Tools include disclosure, information barriers, prohibition, compensation reform and independent review; regulatory principles require conflicts and misaligned incentives to be avoided, eliminated, disclosed or otherwise managed, and disclosure alone cannot remove structural conflicts[3].

Client assets

The goal of client-asset protection is to keep client assets separately managed from the firm's own assets and to identify and return them as far as possible in insolvency. Client-asset protection usually includes a qualified custodian, segregation, reconciliation and audit; the SEC custody rule requires advisers to place client assets with a qualified custodian and provide account statements[2]. Full return also depends on legal ownership, custody arrangements and insolvency proceedings.

Regulatory duties

Regulators handle entry, prudential supervision, conduct, market oversight and resolution. Responsibilities should be clear, the regulator independent and accountable with adequate powers and resources[3]. Regulation can also fail through capture, information gaps, coordination problems and lag; accountability and independent evaluation are part of regulatory governance.

Conditions and limits

  1. Rule analysis must state the subject, trigger, disclosure requirement and consequence; restating principles does not reveal effects.
  2. Disclosure depends on investor understanding and comparison cost; complex products need more than disclosure to protect vulnerable clients.
  3. Conflict remedies must target structure, not only individual morality; compensation and business models are often the root.
  4. Client-asset protection depends on legal ownership, custody and insolvency; cross-border cases need recognition in each jurisdiction.

Worked example

Suppose a client buys a 10,000 yuan product; option A charges a 2% sales commission, option B a 0.5% management fee. These are teaching assumptions.

Option Charge First-year cost Potential conflict
A 2% commission 200 yuan Incentive to recommend high-commission products
B 0.5% management fee 50 yuan Lower cost, but possible asset-gathering motive

The commission option pays the broker 150 yuan more in year one; if the product is not more suitable, the conflict is real. Remedies include disclosing the commission, switching to asset-based fees, independent advice or banning certain sales incentives; each has costs and residual conflicts, and disclosure is not automatically enough.

Common mistakes

Compliance is ethics. Compliance is the minimum rule; ethics also asks for right judgment where conduct is legal but harms clients or markets[4].

Disclosure solves conflicts. Disclosure informs but does not change incentives; high commissions can still drive unsuitable recommendations.

Knowledge-point review

financial rights

Definition: Financial rights are investors', depositors', policyholders' and borrowers' rights to information, fair dealing, redemption, complaint and remedy.

Mechanism: They rest on securities, banking, consumer-protection law and contract enforcement, delivered through disclosure, suitability, cooling-off and compensation.

Example: For example, an investor should receive a prospectus and risk warning before a complex product and can complain about mis-selling.

Boundary: Rights are not guaranteed returns; investors still bear market risk, and enforcement depends on information, cost and regulatory capacity.

disclosure

Definition: Disclosure publishes financial, risk, governance and transaction information to reduce information asymmetry.

Mechanism: It runs through prospectuses, periodic reports, material events and advertising rules; timeliness, completeness, comparability and verifiability matter.

Example: For example, a listed firm reports financials and related-party deals so investors can value and vote.

Boundary: More disclosure is not always better; overload, boilerplate, selective release and false statements weaken it, and proprietary information and privacy need balance.

market integrity

Definition: Market integrity means prices and trading are not undermined by fraud, manipulation, insider dealing or false information.

Mechanism: Regulators prohibit market abuse, monitor trading, protect whistleblowers and enforce; market making, short selling and algorithms must operate within rules.

Example: For example, an insider trading on undisclosed information is investigated and penalised to protect other investors.

Boundary: Integrity is not zero risk; enforcement resources, cross-border evidence and technology hide violations, and over-restriction can reduce liquidity.

conflicts of interest

Definition: A conflict of interest arises when an intermediary's or agent's own interest diverges from the client's, potentially distorting advice, pricing or execution.

Mechanism: Conflicts are managed by separation, disclosure, proprietary-trading bans, pay changes and best-execution rules; full elimination is often impossible.

Example: For example, an investment bank underwriting and recommending a stock may let research be influenced by underwriting revenue.

Boundary: Disclosure is not a cure-all; investors may ignore or misunderstand conflicts, pushing responsibility to clients, while culture and incentives are root causes.

client assets and regulatory duties

Definition: Client assets are cash, securities and fund units held or managed for clients; regulatory duties include licensing, supervision, inspection and resolution.

Mechanism: Client assets need segregation, custody, reconciliation and no diversion; regulators use capital, reporting, on-site checks and enforcement.

Example: For example, if a broker fails, client securities should be separated from the firm's own assets and returned.

Boundary: Regulation is not absolute protection; fraud, cross-border custody and complex structures can pierce segregation, and capture, scarce resources and coordination failures weaken duties.
