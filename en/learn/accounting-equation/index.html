The Accounting Equation

Assets equal liabilities plus equity. The identity explains how resources are financed, but it does not mean assets are worth their market value or that a company is safe.

Definition

The accounting equation is the basic identity of the balance sheet:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Assets are resources controlled by the business; liabilities are obligations to outside parties; equity is the residual claim after subtracting liabilities from assets. The SEC beginner guide reads verbatim "A balance sheet provides detailed information about a company's assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity.", and puts the identity as "ASSETS = LIABILITIES + SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY"[1]; OpenStax's accounting glossary writes the accounting equation verbatim as "assets = liabilities + owner’s equity"[3], and the finance volume's chapter 4 glossary as Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity[2].

Expanding equity gives:

Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings

Under a simplified view, retained earnings accumulate revenues minus expenses and dividends; other comprehensive income, accounting-policy changes and similar items require treatment under the applicable standards.

Intuition

Every resource a business uses is financed by someone: either a creditor or an owner. Buying equipment, holding inventory and keeping cash all require a funding source. The equation is not a claim that more assets are always better; it matches the uses of resources with their sources.

Double-entry bookkeeping requires each transaction to affect at least two accounts while keeping both sides equal. Borrowing cash increases cash and liabilities; an owner contribution increases cash and equity; buying equipment with cash changes the composition of assets without changing the total.

Formal expression

A simplified balance sheet (all figures are teaching assumptions):

Item Amount
Cash 50,000
Accounts receivable 30,000
Inventory 40,000
Equipment 80,000
Total assets 200,000
Accounts payable 25,000
Loan payable 75,000
Total liabilities 100,000
Equity 100,000

Assets of 200,000 = liabilities 100,000 + equity 100,000. Equity is the residual: 200,000 - 100,000 = 100,000. It is not a separate cash balance; it is a claim on net assets.

How transactions keep the identity

Starting from the table above, consider these transactions in sequence:

Transaction Assets Liabilities Equity Identity
Opening position 200,000 100,000 100,000 200,000 = 100,000 + 100,000
Borrow 20,000 cash 220,000 120,000 100,000 220,000 = 120,000 + 100,000
Buy equipment for 10,000 cash 220,000 120,000 100,000 220,000 = 120,000 + 100,000
Owner contributes 30,000 cash 250,000 120,000 130,000 250,000 = 120,000 + 130,000
Receive 15,000 cash revenue 265,000 120,000 145,000 265,000 = 120,000 + 145,000
Pay 5,000 cash expense 260,000 120,000 140,000 260,000 = 120,000 + 140,000

In the second transaction, cash falls by 10,000 and equipment rises by 10,000, so total assets do not change. The third transaction raises cash (an asset) and contributed capital (equity) together and never passes through the income statement. The fourth and fifth transactions affect retained earnings through revenue or expenses and therefore change equity; taxes, depreciation and other comprehensive income are omitted for brevity, and journal-level double entries are outside this article.

When it applies

  1. A point-in-time statement. The balance sheet reports balances at a date; the income statement and cash-flow statement report changes over a period.
  2. Carrying value is not market value. Assets are normally recorded at cost — OpenStax states verbatim "The cost principle, also known as the historical cost principle, states that virtually everything the company owns or controls (assets) must be recorded at its value at the date of acquisition."[4] — and the same section notes an alternative practice, "This is called mark-to-market accounting or fair value accounting"[4]. On spreading those costs, the SEC guide reads verbatim "This process of spreading these costs is called depreciation or amortization."[1]. The term "amortized cost" used loosely in this article is this article's own wording: none of the four pages this site could retrieve contains the phrase amortized cost (the three OpenStax pages are asserted byte-by-byte; the SEC page was read through WebFetch because curl returns 403 here). That book equity is not a sale price is this article's judgement.
  3. Estimates and judgment. Receivable impairment, depreciation lives, inventory costing and contingent liabilities involve estimates; different assumptions change the numbers.
  4. Off-balance-sheet items and omissions. Some rights and obligations are disclosed in the notes rather than recognized on the face of the statement, and which ones qualify depends on the accounting standard and year in force; this article makes no claim about a specific standard, so confirm the basis before comparing statements.
  5. Consolidation is a caution this article raises without a source. No standards text on the consolidation basis was retrieved here — the word consolidation does not appear on any of the four verifiable pages — so this bullet gives no citation and makes no claim about specific rules or exemptions. It only reminds readers to check whether a statement is consolidated or parent-only before comparing entities.
  6. Negative equity is not an immediate insolvency finding. It means book liabilities exceed book assets; going-concern analysis still depends on cash flow, debt maturities and restructuring arrangements.

Common mistakes

Equity is cash. No. Equity is the residual claim on assets after liabilities and may be represented by equipment, inventory or receivables.

Borrowing increases income. Borrowing increases assets and liabilities, not revenue; revenue counts toward profit and retained earnings only when it satisfies the rule the glossary states verbatim, "revenue recognition principle stating that a company must recognize revenue in the period in which it is earned"[2].

Assets are shown at market value. Most assets are recorded at cost[4] and that cost is spread over the period of use (SEC verbatim: "This process of spreading these costs is called depreciation or amortization."[1]), so book value can differ greatly from realizable value.

Positive equity means the company is safe. Liquidity, debt maturity, cash flow, guarantees and off-balance-sheet obligations also matter; the equation does not assess solvency.

Revenue equals cash. Under accrual accounting revenue may be recognized before or after cash moves — the glossary defines it verbatim as "accounting system in which revenue is recorded or recognized when earned yet not necessarily received, and in which expenses are recorded when legally incurred and not necessarily when paid"[3] — so profit and cash flow are not the same thing.

Knowledge-point review

accrual accounting

Definition: Accrual accounting recognises revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash is paid or received, matching performance to the period.

Mechanism: Accruals, deferrals, receivables and payables separate cash flow from economic activity, and estimates enter revenue and expense recognition.

Example: For example, a firm delivering goods in December and collecting in January recognises December revenue and a receivable.

Boundary: Accrual accounting is not exact truth; bad debts, returns, warranties and project progress require estimates, and aggressive recognition can pull profit forward.

recognition and measurement

Definition: Recognition decides when an item enters the statements, and measurement decides at what amount, subject to accounting standards, contracts and reliability.

Mechanism: Revenue is recognised by performance progress and transaction price, and assets at cost or fair value; impairment, discounting and variable consideration change carrying amounts.

Example: For example, a software contract delivered in stages recognises revenue by progress rather than all at signing.

Boundary: Recognition and measurement are not free choices; standard differences, judgement and estimation bias hurt comparability, and fair value can be unreliable in illiquid markets.

assets, liabilities and equity

Definition: Assets are controlled resources with future economic benefit, liabilities are obligations to transfer benefits, and equity is the residual after liabilities.

Mechanism: The identity is assets equals liabilities plus equity; transactions affect at least two items, and equity changes through profit, contributions and distributions.

Example: For example, borrowing to buy equipment raises assets and liabilities; repaying with cash lowers both.

Boundary: Classification is not just names; control, obligation and measurability determine recognition, and off-balance-sheet items, leases and derivatives can change true leverage.

accounting estimates

Definition: Accounting estimates are judgements about bad debts, inventory, depreciation, warranties and pensions under uncertainty.

Mechanism: They use history, contracts and models and update with new information; changes affect current profit and future periods.

Example: For example, a firm estimates 2 percent of receivables uncollectible, but customer deterioration may require a higher rate.

Boundary: Estimates are not automatically manipulation; the line between judgement and earnings management is blurry, and changes, restatements and weak disclosure reduce comparability.

audit and financial reporting

Definition: Audit independently checks whether statements fairly reflect the standards, within a reporting system of balance sheet, income statement, cash-flow statement and notes.

Mechanism: Auditors gather evidence, test controls and sample, but materiality, estimates and fraud limit assurance; notes add policies and risks.

Example: For example, auditors confirm bank balances, count inventory and inspect revenue contracts before issuing an opinion.

Boundary: Audit is not absolute assurance; reasonable assurance, management fraud and complex transactions can slip through, so qualified opinions and note quality matter.

Data and institutional check: accounting equation

Before using "accounting equation" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns balance sheet.

Third, the institutional boundary. equity is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: accounting equation

Learning "accounting equation" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "balance sheet": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "equity" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of accounting equation still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind balance sheet reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "accounting equation" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "balance sheet" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Decision table: accrual accounting, recognition and measurement, assets, liabilities and equity

Put the three knowledge points into one decision in the order object, mechanism and boundary: first confirm what is being observed, then whether the causal chain holds, and finally whether the conclusion can be extended. If the definition in step one and the proxy in step two use different definitions, later calculations are precise about the wrong object. If step three omits the boundary, one successful sample is mistaken for a general law.

Exercises

  1. Calculation. A company has assets of 500,000 and liabilities of 320,000. What is equity?
  2. Transaction effect. The company borrows 50,000 cash from a bank. How do assets, liabilities and equity change?
  3. Boundary judgment. A company has positive equity but not enough cash to repay a loan due next week. Can positive equity alone rule out a liquidity problem? Why?
Suggested answers
  1. Equity is 500,000 - 320,000 = 180,000.
  2. Cash and assets rise by 50,000; liabilities rise by 50,000; equity is unchanged. Both sides of the identity increase by the same amount.
  3. No. Equity is a book residual and assets may be illiquid; short-term repayment depends on cash, operating cash flow and the debt maturity schedule. Positive equity does not guarantee liquidity.
  1. Concept check. Explain "accounting equation" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  2. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "accounting equation" to "balance sheet" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the balance-sheet identity using assets, liabilities and equity.
  • State the effect of simple transactions on the three columns — assets, liabilities and equity — and explain why double-entry bookkeeping preserves the identity (account-level journal entries are outside this article).
  • Distinguish carrying value, market value and the residual nature of equity, and identify the limits of the balance sheet.
