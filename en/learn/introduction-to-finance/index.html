Introduction to Finance

Finance is not about money making money; it rearranges savings, risk, maturity and information between surplus and deficit units.

ModuleFIN-M01 · 金融导论
LevelFoundation
Read5 min · 12 sections

Definition

Finance studies how saving becomes investment, how risk and maturity are reallocated, and how surplus and deficit units connect through instruments and intermediaries. The textbook stresses that financial tools help people make consistent, repeatable decisions[1]. Its core is not “money making money” but arranging cash flows and risk across time and agents; financial claims are rights to future cash flows or assets, structured as debt, equity or other forms.

Intuition

A household earns more than it spends this year; a firm needs funds to build a plant but will earn later. The household deposits, the firm borrows, and a bank in between takes deposits, makes loans and bears maturity mismatch. The two could trade directly, but search, due diligence and contracting costs would be higher.

Formal expression

Financial functions

A financial system supplies at least three vital services—payments and settlement, intermediation between savers and investors, and insurance against and dispersion of risk[3]; market trading also generates price signals that help participants value assets, which is price discovery[4]. An arrangement is judged by which cost it lowers and to whom it shifts risk, not by how sophisticated it looks.

Surplus and deficit units

A surplus unit earns more than it spends in the period; a deficit unit spends more than it earns. They can connect through lending, equity, leasing or insurance; the form changes both risk and control allocation.

Intermediation

A financial intermediary transforms maturity, credit and liquidity and processes information, turning dispersed savings into usable funding. Banks take short-term deposits and make long-term loans, bearing run and credit risk; lending expands their balance sheets and creates deposit money, which disappears as the loan is repaid[2]; funds pool money and bear fiduciary duties.

Direct and indirect finance

Direct finance connects savers and users through securities markets; indirect finance has an intermediary raise funds first and then lend or invest[5]. Direct finance relies more on disclosure and market pricing; indirect finance on credit assessment and the intermediary's balance sheet.

Financial claims

Financial claims include deposits, bonds, shares, insurance payouts and derivatives. Analysis must state the obligor, trigger, seniority, maturity and transferability.

Conditions and limits

  1. Functional analysis must state the legal and regulatory setting; the same instrument can carry different risks and rights across jurisdictions.
  2. Intermediation's maturity transformation creates liquidity risk; short-term liabilities funding long-term assets bring run and fire-sale risk.
  3. Direct finance depends on disclosure and investor protection; weak disclosure distorts price discovery.
  4. Seniority and enforceability depend on insolvency, secured-credit and judicial efficiency, not contract text alone.

Worked example

Suppose a bank holds 100 yuan of demand deposits, makes 90 yuan of one-year loans and keeps 10 yuan of reserves. These are teaching assumptions.

Item Amount (yuan) Maturity
Demand deposits 100 Withdrawable on demand
Loans 90 One year
Reserves 10 Immediately available

The bank funds one-year assets with on-demand liabilities: that is maturity transformation. If depositors withdraw more than 10 at once, the bank must sell loans or borrow; if loans are discounted, the bank may become insolvent. The value and the fragility of intermediation come from the same transformation.

Common mistakes

Finance is just speculation. Its core functions are payments, allocation, risk management and price discovery[3][4]; speculation is one motive, not the whole.

Intermediaries merely add a fee. They lower search, due diligence, contracting and maturity-mismatch costs; without them, those costs fall on the parties.

Knowledge-point review

functions of finance

Definition: The financial system mobilises saving into investment, provides payments, manages risk, discovers prices and allocates maturity and liquidity.

Mechanism: Functions are delivered through intermediaries, markets and contracts; different structures can perform similar functions with different efficiency, stability and distribution.

Example: For example, banks gather household deposits, lend to firms and provide payment and risk-management services.

Boundary: Finance is not only fundraising; over-financialisation, maturity mismatch and weak regulation can turn functions into speculation and risk accumulation.

surplus and deficit units

Definition: Surplus units earn more than they spend and hold net financial assets; deficit units spend more than they earn and need external finance.

Mechanism: Surplus flows to deficit units through deposits, bonds, funds and insurance; maturity, risk and return preferences shape matching.

Example: For example, a household saves part of monthly income in a bank, which lends to a firm expanding capacity.

Boundary: Surplus need not fund productive investment; hoarding, foreign assets and speculation can idle funds, and deficits may finance consumption rather than investment.

intermediation

Definition: Financial intermediation links savers and borrowers, reducing information asymmetry and transaction costs while transforming maturities.

Mechanism: Intermediaries pool small deposits into large long-term finance through diversification, credit assessment, collateral and reputation, but create agency and systemic links.

Example: For example, a bank assesses borrowers, pools deposits and makes mortgages, so savers need not screen each borrower.

Boundary: Intermediation is not a free pipe; spreads, fees and implicit guarantees are borne by users, intermediary failure can spread, and disintermediation does not remove risk.

direct and indirect finance

Definition: Direct finance has savers and borrowers transacting through securities, shares or bonds; indirect finance runs through bank-like intermediaries' balance sheets.

Mechanism: Direct finance spreads risk and prices transparently but needs disclosure and market depth; indirect finance allows relationship lending but concentrates risk in banks.

Example: For example, a firm issuing bonds to a fund is direct finance, while borrowing from a bank is indirect.

Boundary: The two are not substitutes; law, tax, information and investor structure shape the mix, and direct finance also creates fund and custody chains.

financial claims

Definition: Financial claims are rights to future cash flows, assets or services, embodied in deposits, bonds, shares, insurance and derivatives.

Mechanism: Claims are layered by seniority, maturity, control and payment conditions; contracts and law define who gets how much under what conditions.

Example: For example, depositors hold a claim to principal and interest, while shareholders hold residual earnings and votes with different seniority and risk.

Boundary: Claims are not the underlying real asset; counterparty default, inflation, dilution and contract interpretation change value, and legal enforcement shapes strength.
