Bank Balance Sheets

A bank balance sheet lists reserves, loans and securities as assets and deposits and borrowings as liabilities, with equity as the difference; it shows that banks operate mainly with other people's money, and it explains why deposits are not the bank's own funds.

LevelFoundation

By the end of this article, you can read a bank balance sheet to see where the money comes from, where it goes, and which loss would hit equity first.

Definition

A bank balance sheet records a bank's assets, liabilities and owners' equity at a point in time and follows assets = liabilities + equity. Loans, securities and reserves are assets; deposits and borrowings are liabilities; the difference is equity, or net worth[1].

Intuition

When a depositor hands money to a bank, the bank owes an amount that can be withdrawn at any time, so deposits sit on the liability side. A loan is a claim the bank will collect in the future, so it sits on the asset side. The bank earns the spread between asset returns and funding costs, while its own equity is only a small share of total assets.

Formal expression

Table 1: Simplified balance sheet (amounts in CNY, teaching assumptions)

Item Amount
Cash and reserves 15
Loans 760
Securities 180
Other assets 45
Total assets 1,000
Deposits 850
Interbank borrowing and other liabilities 90
Total liabilities 940
Equity 60

Assets 1,000 = liabilities 940 + equity 60. Two common ratios: the liabilities-to-assets ratio is 940 ÷ 1,000 = 94%, and the equity multiplier is 1,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 16.7 times. One more scope note, because it is easy to conflate: cash and reserves of 15 are 15 ÷ 850 = 1.76% of deposits (about 1.8%) and 15 ÷ 1,000 = 1.5% of assets — that is an accounting outcome. A required reserve is a legislated share of deposits, which the textbook states verbatim: "The Federal Reserve requires that banks keep a certain percentage of depositors’ money on “reserve,” which means either in their vaults or at the Federal Reserve Bank."[1]; the 10% used in FIN-004 is that share turned into a teaching parameter. The two quantities are not comparable, and neither describes a real bank.

Conditions and limits

  1. Point in time versus period. A balance sheet is a balance at one date; an income statement covers a period, and the two should not be mixed.
  2. Book value is not market value. Assets are recorded at cost in principle — OpenStax's accounting text reads verbatim "The cost principle, also known as the historical cost principle, states that virtually everything the company owns or controls (assets) must be recorded at its value at the date of acquisition."[4] — and the exception the same section names reads verbatim "The primary exceptions to this historical cost treatment, at this time, are financial instruments, such as stocks and bonds, which might be recorded at their fair market value."[4], which is why securities can carry market values. How loss estimates are handled follows from conservatism, verbatim "Conservatism states that if there is uncertainty in a potential financial estimate, a company should err on the side of caution and report the most conservative amount. This would mean that any uncertain or estimated expenses/losses should be recorded, but uncertain or estimated revenues/gains should not."[4], so "impairment allowances rely on estimates" is that principle applied to loans rather than this article's guess. The phrase "amortized cost" in the earlier version of this bullet is this article's own wording: none of the four pages retrieved here contains amortized cost (asserted page by page), so the specific line-item name belongs to no registered source. That a fall in market prices need not appear at once is this article's inference from the cost principle.
  3. Maturity transformation. Demand deposits can be withdrawn at any time, while loans are repaid at maturity; banks rely on liquid assets and stable funding to bridge the gap[1].
  4. Regulatory capital. The Basel III framework of the BIS sets minimum capital standards and requires a stable funding profile covering on- and off-balance sheet activities[3]. Capital is a residual claim, not vault cash.
  5. Measurement conventions. The Federal Reserve's H.8 release separates bank credit, securities, loans and leases, cash assets, deposits and borrowings[2]; consolidated and parent-only statements also differ, so check the basis before comparing.

Example and calculation

Using Table 1 (teaching assumptions): assets 1,000 yuan, liabilities 940 yuan, equity 60 yuan. If a 100 yuan loan recovers only 30 yuan, assets fall by 70 yuan to 930 yuan and equity falls by 70 yuan to -10 yuan. A loss of just 7% of assets wipes out all equity, leaving the bank insolvent.

Common mistakes

  • Deposits are the bank's own money. Deposits are liabilities owed to depositors and can be withdrawn.
  • Banks lend out every deposit. Banks keep reserves and liquid assets and must also meet capital and stable-funding requirements[3].
  • Bigger assets mean safer banks. Equity is only 6% of assets here, so a 7% asset loss would erase all of it.

Knowledge-point review

bank balance sheet

Definition: A bank balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and capital: loans and securities are assets, deposits and borrowings are liabilities, and capital is the net buffer.

Mechanism: Banks fund long assets with short liabilities to earn a spread; asset quality, liquidity and capital adequacy determine solvency.

Example: For example, a bank takes 100 yuan of demand deposits, keeps 10 as reserves and lends 90, expanding both sides.

Boundary: The sheet is not static; rates, defaults, valuation and off-balance-sheet business change risk, and maturity mismatch can make book capital understate stress losses.

deposits and loans

Definition: Deposits and loans are a bank's core business: deposits fund the bank, while loans create interest-earning assets and credit risk.

Mechanism: Banks earn spreads, fees and cross-selling; approval, collateral, maturity and pricing determine risk and return.

Example: For example, mortgage rates exceed deposit rates, and the spread covers funding, credit losses and operating costs.

Boundary: Deposits and loans are not simple money-moving; lending creates deposits that support further lending, while capital and liquidity rules cap scale and bad loans erode capital.

credit creation

Definition: Credit creation occurs when a bank lends and simultaneously credits the borrower's deposit, expanding broad money.

Mechanism: It is constrained by reserves, capital, cash leakage, credit demand and regulation; repayment or securitisation shrinks or shifts money.

Example: For example, a 1 million yuan loan raises the borrower's deposit, and recipients' redeposits can support further lending.

Boundary: Banks cannot create credit without limit; weak capital, liquidity stress and defaults constrain it, and central banks control base money rather than each loan.

bank capital

Definition: Bank capital is shareholders' equity and eligible capital instruments that absorb losses and keep the bank solvent under stress.

Mechanism: Capital adequacy compares capital with risk-weighted assets; retained earnings, issuance, subordinated debt and provisions shape it.

Example: For example, a 50 yuan loan loss wipes out a bank with only 40 yuan of capital, while a buffer absorbs it.

Boundary: More capital is not always better; excessive capital can constrain lending, risk weights and off-balance-sheet items distort ratios, and accounting and regulatory capital differ.

maturity and liquidity transformation

Definition: Maturity transformation funds long assets with short liabilities; liquidity transformation turns liquid liabilities into less liquid assets.

Mechanism: Transformation creates credit and yield but also run and interest-rate risk; deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort and liquidity rules mitigate it.

Example: For example, demand deposits can be withdrawn anytime yet fund 30-year mortgages; a mass withdrawal forces asset sales at discounts.

Boundary: Transformation is not risk-free arbitrage; larger mismatches increase stress fragility, and money funds or shadow banks may transform similarly with weaker backstops.

Exercises

  1. Concept check. A bank takes a 200 yuan deposit and at the same time makes a 200 yuan loan. On which side of the balance sheet does each item appear?
  2. Calculation. A bank has assets of 500 yuan and liabilities of 470 yuan. What is equity, and what is the equity multiplier? If assets are written down by 40 yuan, what does equity become?
  3. Conditions. A bank has positive book equity, but depositors withdraw large amounts at the same time. Can the balance sheet alone show that the bank is safe?
Suggested answers
  1. The deposit is a liability (the bank owes the depositor); the loan is an asset (the borrower owes the bank).
  2. Equity is 500 - 470 = 30 yuan; the equity multiplier is 500 ÷ 30 ≈ 16.7; after the write-down equity is 30 - 40 = -10 yuan.
  3. No. Demand deposits can be withdrawn at once while loans cannot be sold quickly, so positive book equity does not rule out a liquidity crisis; liquid assets, the deposit base and funding sources also matter.
