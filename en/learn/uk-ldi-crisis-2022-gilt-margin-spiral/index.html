The 2022 UK LDI Crisis: Margin Spirals and Pensions

After the September 2022 mini-budget, 30-year gilt yields rose more than 270 basis points in weeks, forcing leveraged LDI funds to sell gilts for collateral. Put yields, collateral and pension liabilities on one timeline to see how non-bank leverage became a systemic risk.

Background and timeline

On 23 September 2022, the UK government announced a package of tax cuts and spending measures, and the market questioned fiscal sustainability. Long-dated gilt yields rose sharply over the following days; by 14 October, the 30-year yield briefly exceeded 5%, more than 270 basis points above 1 August, a much larger move than US Treasuries or German bunds over the same period[5]. Higher yields raise the discount rate on pension liabilities and lower their present value, which looks helpful; but for leveraged LDI funds, higher yields mean collateral values fall and margin requirements rise.

Funds must quickly raise cash or sell assets; when many funds sell gilts at once, yields rise further, creating a “margin spiral”[5]. On 28 September, the Bank of England announced temporary, targeted purchases of long-dated gilts, with an initial cap of £65 billion. It actually bought £19.3 billion between 28 September and 14 October (£12.1 billion conventional and £7.2 billion index-linked).

Market functioning improved; the Bank sold the holdings in an orderly way between 29 November and 12 January 2023, making about £3.8 billion of profit[5].

Information available and actors

The main actors were the UK Treasury, pension regulators, defined-benefit pension schemes, LDI managers, banks financing the leverage, and the Bank of England. Pension schemes aim to match long-term liabilities; LDI uses derivatives and repo to replicate long-duration exposure with less capital. The LDI decision is not “buy gilts or not” but “how much leverage to replicate duration”.

More leverage lowers capital use but also lowers tolerance to rising yields. Funds typically hold gilts or gilt derivatives as collateral; when collateral value falls, financiers demand more margin, and the fund must sell assets or ask the pension scheme for cash. The Bank of England faced a dilemma: without intervention, forced selling could become a systemic financial crisis; with intervention, future leverage might be encouraged.

It chose temporary, targeted purchases at penalty prices with a clear time limit, trying to stabilise the market without a permanent subsidy.

Outcome and the number chain

Key numbers: 30-year gilt yields rose more than 270 basis points from 1 August to 14 October, briefly above 5%; the Bank announced a £65 billion cap and bought £19.3 billion; £12.1 billion conventional and £7.2 billion index-linked; about £3.8 billion profit on the unwind; regulators asked LDI buffers to withstand 300–400 basis points of yield change[5]. Chain them: fiscal announcement → long yields rise → collateral value falls → margin calls → funds sell gilts → yields rise again. A 270 basis-point rise on a 3× levered, 20-year duration portfolio can wipe out several times the fund’s capital; not selling means finding margin, selling means pushing prices lower.

The £65 billion was a cap, £19.3 billion was actual use; the gap shows the Bank’s backstop was priced above the market and only the most pressed institutions used it. That reduced moral hazard but also meant the intervention was not an unlimited guarantee.

Mechanism

The first layer is leverage. LDI uses repo or derivatives to replicate long duration with little capital, making it sensitive to collateral prices. The second is collateral.

Falling gilt prices lower collateral value, and financiers demand more margin or a larger haircut. The third is synchronisation. Many funds hold similar portfolios; selling at once amplifies the price fall and triggers more margin[5].

The three layers form a “collateral–margin–selling” loop. Higher yields lower the present value of pension liabilities, but for leveraged funds the liquidity shock arrives first; with enough cash or buffer, a fund could wait for the liability revaluation and would not be forced to sell. The crisis was not “pensions are insolvent” but “funds lack cash”.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, with lower LDI leverage and larger buffers able to absorb more than 300–400 basis points, funds would not have had to sell in the same week and the spiral would not start. Second, if the fiscal announcement had included a credible medium-term fiscal constraint, the long-gilt risk premium would have risen less and the yield shock would be milder. Third, if the Bank of England had offered liquidity support on 26 September, funds would not have sold at the low, but moral hazard would be higher and future leverage might rise.

Fourth, if pension regulation required funds to hold more standalone liquidity buffers instead of relying on scheme cash injections, forced-selling synchronisation would fall.

Limits to generalization

The case can show that leveraged non-bank institutions, when collateral prices fall fast, can amplify a shock into the core government bond market through margin and forced selling; a temporary, targeted, penalty-priced central-bank intervention can break the spiral; pension liabilities and fund liquidity are two separate books. It cannot show that all LDI funds were insolvent; that central-bank intervention always works; that higher yields hurt pensions only; or that the UK institutional arrangement applies directly elsewhere. The key is leverage, collateral and synchronisation, not “pensions are always safe” or “regulators always failed”.

Decision review

A pension trustee’s decision is not “hedge rates or not” but “how much leverage to use for the hedge”. With no hedge, falling yields raise the present value of liabilities and widen the funding gap; holding long gilts in cash uses too much capital. LDI was meant to replicate long duration with less capital; the problem was the balance between leverage and liquidity buffer.

An LDI manager’s decision is collateral-constrained. When yields rise, the value of gilts or derivative collateral falls and the financier demands more margin; the fund can ask the pension scheme for cash or sell gilts. If the scheme lacks cash, selling becomes the only option; when many funds sell at once, prices fall and margin rises again.

The regulator faces “moral hazard versus systemic risk”. Without action, forced selling could hit the core gilt market; with action, future LDI might lever up again. The Bank of England chose temporary, targeted purchases at penalty prices with a clear deadline, trying to separate “saving the market” from “saving the institution”.

The design is not perfect, but it is closer to incentive compatibility than an unconditional backstop.

Review record

Fact layer: checked yield change, peak, purchase size and window against the Bank of England report. Number layer: separated the £65bn announced cap from the £19.3bn actually bought, never treating the cap aspurchases. Mechanism layer: confirmed leverage, collateral and synchronised selling.

Counterfactual layer: changed leverage, fiscal announcement and central-bank timing one at a time. Boundary layer: made clear that lower pension liability value does not mean adequate fund liquidity.
